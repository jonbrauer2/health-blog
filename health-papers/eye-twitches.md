Most eyelid twitches are the harmless, temporary kind called eyelid myokymia. They usually fade within days to weeks once you get more rest, cut back on caffeine, and give your eyes a break. A smaller group of twitches that affect both eyes, force the eyelid shut, or spread across one side of the face are separate conditions, and those deserve a clinician's look.

**TL;DR**

- The everyday flutter in one eyelid (myokymia) is common and benign, and it usually settles on its own. Sleep, stress relief, less caffeine, screen breaks, and care for dry or irritated eyes are the most sensible first steps.
- Popular remedies sit on a spectrum. Rest, warm compresses, preservative-free lubricating drops, and mineral-rich foods are reasonable and low-risk. Supplements like magnesium have limited or indirect evidence. Tonic water (which contains quinine) and potassium pills call for caution. A lit cigarette in the ear, ear candling, and household oils in the eye are not recommended.
- See a clinician if the twitch:
  - lasts more than a few weeks
  - closes the eye fully
  - spreads to the cheek or mouth
  - involves both eyes with growing squeezing
  - comes with drooping, weakness, redness, discharge, vision changes, or other nerve symptoms

## A Tiny Flutter With a Big Personality

You're halfway through a meeting, or finally settling into a good book, when your lower eyelid starts to jump. It feels like a small moth is trapped under your skin. You glance at the person across the table, sure they can see it. They almost certainly can't. These twitches are usually far more obvious to the person having them than to anyone watching.[^2]

If this sounds familiar, you're in very good company. The American Academy of Ophthalmology describes the common eyelid twitch as a brief, minor annoyance for most people. It can come on suddenly and last for a minute, a few hours, or occasionally days.[^1] Mayo Clinic notes that this most common type, myokymia, "happens to most people at some point."[^5]

In many cultures around the world, a twitching eye is read as a sign: good news on the way, a visitor coming, money arriving, or a reason to be careful. Sometimes the meaning depends on which eye twitches. These traditions are part of the rich way people have always made sense of their bodies. This handout tells the other side of the story: what's happening in the body, why twitches show up, and what helps them settle down.

> **Key Fact:** Most eyelid twitches are benign and resolve without treatment. Clinical reviews describe ordinary eyelid myokymia as self-limited. It usually resolves within days or weeks with rest and fewer triggers.[^2]

## What's Happening Under the Skin

Wrapped around each eye is a thin, circular muscle called the **orbicularis oculi**. It's the muscle that closes your eyelids, whether you're blinking, squinting at the sun, or squeezing your eyes shut during a scary movie. The **facial nerve** (the seventh cranial nerve) runs from the brainstem out across the face, and its branches tell this muscle when to contract.[^2]

Muscles are organized into small working teams called motor units. Each unit is one nerve fiber plus the muscle fibers it controls. Normally, a motor unit fires only when your brain sends a signal. In eyelid myokymia, a few of these units start firing on their own in small, repeating bursts. Recordings of the muscle show a single motor unit firing in rhythmic spurts, often about 3 to 8 times per second. That's what creates the rippling feeling in the lid.[^2]

Here's the honest part: nobody fully knows why these units start firing on their own. Medical reviews describe the cause of eyelid myokymia as "not entirely understood" and its underlying mechanism as "incompletely understood."[^2] What clinicians do know is that it tends to show up in healthy people. It's linked with fatigue, stress, anxiety, exercise, and caffeine.[^2][^4] So when you read confident explanations online about exactly what causes twitches, it's fair to take them with a grain of salt.

## Three Different Conditions That Get Called "Eye Twitch"

This is the most important section of this handout. People use the phrase "eye twitch" for several different conditions, and they are not the same. Knowing the difference helps you relax about the common kind and notice when something else might be going on.

| | **Eyelid myokymia** | **Benign essential blepharospasm** | **Hemifacial spasm** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What it feels like** | Fine, fluttering ripple in the lid | More blinking, then forceful squeezing shut | Twitching that starts near the eye and can pull the cheek or mouth |
| **Where** | Usually one eyelid, most often the lower lid | Both eyes | One side of the face |
| **Who** | Very common; often healthy young adults | Less common; usually middle age or older, more often women | Less common; usually adults |
| **What's behind it** | Not fully understood; linked to fatigue, stress, caffeine | A brain-based movement disorder (focal dystonia) | Often a blood vessel pressing on the facial nerve |
| **Typical course** | Fades in days to weeks | Long-lasting; needs ongoing care | Long-lasting; needs medical care |

**Eyelid myokymia** is the everyday twitch. It usually affects one eyelid, more often the lower one, and comes and goes for seconds to hours at a time.[^2] Sometimes it sticks around for several days or a few weeks before fading, and long-lasting cases of several months do happen.[^2][^4] Even then, it's usually harmless. One study followed 15 people whose myokymia stayed in the eyelid for a long time. None turned out to have an underlying neurological disease. One later developed hemifacial spasm on the same side, and the twitching faded on its own in four of them.[^3] That's a small study, but it's reassuring.

**Benign essential blepharospasm** is a very different condition. It's a form of focal dystonia, which means the brain's control of certain muscles doesn't work properly. It affects both eyes. It starts with more blinking and can progress to strong, involuntary closing of the eyelids.[^6] It can make reading, driving, and getting around difficult. Researchers estimate it affects roughly 16 to 133 people per million. It's more common in women and usually starts between about age 40 and 70.[^6] The word "benign" means it isn't life-threatening, not that it's minor.

**Hemifacial spasm** involves one side of the face. It often starts near the eye and spreads over time to the cheek and mouth, and the spasms can continue even during sleep. Most cases come from a blood vessel pressing against the facial nerve where it leaves the brainstem.[^12] If a twitch closes the eye completely each time, that's a clue that something other than ordinary myokymia may be happening.[^2]

A few other situations are worth knowing about:

- **Tics.** Children sometimes develop quick, repeated movements like blinking or squinting that come and go.
- **After Bell's palsy.** People who have had Bell's palsy (a temporary weakness of the facial nerve) sometimes notice linked movements later, like the eye closing when they smile. This happens because the nerve heals in a slightly rewired way.
- **Rare nerve conditions.** In rare cases, lasting twitching that spreads beyond the eyelid has been the first sign of a condition such as multiple sclerosis or a problem in the brainstem.[^2][^4]

That last point isn't meant to worry you. It's the reason this handout includes a "when to see a clinician" list later on. The uncommon cases get checked, and the common ones get reassurance.

> **Key Fact:** Myokymia and blepharospasm are different conditions. Myokymia is a passing flutter in one lid. Blepharospasm is a long-lasting neurological movement disorder that affects both eyes and can force them closed.[^2][^6]

## What Tends to Set Twitches Off

Most of what we know about myokymia triggers comes from doctors noticing patterns across many patients, not from controlled trials. That doesn't make the patterns wrong. It means they've been well observed but not precisely measured.

**Sleep loss and fatigue.** Being overtired is one of the most commonly named triggers.[^1][^2] One review mentions that myokymia is sometimes nicknamed the "medical students' disease" because it seems to show up in stressed, sleep-deprived students.[^2]

**Stress.** Clinical reviews consistently link stress and anxiety with twitching.[^2][^4] Stress also tends to come with poor sleep and more coffee, so these triggers often arrive together.

**Caffeine.** Caffeine blocks adenosine, a brain chemical that normally calms nerve activity. In a controlled lab study, caffeine increased how fully people could activate their muscles, a sign that the nervous system becomes more excitable.[^16] That study didn't look at eyelids, so it doesn't prove caffeine causes eye twitches. It does help explain why cutting back on caffeine is standard advice.[^2]

**Alcohol and nicotine.** Clinical reviews list alcohol and smoking among the things worth cutting back on when myokymia shows up.[^2] There's little direct research on eyelids, so think of these as sensible, low-risk changes rather than proven causes.

**Screens and digital eye strain.** Long screen sessions make us blink less. In one study of computer users, people blinked about 8 to 9 times a minute during computer work, compared with about 16 to 22 times when looking straight ahead normally.[^15] Blinking less can dry out the surface of the eye. A link between screens and eyelid twitching makes sense, but it's still being studied.

**Dry eye disease.** Dry eye is a common condition in which the tear film (the thin layer of tears that coats the eye) loses its normal balance, causing discomfort and irritation.[^14] The American Academy of Ophthalmology suggests seeing an ophthalmologist if you have ongoing twitching along with gritty, uncomfortable eyes.[^1]

**Blepharitis and eyelid irritation.** Blepharitis is inflammation along the edges of the eyelids, often with crusting at the lashes. It often goes along with meibomian gland dysfunction, where the tiny oil glands in the lids don't release oil well.[^17] Anything that keeps the eye surface irritated could plausibly add to twitching.

**Allergies, contact lenses, and new eye cosmetics.** Itchy allergy eyes, lenses worn too long, and new mascara or eyeliner can all irritate the eye. The link to twitching comes mostly from what doctors observe. Still, if a twitch started around the time you switched makeup or changed your lens routine, that's a useful clue.

**Certain medicines.** Some medicines have been reported to trigger eyelid myokymia. These include topiramate (used to prevent migraines and seizures), clozapine, flunarizine, and gold salts.[^2] A Spanish study described eyelid myokymia in people with migraine who were taking topiramate.[^13] Other kinds of drugs, such as stimulants, antihistamines, and some psychiatric medicines, come up in discussions about twitching. But there's little published evidence for these, and twitching caused by medicine is uncommon overall.[^2] If a twitch started soon after a new medicine, **talk to your prescriber or pharmacist**. Please don't stop a prescribed medicine on your own.

## What Conventional Care Offers

### For ordinary myokymia: reassurance and fewer triggers

For the common twitch, clinical reviews recommend gentle care: rest, reassurance, and cutting back on caffeine, alcohol, and smoking.[^2] This isn't a brush-off. The twitch usually fades on its own, and stronger treatments carry more risk than the twitch itself. If the twitch comes with dry or irritated eyes, it makes sense to treat those problems with lubricating drops, warm compresses, or an eye exam.[^1][^14]

In rare cases, myokymia lasts more than about three months and really disrupts life. Then botulinum toxin injections can be used.[^2] The same review points out that it's hard to tell whether improvement after an injection comes from the treatment or from the twitch's natural tendency to fade.[^2] That kind of honesty is a good model for thinking about any remedy.

### For blepharospasm and hemifacial spasm: botulinum toxin

Botulinum toxin (often known by brand names such as Botox) temporarily blocks the chemical signal between nerve and muscle, so the overactive muscles relax. It became a standard treatment for blepharospasm in 1989, the same year the FDA approved it for blepharospasm and hemifacial spasm.[^7]

In 2016, the American Academy of Neurology updated its guideline on botulinum toxin. It concluded that two versions (onabotulinumtoxinA and incobotulinumtoxinA) are "probably effective and should be considered" for blepharospasm. It called a third (abobotulinumtoxinA) "possibly effective and may be considered."[^9] The careful wording reflects a gap: the treatment is widely used and trusted, but there are few high-quality trials comparing it with a placebo (a dummy treatment). A 2020 Cochrane review found that the treatment likely raises the risk of vision complaints and eyelid drooping. It also found that trials haven't yet shown the best dose or the best time between injections.[^10]

**Benefits:** For many people with blepharospasm, injections bring real relief. The effect usually lasts about three months, and then injections are repeated.[^8] For hemifacial spasm, a 2024 review of 35 studies found that injections worked in about 88% of cases.[^11]

**Limitations and side effects:** The most common side effects are a drooping eyelid (ptosis) and trouble fully closing the eye. Dry eye and double vision are less common.[^7] Injections aren't a cure. They need to be repeated for as long as the condition lasts, and some people don't respond well.[^8]

**Financial interests:** The authors of the 2016 guideline published their financial disclosures. These included consulting fees, speaking fees, or research support from companies that make botulinum toxin products.[^9] Having these ties doesn't mean the conclusions are wrong. It's the same kind of openness worth asking for from supplement makers too.

### Pills for blepharospasm

Several kinds of pills have been tried for blepharospasm. They include anticholinergics (such as trihexyphenidyl), benzodiazepines (such as clonazepam), and baclofen. A 2020 review summed them up this way: "Medication efficacy is at best modest and does not show the same level of efficacy when compared to BoNT" (botulinum toxin). Side effects throughout the body also limit their use, and benzodiazepines carry a risk of tolerance and dependence.[^7] Patient resources describe results as "usually moderate or unsatisfactory and often temporary."[^8] Pills may still help some people, but it's best to keep expectations modest.

### Surgery for hemifacial spasm

Hemifacial spasm is usually caused by a blood vessel pressing on the facial nerve. Surgeons can perform **microvascular decompression**, which means gently moving the vessel away from the nerve and placing a cushion between them. A systematic review of 5,685 patients found that symptoms went away completely in 91.1% of patients over about three years of follow-up, and they rarely came back.[^12] It's the only treatment that offers a chance at a lasting fix.

The trade-off is that it's brain surgery. Possible complications include hearing loss, facial weakness, and leaks of the fluid around the brain and spinal cord. The review found these were uncommon and usually temporary. Even so, the decision is a personal one to make with a specialist.[^12]

## Lifestyle Steps That Help Calm the Lid

The steps in this section are good for your whole body. Some have direct support for eye comfort. Others are general wellness steps that haven't been tested on eyelids. Each one is labeled below.

**Keep a steady sleep rhythm.** Going to bed and waking up at about the same times, and keeping bright screens out of the hour before sleep, helps you rest better overall. Fatigue is one of the most commonly named twitch triggers,[^1][^2] though no trial has tested sleep changes on eyelid twitching directly.

**Make room for calm.** Stress is closely linked with twitching.[^2] Slow breathing (for example, in for a count of four and out for a count of six), a daily walk outside, and regular pauses in your day can ease the body's stress response. These general wellness habits haven't been tested for eyelid twitching, but they're low-risk and helpful in many other ways.

**Give your eyes screen breaks.** The **20-20-20 guideline** says that every 20 minutes, you look at something at least 20 feet away for at least 20 seconds. In a 2023 study, 29 regular computer users got software reminders to follow this guideline for two weeks. Their eye strain and dry eye symptoms improved. But the benefit faded a week after they stopped, and their blink rate and the measurable signs on the eye surface didn't change.[^15] So the guideline has modest, early support for eye comfort, and none yet for twitching. Pair it with **blink awareness**: every so often, do a few slow, complete blinks.

**Cut back on caffeine gradually.** If you drink a lot of coffee, tea, energy drinks, or cola, cutting down is standard advice for twitches.[^2] Go slowly, though. Stopping all at once can bring withdrawal headaches, tiredness, and grumpiness for a few days, because your brain has gotten used to caffeine. Replacing one cup at a time over a week or two is kinder to your body.

**Drink enough water.** Staying hydrated helps you feel comfortable overall. There's no direct research on water and eye twitches, so this is a general wellness step.

**Care for your eyelids.** If your lids are crusty or red along the edges, or your eyes feel gritty, try warm compresses. Soak a clean washcloth in warm (not hot) water and hold it over your closed eyes for several minutes. The warmth softens the oils in clogged eyelid glands. Gently cleaning along the lash line with a product made for eyelids can also clear away debris. Both are standard care for blepharitis and meibomian gland dysfunction.[^17]

**Eat a plant-rich, whole-food diet.** Magnesium is found in leafy greens, beans and lentils, nuts, seeds, and whole grains. Refining grains removes much of it.[^20] Here are some examples:[^20]

- 1 ounce of roasted pumpkin seeds: about 156 mg of magnesium
- Half a cup of boiled spinach: about 78 mg
- Half a cup of cooked black beans: about 60 mg

Bananas, potatoes, beans, and dried fruits like figs are good sources of potassium. Leafy greens, fortified plant milks, and tofu made with calcium add calcium.[^21][^22] No trial shows that eating this way stops eyelid twitching, but it's one of the safest and most nourishing things you can do.

> **Key Fact:** Plant foods are rich sources of magnesium. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements lists green leafy vegetables, beans and lentils, nuts, seeds, and whole grains as good sources, and says foods with fiber generally provide magnesium.[^20]

## Popular Home and Natural Remedies: What the Evidence Shows

Search "how to stop eye twitching" and you'll find dozens of remedies. They come from folk-remedy websites full of reader stories, wellness blogs, and family advice. Many people report real relief, and those experiences matter. When someone says, "I drank tonic water and my twitch stopped in two days," that's an honest account of what they lived through.

One idea helps make sense of these stories. Ordinary myokymia usually fades on its own within days or weeks. So almost anything a person tries while the twitch is at its worst will seem to work. We can call this the **natural-course effect**. It doesn't mean the person imagined their relief, or that the remedy did nothing. It means that without a comparison group, we can't tell how much of the improvement came from the remedy and how much came from the body settling down on its own. Researchers writing about tonic water noted that improvement "may not be attributable to tonic water alone given the self-limiting nature of the condition."[^4]

Below, common remedies are sorted along a spectrum. We try to be clear about the difference between "not studied yet" and "studied and found not to help," and between "limited evidence" and "no evidence."

### Reasonable and low-risk

**Rest and sleep; stress reduction and meditation; cutting back on caffeine and alcohol.** People commonly say their twitches ease after a good night's sleep, a calmer week, or less coffee. These steps target the triggers clinicians see most often, and they're the core of standard care.[^2] They haven't been tested in trials for eyelid twitching, but the risks are close to zero. *Fair conclusion: a sensible first step for almost everyone.*

**Lubricating eye drops (artificial tears).** Many people find that drops soothe the eye and seem to calm the twitch, especially when the eyes feel dry or gritty. Drops don't act on the nerve or muscle directly, but they can reduce irritation on the surface of the eye.[^14] If you use drops more than a few times a day, choose **preservative-free single-use vials**. The preservatives in multi-use bottles can irritate the eye with frequent use. Always use sterile products made for the eye. *Fair conclusion: reasonable and low-risk, and especially helpful if you have dry eye.*

**Warm compresses.** Warmth over closed eyes feels soothing and helps loosen clogged oils in the eyelid glands. This is standard care for blepharitis and meibomian gland dysfunction.[^17] *Fair conclusion: reasonable and low-risk, especially for twitches that come with irritated lids.*

**Foods rich in magnesium and potassium; water.** People often say that eating more greens, bananas, or nuts seems to help. There are no trials on eyelid twitching, but getting enough of these nutrients from food supports normal nerve and muscle function. Many Americans also don't get enough magnesium.[^20][^21] *Fair conclusion: reasonable and low-risk, with wide health benefits.*

### Plausible but mostly unstudied (low-risk if gentle)

**Gentle acupressure around the brow.** Some people press lightly on points along the eyebrow or at the temples and feel the twitch ease. It may help by relaxing the area or giving a calming moment. It hasn't been studied for eyelid myokymia. Keep the pressure light, stay on the bony rim around the eye, and never press on the eyeball itself. *Fair conclusion: plausible, unstudied, and fine to try gently.*

**Eye movement exercises and quick, deliberate blinking.** Some people say that slow eye rolls, quick blinking for a few seconds, or squeezing the eyes shut and then relaxing them "resets" the twitch. One clinical review notes that gently pulling on the lid often makes the twitch feel better for a moment.[^2] But no trials have tested these techniques for myokymia. *Fair conclusion: plausible and unstudied. Harmless if done gently, though relief may be brief.*

**Coconut water.** Coconut water contains potassium and some magnesium, and some people drink it to replace minerals. There's no research on coconut water and eyelid twitching. It does contain natural sugars. People with kidney disease or who take medicines that raise potassium should check with a clinician first.[^21] *Fair conclusion: plausible, unstudied, and low-risk for most people.*

### Limited or mixed evidence

**Magnesium supplements.** Magnesium is one of the most commonly suggested supplements for twitching, because it plays a role in how nerves and muscles work.[^20] But there are **no clinical trials of magnesium for eyelid twitching**.[^4] The closest research is on muscle cramps:

- A 2020 Cochrane review of 11 trials found, with moderate certainty, that magnesium is unlikely to reduce how often or how badly older adults get muscle cramps.[^25]
- For leg cramps during pregnancy, the trials disagreed and had quality problems, so the question is still open.[^25]
- Two of the trials in that review were funded by a company that makes magnesium tablets.[^25]

Cramps aren't the same as eyelid twitches, so this evidence is indirect.

Minor side effects, mostly diarrhea and nausea, were common in the trials.[^25] People with kidney disease can build up magnesium to unsafe levels. Magnesium can also get in the way of the body absorbing certain antibiotics (such as tetracyclines and quinolones) and bone-strengthening drugs called bisphosphonates.[^20] *Fair conclusion: limited and indirect evidence. If you're thinking about a supplement, go over it with a pharmacist or clinician first, especially if you have kidney problems or take other medicines. Food sources come first.*

**Calcium supplements.** Some remedy sites suggest calcium for twitches, sometimes insisting on a strict "2:1 calcium-to-magnesium ratio." There's no research on calcium supplements for eyelid twitching. We also found no trial evidence supporting a 2:1 ratio for twitching or muscle health. Official intake recommendations set separate amounts for calcium and magnesium rather than a ratio.[^20][^22]

There's also an ongoing scientific debate about calcium supplements and heart health. A 2010 analysis linked calcium supplements to about a 30% higher risk of heart attack.[^27] A 2016 review, though, found that calcium intake within recommended amounts, from food or supplements, didn't clearly raise heart and blood vessel risk.[^26] Because of this uncertainty, many experts prefer getting calcium from food when possible. *Fair conclusion: limited evidence for twitching. Getting calcium from food is the safer path.*

**B vitamins.** Low vitamin B12 can cause real nerve symptoms, including numbness, tingling, balance problems, and memory changes.[^23] That's exactly why it calls for a proper blood test rather than guesswork. Treating yourself can hide a deficiency that needs a clinician's follow-up. There's no direct evidence that B vitamins stop ordinary eyelid twitching. *Fair conclusion: limited evidence for twitching. If you have nerve-type symptoms or eat few animal foods, ask a clinician about testing.*

**Acupuncture.** Acupuncture has a long history in traditional Chinese medicine. Many trials, mostly from China, have tested it for hemifacial spasm and blepharospasm, and some report encouraging results. But a 2022 overview looked at eight systematic reviews of acupuncture for facial spasm, published between 2012 and 2022. It rated the quality of all eight as "critically low."[^32] That means we can't trust their results to show acupuncture's true effect, good or bad. There are no good trials for ordinary myokymia. Acupuncture by a licensed practitioner using sterile needles is generally low-risk, but needling near the eyes takes special skill. *Fair conclusion: limited, low-quality evidence. If you try it for a diagnosed spasm condition, use it alongside medical care, not instead of it.*

### Caution

**Potassium supplements.** Too much potassium in the blood (**hyperkalemia**) can cause dangerous heart rhythm problems. The risk is highest for people with kidney disease and for people taking certain medicines:[^21]

- ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), which are common blood pressure medicines
- potassium-sparing diuretics (water pills)
- other products that contain potassium

There's no research on potassium supplements for eyelid twitching. *Fair conclusion: caution. Get potassium from foods like beans, potatoes, bananas, and leafy greens, and don't take potassium pills unless a clinician recommends them.*

**Tonic water and quinine.** This is probably the most popular folk remedy for twitching, so it deserves a careful look. Tonic water gets its bitter taste from quinine, a drug that was once widely used for leg cramps at night. The idea is that quinine calms the connection between nerve and muscle.[^4]

In the United States, drinks can contain at most 83 parts per million of quinine. That works out to no more than about 83 mg in a full liter of tonic water, far less than the amounts studied as a medicine for cramps.[^4] So it's uncertain whether a glass or two has any real effect on muscles. **No clinical trials have tested tonic water for eyelid twitching.** Researchers doubt any will be done, since the condition is so harmless.[^4]

At medicine-level doses, quinine can be dangerous. In 2010, the FDA warned that the prescription quinine drug Qualaquin "should not be used for night time leg cramps." The reason was serious and life-threatening reactions. These included a severe drop in platelets, the blood cells that help clotting (thrombocytopenia), and a blood and kidney disorder called hemolytic-uremic syndrome/thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura.[^28] FDA reviews described 38 serious cases reported between 2005 and 2008, including two deaths.[^29] Quinine can also affect heart rhythm.[^4]

Even the small amounts in drinks can cause trouble for people who are sensitive to quinine. Doctors have described "cocktail purpura." In these cases, quinine-containing drinks set off an immune reaction that destroyed platelets and caused bruising and bleeding.[^30] One case report described a 64-year-old man whose platelets dropped after he drank tonic water to relieve leg cramps at night.[^31]

The authors of a 2020 article on this exact question concluded that for most people, a short trial of one to two glasses of tonic water a day for a few days is unlikely to cause significant harm. They stressed that a medical history is important to find people at higher risk.[^4] Regular tonic water also contains a fair amount of sugar.

*Fair conclusion: an occasional glass is low-risk for most people, but it isn't a remedy with evidence behind it. Avoid it if you:*

- *have ever reacted to quinine*
- *have a heart rhythm condition*
- *take medicines that affect heart rhythm or blood clotting*
- *are pregnant*

*If you're unsure, check with a pharmacist.*

> **Key Fact:** Tonic water contains only a small fraction of a medical dose of quinine, at most about 83 mg per liter in the U.S. Even so, the FDA has warned that prescription quinine should not be used for leg cramps because of serious blood-related reactions.[^4][^28]

**Castor oil for the eyes.** Castor oil shows up often in natural remedy circles. There's some interesting research on it, but the details matter a great deal.

*Sterile eye products made with castor oil* are very different from the bottle in your kitchen cabinet. Castor oil is used in some lubricating eye drops, where it helps strengthen the oily layer of the tear film. Here's what the research shows:

- In a small trial of 20 people with meibomian gland dysfunction, specially prepared, low-strength castor oil eye drops were found to be effective and safe compared with a placebo.[^19]
- A 2021 review concluded that castor oil shows promise for blepharitis, meibomian gland dysfunction, and dry eye.[^17]
- In a four-week trial, 26 people with blepharitis applied castor oil to the skin of the eyelids (not in the eye) twice a day with a rollerball applicator. The treated eyes showed improvements in eyelid signs and symptoms, and no side effects were reported.[^18]

These studies are small and short, and they're about eyelid and tear film health, not twitching.

*Household castor oil put directly in the eye* is a different story. It isn't sterile, so it can carry bacteria or other contaminants into the eye. Its thickness can also blur vision and irritate the eye. *Fair conclusion: sterile eye products, and applying castor oil to the eyelid skin with an eye doctor's guidance, have limited but promising evidence for lid and tear problems. Putting household castor oil directly into the eye is not recommended.*

**Lysine.** Some remedy lists include lysine, an amino acid (a building block of protein). We found no research on lysine for eyelid twitching, and no clear reason it would affect the nerves or muscles involved. *Fair conclusion: no known reason or evidence for twitching.*

### Not recommended

Some remedies found online carry a real risk of injury. We'll be kind but direct about these.

**A lit cigarette held near the ear.** Some folk sources describe holding a lit cigarette at the ear to "pop an air bubble" that supposedly causes the twitch. There's no such air bubble connected to the eyelid, and the ear isn't connected to the eyelid muscles in any way that would allow this. The practice carries a clear risk of burns to the ear, face, and hair, and of starting a fire. *This is not recommended.*

**Ear candling.** Ear candling means placing a hollow, wax-coated cone in the ear and lighting the other end. The FDA has warned people not to use ear candles because of the risk of burns, punctured eardrums, and blockage of the ear canal. It adds that these injuries can happen even when the candles are used as directed.[^33] Testing has shown that ear candles don't create the suction they're claimed to produce. A survey of 122 ear, nose, and throat doctors found 21 ear injuries from candle use.[^34] The FDA also points to a quieter risk: putting off care for problems that need real treatment.[^33] *This is not recommended.*

> **Key Fact:** A remedy should never risk more harm than the problem it's meant to fix. The FDA has warned that ear candles can cause burns, punctured eardrums, and ear canal blockage, even when used as directed.[^33]

## Different Perspectives on Some Contested Questions

Health is a field where thoughtful people reach different conclusions. This section explores a few areas where views differ, without trying to declare a winner.

### Is hidden magnesium deficiency behind twitching?

Many practitioners of integrative and functional medicine see muscle twitching as a possible sign of low magnesium, even when a standard blood test comes back normal. Parts of this view have real science behind them:

- Most of the body's magnesium is stored in bones and inside cells, and less than 1% is in the blood.[^20]
- The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements notes that blood magnesium levels "have little correlation with total body magnesium levels." It also says no single test for magnesium status is considered satisfactory.[^20]
- Low intake is common. National survey data from 2013–2016 found that 48% of Americans get less magnesium from food and drinks than the average person needs.[^20]
- Muscle cramps and spasms are recognized symptoms of true magnesium deficiency.[^20]

On the other hand, magnesium deficiency that causes symptoms is uncommon in otherwise healthy people. That's because the kidneys hold on to magnesium when intake is low.[^20] And the closest trial evidence, from muscle cramps, shows little benefit from supplements in older adults.[^25]

So how convincing is the magnesium theory for eyelid twitching? It's plausible, and eating more magnesium-rich foods makes sense for many people. But it isn't proven. The evidence doesn't support assuming you're deficient or reaching for high-dose supplements without guidance.

### Caffeine: avoid it or not?

Many wellness traditions encourage avoiding caffeine altogether, and many people who've done so say they feel calmer and sleep better. Others enjoy their morning cup with no trouble at all. Research shows caffeine makes the nervous system more excitable,[^16] and clinicians routinely suggest cutting back when twitches appear.[^2] What we don't know is how much caffeine matters for any one person's eyelid. A respectful middle path is a personal experiment. Cut back on caffeine gradually for a week or two, notice what happens to your twitch, sleep, and energy, and decide what works for you.

### B12 and plant-based eating

Plant-based diets have many well-documented health benefits. At the same time, vitamin B12 is found naturally almost only in animal foods. So vegetarians, and especially vegans, need a reliable source such as fortified foods or a supplement.[^23] A review of studies using sensitive tests found that low B12 was common among vegetarians, and even more common among vegans.[^24] This isn't a criticism of plant-based eating. It's a common problem that's easy to prevent. If you eat mostly plant foods and notice numbness, tingling, balance changes, unusual tiredness, or memory trouble, ask a clinician about B12 testing.[^23] A twitching eyelid on its own isn't a typical sign of low B12.

> **Key Fact:** B12 matters for people who eat plant-based diets. Vitamin B12 comes almost entirely from animal foods and fortified products. Vegetarians and vegans benefit from a reliable source, and from a clinician's check when symptoms suggest a problem.[^23][^24]

### Screens and modern life

Screen use clearly makes us blink less and is linked to eye strain and dry eye symptoms. But research connecting screen time to eyelid twitching is still thin, and studies of screen-break strategies have been small and short.[^15] Screen breaks are still low-cost comfort measures worth trying.

### Nutrient-focused care versus reassurance-first care

Functional medicine often starts by looking for nutrient patterns and root causes. Conventional care for myokymia usually starts with reassurance and cutting back on triggers, saving tests for warning signs or twitches that don't go away.[^2] The first approach can prompt helpful changes in diet and catch real problems like low B12. The second avoids unnecessary tests and costs, and it's good at spotting the rare cases that need a neurologist. Many people are best served by a bit of both.

### Money on both sides

Supplements are a big business, and some supplement studies are paid for by the companies that sell them. That was true of two of the magnesium cramp trials in the Cochrane review.[^25] On the conventional side, botulinum toxin injections and specialized procedures are also big businesses. Guideline authors often report ties to the companies that make those products.[^9] Neither fact makes a treatment good or bad.

## Safety First: When to See a Clinician

Most twitches need nothing more than time and some gentle self-care. But based on guidance from eye care and clinical sources,[^1][^2][^5] please see a clinician if:

- The twitching **lasts more than a few weeks** or keeps coming back
- Your **eyelid closes completely** with each twitch, or you have **trouble opening the eye**
- The twitching **spreads** to your cheek, mouth, or other parts of your face or body
- **Both eyes** are involved, with more and more blinking or squeezing
- You notice **eyelid drooping**, facial weakness, or a face that looks newly uneven
- The eye is **red, swollen, or painful**, or has **discharge**
- You have **vision changes** or are newly sensitive to light
- You have **other nerve symptoms**, such as numbness, weakness, or trouble speaking (get urgent care if these come on suddenly)
- The twitching **began after starting a new medicine** (talk to your prescriber, and don't stop the medicine on your own)
- A **child** has twitching or tics that don't go away

> **Key Fact:** Some signs mean it's time to see a clinician. Mayo Clinic advises making an appointment if twitching doesn't go away within a few weeks, if the eyelid closes completely with each twitch, or if twitching happens in other parts of the face.[^5]

### Supplement safety

"Natural" doesn't always mean safe for everyone. Before starting magnesium, potassium, calcium, quinine-containing products, or high-dose vitamins, go over your plan with a pharmacist or clinician if you:

- Have **kidney disease**, which raises the risk of a dangerous buildup of magnesium or potassium[^20][^21]
- Have a **heart rhythm condition**[^4][^21]
- Are **pregnant or breastfeeding**[^4]
- Take **prescription medicines**, including blood pressure drugs (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing water pills), certain antibiotics, bone-strengthening drugs, or blood thinners[^20][^21]

One free, easy step: bring all your supplement bottles to your next pharmacy or clinic visit and ask, "Is this safe with everything else I take?"

## How to Talk About Eye Twitching With Someone You Care About

If a friend, family member, or someone you support mentions a twitchy eyelid, you can be a calming presence without playing doctor. A few helpful things to say:

- "Most twitches like that are harmless and fade on their own."
- "Have you been short on sleep lately? That seems to set them off for a lot of people."
- "It might be worth trying less coffee and some screen breaks for a week or two."
- "Since it's been going on a while, this is worth having checked."

You can also try small habit changes together, like an evening walk or a caffeine-free afternoon drink. Invite, don't pressure.

It's just as important to know where helpful support ends. Please don't try to figure out whether someone has blepharospasm or hemifacial spasm. Don't interpret their blood tests or suggest supplement doses. If you notice any of the warning signs above, the kindest thing you can do is gently encourage a visit: "I'd feel better if you had someone look at that. Want help finding an appointment?"

## Your Calm-the-Eyelid Plan

If your eyelid is jumping right now, here's a gentle plan for the next one to two weeks:

1. **Protect your sleep.** Aim for a steady bedtime and wake-up time, and dim your screens in the last hour before bed.
2. **Cut back on caffeine slowly.** Each day, swap one caffeinated drink for water, herbal tea, or decaf.
3. **Take screen breaks.** Every 20 minutes or so, look at something far away, and add a few slow, complete blinks.
4. **Soothe your eyes.** Use warm compresses, and try preservative-free lubricating drops if your eyes feel dry or gritty.
5. **Put food first.** Fill your plate with leafy greens, beans, nuts, seeds, whole grains, potatoes, and fruit.
6. **Notice patterns.** Keep a short journal. Write down when the twitch shows up, how you slept, how much caffeine you had, how stressed you felt, and how long you were on screens.
7. **Know the warning signs.** Keep the checklist above handy, and contact a clinician if anything on it appears.

If it helps, you might think of a twitching eyelid as your body's gentle nudge toward rest. People from nearly every tradition, faith, and philosophy have recognized the wisdom of regular rhythms: a time to work and a time to stop, a time for noise and a time for stillness. Pausing to breathe, stepping outside, going to bed a little earlier, or noticing what you're grateful for won't just help your eyelid settle. These habits are good for the whole of you. Most of the time, the twitch will quiet down on its own, and you'll forget it was ever there.
