*A health education handout for coaches, ministry leaders, and individuals weighing whether to pursue Live Blood Analysis (LBA)*

-----

## TL;DR

- **Live Blood Analysis (LBA) — a fingertip blood drop viewed live under a darkfield microscope — is not accepted as a valid diagnostic test by mainstream medicine**, and the small body of peer-reviewed research that exists shows poor reliability and very low accuracy.[^1][^2]
- **Many of the “findings” LBA practitioners point out — rouleaux stacks, “crystals,” moving “parasites,” and “fungal forms” — are well-documented drying and handling artifacts or normal blood vesicles, not signs of disease.**[^3][^4]
- **The session can feel deeply convincing because of the visual drama and the relationship with the practitioner, but using LBA as a basis for diagnosis or treatment carries real risks**: missed serious illness, false anxiety, costly supplement protocols, and — in documented criminal cases — direct harm.[^5][^6]

-----

## 1. What Is Live Blood Analysis?

Live Blood Analysis (LBA) — also called **live blood cell analysis, nutritional blood analysis, live cell microscopy, Hemaview, the Bradford Assessment, or nutritional microscopy** — is a procedure in which a practitioner pricks your finger, places a single drop of blood on a glass slide under a coverslip, and projects the magnified image onto a video monitor that you watch together.[^7][^8] The microscope used is a **darkfield microscope** — an instrument that lights the sample from the side so cells appear bright against a black background, the same way dust looks lit up in a sunbeam.

Practitioners typically say they can see, in real time, things like:

- **Rouleaux** (red blood cells stacked like coins)
- White blood cell activity
- “**Crystalline structures**” said to represent toxins, uric acid, or undigested protein
- pH or “acidity” indicators
- “**Parasites**,” “**fungal forms**,” or “**candida**”
- “**Free radical damage**” (often viewed on a separate dried sample, sometimes called the Oxidative Stress Test or HLB test)[^9][^10]

These observations are then interpreted as nutritional deficiencies, candida overgrowth, toxin accumulation, organ stress, poor circulation, or weakened immunity.[^9][^11]

**Who offers it?** Naturopaths, chiropractors, nutritionists, “nutritional microscopists,” and alternative health coaches.[^7] **LBA is not a regulated medical practice in most countries**, and training is typically a private 12-week online course (~$995) or a 3-day workshop from a microscope vendor such as Biomedx.[^11]

**Typical costs:** Sessions range from about **$79 to $475** per visit; one Texas clinic charges $475 for the initial visit and $225 for follow-ups.[^12] Microscopes and accessories start around **$4,600–$5,000** for practitioners.[^13] Follow-up appointments and supplement protocols are usually recommended and can add hundreds of dollars per month.

**Historical context.** LBA traces to **Günther Enderlein (1872–1968)**, a German zoologist who in his 1925 book *Bakterien-Cyklogenie* proposed that bacteria in the blood change form through different “life cycles” — a theory called pleomorphism that mainstream microbiology rejected long ago.[^14][^1] The modern American version was developed by **Robert W. Bradford (1931–2011)**, who built the **Bradford Variable Projection Microscope** and partnered with American Biologics in Chula Vista, California, to sell related supplements.[^15] Other influential figures include **Robert O. Young**, author of the *pH Miracle* book series.[^16]

## 2. The Evidence Landscape

The peer-reviewed literature on LBA is small but consistent.

**Teut, Lüdtke, and Warning (2006)** tested two trained darkfield specialists on 48 capillary blood samples from 24 patients with diabetes. The study, published in *Alternative Therapies in Health and Medicine*, concluded that “Enderlein darkfield analysis is very difficult to standardize and that the reliability of the diagnostic test is low.”[^1] In plain language: two trained practitioners looking at the same blood often did not see the same things.

**El-Safadi and colleagues (2005)**, publishing in *Forschende Komplementärmedizin*, asked a trained darkfield practitioner — without knowing patients’ diagnoses — to identify cancer in 110 patients. Of 12 patients with confirmed metastatic cancer, only 3 were identified. Sensitivity was **0.25**, specificity **0.64**, positive predictive value **0.09**.[^2] The authors concluded darkfield microscopy “does not seem to reliably detect the presence of cancer.”

An Australian validity study (Ramshaw et al., 2016, *Advances in Integrative Medicine*) — partly funded by an LBA equipment retailer — tested whether the technique could screen for iron and B12 deficiency in 29 participants.[^17] The study found some cell-size measurements correlated with lab results but concluded that “further research into all FCB-DM parameters is required to validate their use.” No subsequent validation has appeared.

**Mauerhofer and colleagues (2017)**, in *Scientific Reports*, examined the “bacteria-like” structures that LBA proponents claim to see. Using lipid analysis and Western blotting, they showed these structures are **non-living membrane vesicles and protein aggregates** — not microorganisms.[^3]

**Professional position statements.** No major medical body — the American Society of Hematology, the American Society for Clinical Pathology, the College of American Pathologists, or the FDA — recognizes LBA as a valid diagnostic test. Andrew Weil, MD, a leading integrative medicine physician, has written that dark-field microscopy combined with live blood analysis “may sound like cutting-edge science, but it is old-fashioned hokum.”[^18] Edzard Ernst, professor of complementary medicine, concludes “No credible scientific studies have demonstrated the reliability of LBA.”[^8]

**FDA and regulatory actions.**

- In July 2006, the FDA issued a consumer warning against **Bismacine** (also called Chromacine), an injectable bismuth product manufactured by Robert Bradford’s American Biologics and frequently prescribed alongside LBA findings. The FDA warned it was “not approved for anything” and was linked to one death and several injuries.[^19]
- In September 2020, the FTC sent **Robert O. Young** a warning letter for unsubstantiated COVID-19 claims tied to his pH Miracle supplement business.[^16]
- On **May 28, 2025, Judge Laura Duffy of Vista Superior Court sentenced Young to five years and eight months in California state prison** for four felony counts including willful abuse of 79-year-old Jane Clayson, who suffered liver and thyroid disease.[^20][^21]
- In November 2018, a San Diego Superior Court jury in *Kali v. Young* awarded cancer patient Dawn Kali **$90 million in compensatory damages plus $15 million in punitive damages**, after Young advised her to forgo chemotherapy; the trial judge later reduced the verdict to just over $25 million.[^16]
- In the UK, LBA practitioner **Errol Denton** was convicted in March 2014 under the Cancer Act 1939.[^14]
- In June 2023, **Jaqlyn Tinaro** of Sarasota, Florida, was arrested on four felony counts of unlicensed practice of a healthcare profession after using LBA to prescribe IV ozone therapy, parasite medications, and thyroid drugs.[^22]

## 3. What Darkfield Microscopy Actually Is — and Isn’t

Darkfield microscopy is a **real and legitimate** laboratory technique. It works by blocking the central light path so only light scattered by the specimen reaches the lens, making transparent objects glow against a dark background.

**Where it is genuinely useful:**

- **Diagnosing syphilis** by spotting the corkscrew-shaped *Treponema pallidum* spirochete in fresh fluid from a skin lesion. Per the Canadian Public Health Laboratory Network guidelines, “the sensitivity of dark-field microscopy for the diagnosis of syphilis is approximately 86% to 97%…and specificity is 100%” when performed properly.[^23]
- Examining other thin, motile spirochetes and certain protozoa.
- Some research-grade imaging of unstained living cells.[^24]

**What darkfield microscopy of finger-prick blood cannot reliably do:**

- Diagnose nutritional deficiencies. Iron, B12, vitamin D, magnesium, and folate are diagnosed by blood chemistry, not by appearance under a microscope.
- Diagnose candida overgrowth, cancer, parasites, “toxins,” or “acidity.”
- Replace the routine **brightfield, stained peripheral smear** used in hematology labs, where features like cell shape, inclusions, and abnormal white cells are evaluated against trained morphology standards.

## 4. Why LBA Interpretations Raise Serious Scientific Concerns

Most of what LBA practitioners point out is genuinely visible on the screen. The problem is what they *say* it means.

**Rouleaux (stacks of cells) is a normal artifact.** The American Society of Hematology image bank notes that the appearance of rouleaux “may be artificially caused by a poor preparation of the smear or by viewing the slide in a thickened area.”[^4] True pathological rouleaux on a stained smear can indicate inflammation or multiple myeloma — but you cannot tell from a fresh wet drop whether stacking reflects disease or simply the way the drop is drying near the coverslip edge.

**“Crystals” are usually drying artifacts.** As a blood drop dries on a slide, dissolved salts crystallize. Microscopic dust, glass shards, fibrin strands, or stain precipitates are routinely misidentified as “uric acid,” “cholesterol plaques,” or “toxins.”[^25]

**Moving “parasites” are usually moving cells, bubbles, fibrin, or vesicles.** As shown by Mauerhofer et al. (2017), the small refringent particles that swim and divide in fresh blood are membrane vesicles and protein clumps, not living organisms.[^3] True bloodborne parasites (malaria, babesia, trypanosomes) require stained smears and species-specific identification.

**Blood changes rapidly on a slide.** Within minutes, red cells crenate (spike), echinocytes form, and proteins aggregate. Many “findings” simply reflect how long the drop has sat under the coverslip.

**There is no standardized interpretation guide.** Different LBA schools (Enderlein, Bradford, Biomedx, Hemaview, Naturecure) teach different “signs” with different names and meanings. There is no demonstrated inter-rater reliability — two practitioners often disagree on the same slide.[^1]

**The findings do not correlate with clinical status.** El-Safadi et al. (2005) showed sensitivity of only 25% for known metastatic cancer.[^2]

## 5. Specific Claims — Examined Directly

**“I saw live parasites moving in my blood.”** What is most often moving is Brownian motion of tiny particles, fibrin strands shifting as the drop dries, or membrane vesicles.[^3] Real bloodborne parasites are rare in developed countries and are diagnosed by stained thick and thin smears.

**“My blood showed candida overgrowth.”** *Candida* in the bloodstream (candidemia) is a serious, life-threatening hospital-level infection, almost always in severely immunocompromised patients.[^26][^27] A walking, talking person in a wellness office does not have visible candida circulating in their blood. The vague “systemic candida” syndrome popular in some alternative-health circles is not recognized by infectious-disease medicine.

**“Crystalline structures show toxins or undigested protein.”** What is most often seen is salt crystallization from a drying sample, glass debris, or stain precipitate.[^25]

**“Red blood cells were clumped — poor circulation or bad nutrition.”** Real-time clumping at the slide edge is normal as blood begins to clot on glass and does not indicate circulation problems inside blood vessels.[^4]

**“My blood showed free radical damage.”** No reproducible biomarker for free radical damage can be seen with darkfield microscopy. Oxidative stress is measured in research labs with assays like F2-isoprostanes, not by eye on a drying drop.

**“LBA detected vitamin and mineral deficiencies.”** Iron, B12, vitamin D, and other levels are measured by specific blood chemistry assays. The one study attempting validation found the technique was inadequately reliable for screening.[^17]

**“My blood looked much better after treatment.”** Two factors explain this almost entirely: practitioner narration and normal variation between sessions. No controlled study shows “improved” LBA images predict any real health improvement.[^8]

**“It’s more comprehensive than regular lab tests.”** A standard CBC measures more than 15 specific values with known population reference ranges. LBA produces zero quantitative values.[^28]

## 6. Legitimate Blood Testing — What Doctors Actually Order

These are tests with published reference ranges, automated analyzers, external proficiency programs, and decades of clinical correlation:

- **Complete Blood Count (CBC):** counts and measures red cells, white cells (with differential), platelets, hemoglobin, hematocrit, MCV, MCH, RDW.[^28] Detects anemia, infection, leukemia, clotting issues, and many other conditions.
- **Comprehensive Metabolic Panel (CMP):** glucose, kidney function (BUN, creatinine), electrolytes, and liver enzymes (ALT, AST, bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, total protein).
- **Lipid panel:** total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides.
- **Thyroid panel:** TSH, free T4, sometimes free T3.
- **Nutrient testing:** ferritin and full iron studies, vitamin B12, folate, 25-OH vitamin D, serum or RBC magnesium.
- **Inflammatory markers:** C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR).
- **Peripheral blood smear:** a stained slide examined under brightfield by a trained hematologist or medical laboratory scientist — the conventional standard for evaluating blood cell shape and structure.

Most of these are covered by insurance when ordered for a clinical reason. Even without insurance, retail labs (Quest, LabCorp) offer a CBC for roughly $30 and a full panel for under $200.

## 7. Why LBA Feels So Convincing

Smart, careful people leave LBA sessions feeling they finally have answers. There are real psychological reasons for that:

- **Visual impact.** Seeing your own blood pulsing on a giant screen is unforgettable. The mind tends to accept any plausible narrative attached to such a powerful image.[^29]
- **Practitioner narration.** The expert pointing and naming structures gives an authoritative running commentary.
- **Therapeutic alliance.** Research on placebo effects shows that warm, attentive practitioners produce real symptom improvement regardless of treatment specifics — and LBA sessions tend to involve long appointments and personal attention.[^30]
- **Before/after effect.** Hydration, rest, and a slightly cleaner slide preparation can make follow-up images look “better.”
- **Confirmation bias.** If you arrived already suspecting candida or “toxins,” the slide easily seems to confirm it.[^29]
- **Effort justification.** People who have already spent significant time and money want the experience to be valuable.

None of this means clients are foolish. It means LBA is an unusually effective package for *feeling* informed, even when the information has no diagnostic basis.

## 8. Financial and Ethical Considerations

- Initial sessions typically run **$80–$475**, with follow-ups recommended every few weeks.[^12]
- Supplement protocols often cost **hundreds of dollars per month**, sometimes for years.
- The practitioner usually **both interprets the test and sells the products** that supposedly fix what the test “found” — a built-in conflict of interest.[^13]
- Marketing language often promises early detection of “imbalances years before disease appears” — a claim no peer-reviewed evidence supports.
- **Insurance does not cover LBA** because it is not a validated diagnostic test.

-----

## 9. Risks and Harms

- **Missed or delayed diagnosis.** Real blood disorders — iron-deficiency anemia, B12 deficiency, leukemia, lymphoma, clotting disorders, multiple myeloma — are diagnosed by tests LBA cannot perform.
- **False diagnoses creating anxiety.** A documented case report describes a 45-year-old woman who was told she had “blood parasites” after an LBA session and presented to an infectious-disease specialist in distress, with no actual infection.[^31]
- **Over-supplementation.** Supplement-related liver failure requiring U.S. transplant waitlisting rose roughly eightfold — from 2.9% to 24.1% — between 1995 and 2020.[^32] A 2017 review found herbal and dietary supplement-induced injury accounts for about 20% of all drug-induced liver injury cases nationally.[^33]
- **Children at risk.** Restrictive “anti-candida” or “alkaline” diets imposed on children based on LBA findings can cause real nutritional harm.
- **Worst-case fatalities.** In multiple criminal cases, terminally ill patients were steered away from conventional care after LBA-based assessments. Dawn Kali, a stage-IV breast cancer patient who relied on Robert O. Young’s LBA-driven advice, won $90 million in compensatory damages.[^16] Several patients who received Bismacine injections prescribed after LBA readings were hospitalized or died.[^19]

## 10. Common Ground — What Genuinely Supports Blood Health

You do not need an unvalidated test to know what helps your body. Evidence consistently supports:

- **Anti-inflammatory eating patterns:** plenty of vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and seeds; less ultra-processed food, sugar, and red/processed meat.
- **Adequate hydration:** dehydration genuinely does make blood more viscous.
- **Regular physical activity:** 150 minutes per week of moderate movement supports cardiovascular and metabolic health.
- **Quality sleep** (7–9 hours for most adults) and stress management.
- **Reducing avoidable exposures:** tobacco, excess alcohol, and known environmental toxins.
- **Routine preventive screening:** age-appropriate labs, blood pressure checks, cancer screenings.

## 11. Navigating the Decision — Parallel Perspectives

**Potential benefits of pursuing LBA**

- A vivid, memorable visual experience that can be motivating.
- Long appointments with attentive practitioners.
- Possible lifestyle nudges that turn out to be helpful even if the test wasn’t.

**Potential risks of pursuing LBA**

- False reassurance about a real disease.
- False alarm about a nonexistent one.
- Significant ongoing cost in money, time, and supplements.
- Decisions based on inaccurate information.

**Potential benefits of declining LBA**

- Resources redirected toward validated testing and care.
- Less anxiety from spurious findings.
- A clearer picture of actual health from CBC, CMP, and targeted labs.

**Potential risks of declining LBA**

- Missing a motivating experience. (No documented health risk has been identified from declining LBA.)

## 12. Practical Guidance

**Red flags for unvalidated testing**

- A single test claimed to diagnose dozens of unrelated conditions.
- The practitioner who interprets the test also sells the recommended supplements.
- Insurance won’t cover it because “they don’t want you to know.”
- Claims to detect disease “years before symptoms.”
- Bold disease names (“candida overgrowth,” “heavy metal toxicity”) presented as definitive diagnoses without confirmatory testing.

**Questions worth asking**

- What peer-reviewed studies show this test is reliable and accurate?
- What is the sensitivity and specificity?
- Do you sell the products you recommend based on results?
- If two trained practitioners read my sample blinded, would they agree?

**When to seek conventional blood testing**

- Unexplained fatigue, weakness, or shortness of breath
- Easy bruising or bleeding
- Persistent fevers, drenching night sweats, unintended weight loss
- New, unexplained pain, especially bone or abdominal pain
- Routine preventive care, especially after age 40 or with a family history of blood disorders

**Emergency symptoms — go to urgent care or the ER**

- Chest pain, sudden severe shortness of breath, fainting
- One-sided weakness, slurred speech, facial droop, sudden severe headache
- Heavy unexplained bleeding or large unexplained bruises
- High fever with confusion, rash, or severe pain

## 13. A Reflection on Discernment

Caring about your health is good. So is being skeptical — including of skeptics. The goal is not to “win” against any practitioner, but to use careful thinking the way you would care for any precious thing.

A few simple disciplines tend to protect us:

- **Slow down before any expensive commitment.** Real health problems can wait a week for a second opinion. Real solutions do not require urgency-based sales.
- **Separate the relationship from the test.** A practitioner can be kind and sincere and still be using a method that doesn’t work.
- **Be honest about what the evidence shows.** Truthfulness about our bodies is a form of stewardship.
- **Hold compassion for yourself if you’ve used LBA.** Smart, thoughtful people have. Recognizing the limits of a method is not a verdict on your worth or your judgment.

The most important truths about health — sleep, food, movement, relationships, peace of mind — are not hidden in a drop of blood. They are visible in the way we live each day.

-----

## Recommendations

**If you have already had LBA done:**

1. Bring the practitioner’s report to a licensed primary care clinician and ask for the corresponding validated tests (CBC, CMP, lipid panel, thyroid panel, ferritin, B12, vitamin D).
1. Do not stop any prescribed medication or delay any planned conventional care based on the LBA reading.
1. Review any supplements you were sold with a pharmacist or physician; some can cause real harm at high doses.

**If you are considering LBA:**

1. Before booking, ask the practitioner for the peer-reviewed evidence supporting their specific claims. The absence of such evidence is itself the answer.
1. Spend the money instead on a direct-to-consumer panel through a CLIA-certified lab (Quest, LabCorp, or similar) — for $100–$200 you can obtain a far more informative set of validated values.
1. If you want a deep conversation about diet, sleep, and lifestyle, book a session with a registered dietitian or board-certified lifestyle medicine physician.

**If you are a health coach or ministry leader:**

1. Do not refer clients to LBA practitioners for diagnosis.
1. When clients mention LBA findings, respond with curiosity and compassion — not contempt — and gently redirect to validated testing.
1. Keep a list of trusted clinicians who will see uninsured or under-insured clients for basic labs.
