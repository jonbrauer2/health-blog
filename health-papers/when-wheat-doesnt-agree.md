## Two Stories, One Grain

Maria was tired for most of her thirties. Three times her doctor treated her low iron, and three times it came back. Her stomach was only "a little touchy." Then a new doctor ordered a celiac blood test, and she finally had an answer. Her long wait wasn't unusual. In a survey of nearly 1,700 people with celiac disease, the typical delay from first symptoms to diagnosis was about two years, the average was more than seven, and women waited longer than men.[^1]

Dev cut out bread on a friend's advice, and his bloating faded. His celiac tests, done while he was still eating wheat, were normal. He feels better, yet people tell him it's all in his head.

Both are paying honest attention to their bodies. The trouble is that "gluten intolerance" is one label for several quite different conditions. One is autoimmune, one is an allergy that can be life-threatening, and one is still being defined by researchers.[^2] This handout explains each one, how to tell them apart, and how to live well, including if you love your bread.

## What Gluten Is and Where It Hides

Gluten is a family of storage proteins found in wheat, barley, and rye. In wheat, the two main groups are gliadins and glutenins, which together give dough its stretch. Wheat's close relatives carry the same proteins: spelt, farro (emmer), einkorn, kamut (khorasan wheat), durum, semolina, and triticale, a wheat–rye cross.[^3]

Gluten is rich in proline and glutamine, which makes some fragments hard for digestive enzymes to break down, so long pieces reach the small intestine intact.[^4] In most people that's harmless; in celiac disease, those pieces set off an immune reaction.

**The oats question.** Pure oats don't contain gluten, but most oats are grown, stored, and milled alongside wheat and barley, so ordinary oats are often contaminated. A systematic review of 28 studies found no clear harm from adding uncontaminated oats to a gluten-free diet in celiac disease, though the quality of the evidence was low.[^5] A small minority of people with celiac disease also react to avenin, the protein in oats. That's why guidelines suggest choosing certified gluten-free oats and checking in with your care team when you add them.[^3]

> **Key Fact:** Oats are naturally gluten-free, but regular oats are commonly cross-contaminated with wheat or barley. People with celiac disease should choose oats labeled gluten-free.[^5]

**Hidden gluten** turns up in more places than bread and pasta:

- Soy sauce (most brands are brewed with wheat)
- Malt, malt vinegar, malt flavoring, and most beer
- Soups, gravies, sauces, and marinades thickened with flour
- Seitan ("vital wheat gluten") and many plant-based meat alternatives
- Breaded or battered foods, and fries cooked in shared fryers
- Communion wafers and other ceremonial breads
- Some medications and supplements (ask your pharmacist about fillers)
- Lip balms and lipsticks, which can be swallowed in small amounts

## A Spectrum of Gluten- and Wheat-Related Conditions

### Celiac Disease

Celiac disease is an autoimmune condition. When someone with the right genes eats gluten, their immune system attacks the lining of the small intestine. That lining is covered with villi, tiny finger-like projections that absorb nutrients. In celiac disease the villi flatten, so less gets absorbed.[^3]

Nearly everyone with celiac disease carries one of two gene types, HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8. Yet about a third of people carry these genes, and most never develop the disease, so the genes are necessary but not sufficient. A global analysis of 96 studies found celiac antibodies in 1.4% of people and biopsy-confirmed disease in 0.7%, with higher rates in women and children.[^6] Many people who have it haven't been diagnosed.

> **Key Fact:** Roughly 1 in 100 people worldwide have celiac disease, and many of them don't know it.[^6]

Celiac disease looks different from person to person: classical symptoms such as diarrhea, weight loss, or poor growth; non-classical problems like anemia, fatigue, or bone loss; or "silent" disease with damage but no obvious symptoms. It is more common in people with type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, Down syndrome, Turner syndrome, or a parent, sibling, or child with celiac disease.[^3]

Left untreated, celiac disease can lead to iron-deficiency anemia, osteoporosis, infertility and pregnancy complications, nerve damage, and, rarely, a type of intestinal lymphoma. A small number of people keep having intestinal damage despite a truly strict gluten-free diet. This is called refractory celiac disease, and it needs specialist care.[^7]

### Dermatitis Herpetiformis

Dermatitis herpetiformis (DH) is an intensely itchy rash of small blisters and bumps, often on the elbows, knees, buttocks, and scalp. It is celiac disease showing up in the skin. It's diagnosed with a small skin biopsy taken next to the rash, and it's treated with a lifelong gluten-free diet, often with a prescription medicine called dapsone for the first months while the diet takes effect.[^3]

### Gluten Ataxia and the Nervous System

Some people with celiac disease, and some with celiac-type antibodies but little gut damage, develop balance problems (ataxia), numbness, tingling, or other neurological symptoms. One research group in England considers "gluten ataxia" an under-recognized cause of unexplained balance problems;[^8] other experts question how common it is and how best to test for it. Gluten-related nerve problems are real in celiac disease, but how often they occur without it remains open. New balance trouble or numbness always deserves prompt attention.

### Wheat Allergy

Wheat allergy is a completely different process. It's an IgE-mediated allergy, involving the same antibody type as peanut allergy. Reactions usually come on within minutes to a couple of hours and can include hives, swelling, vomiting, wheezing, and, at worst, anaphylaxis, a severe whole-body reaction that can be fatal. Special forms include *baker's asthma*, from breathing flour dust at work, and *wheat-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis*, where eating wheat and then exercising within a few hours triggers a reaction that neither causes alone.[^9]

Avoidance targets **wheat** specifically. Many people with wheat allergy can eat barley and rye, which people with celiac disease cannot. People at risk of anaphylaxis are usually prescribed an epinephrine auto-injector and should carry it at all times.[^9]

### Non-Celiac Gluten (or Wheat) Sensitivity

Non-celiac gluten sensitivity, often called non-celiac wheat sensitivity, describes people who get gut symptoms (bloating, pain, changes in bowel habits) and often whole-body symptoms (fatigue, brain fog, headaches) after eating wheat or gluten, but who don't have celiac disease or wheat allergy. There is no validated blood test for it. It is a diagnosis of exclusion, meaning other conditions are ruled out first.[^10]

A 2015 expert panel meeting in Salerno, Italy, proposed a research gold standard for diagnosis. After a gluten-free period, the person goes through a blinded challenge in which neither they nor the testers know whether they're getting gluten or a placebo.[^10]

How common is it? A 2025 review estimated that about 10% of people worldwide report sensitivity to gluten or wheat, but only 16–30% of those people react to gluten specifically when tested blind.[^2]

Researchers are studying several possible triggers, and different people likely react to different ones. These include *fructans*, a type of FODMAP (fermentable carbohydrates that can cause gas and bloating); *amylase-trypsin inhibitors* (ATIs), wheat proteins that activated the innate immune system in laboratory studies;[^11] *wheat germ agglutinin*, studied mostly in the lab so far; and gut-brain signaling, including the *nocebo effect*, where expecting harm makes symptoms worse. A 2016 study found blood markers of immune activation and intestinal cell damage in people with wheat sensitivity, which suggests something measurable is happening in at least some of them.[^12]

So is it real? The symptoms are real and can be disabling, but for many people the trigger isn't gluten itself.

### The Conditions Side by Side

| | Celiac disease | Dermatitis herpetiformis | Gluten ataxia | Wheat allergy | Non-celiac sensitivity |

| **What's happening** | Autoimmune attack on the small intestine | Skin form of celiac disease | Immune-related nerve involvement | IgE allergy to wheat proteins | Not fully known; likely several triggers |

## Recognizing the Symptoms

Digestive symptoms are the best known: diarrhea, constipation, bloating, gas, belly pain, nausea, and unintended weight loss. Many people, especially adults, have mostly non-digestive symptoms instead:[^3]

- Tiredness that rest doesn't fix
- Iron-deficiency anemia that keeps coming back
- Brain fog, headaches, low mood, or anxiety
- Joint pain and mouth ulcers
- An itchy, blistering rash
- Numbness or tingling in the hands or feet
- In children: slow growth, delayed puberty, or irritability

Because the symptoms are so varied and overlap with many common problems, diagnosis has often taken years.[^1]

## Getting the Right Answer: Testing and Diagnosis

### Test First, Then Change Your Diet

**If you suspect celiac disease, get tested before you go gluten-free.** Celiac blood tests measure your immune reaction to gluten, and the biopsy looks for the damage gluten causes. Take gluten away and the antibodies fade and the intestine heals, so the tests can come back falsely normal. Going back to gluten for testing, called a gluten challenge, is unpleasant and slow. Current European adult guidance recommends at least 3 grams of gluten a day, about one to two slices of wheat bread, for at least six weeks before testing.[^7]

> **Key Fact:** Celiac blood tests and biopsies can turn falsely negative after you stop eating gluten. Test first, then change your diet.[^3]

### Standard Tests

The usual first test is tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA), together with a total IgA level. If IgA is low, clinicians use IgG-based tests such as deamidated gliadin peptide (DGP) IgG. The endomysial antibody (EMA) test is very specific and is sometimes used to confirm results. For most adults, diagnosis is confirmed with an upper endoscopy, in which a thin camera is passed to the small intestine and several tiny tissue samples are taken.[^3]

Since 2020, European pediatric guidelines have allowed children to be diagnosed without a biopsy when tTG-IgA is at least 10 times the upper limit of normal and a second blood sample is EMA-positive.[^13] Adult guidance is moving more cautiously. The 2023 U.S. guideline still recommends biopsy for adults, while allowing that very high antibody results can indicate "likely celiac disease" in adults who are unable or unwilling to have an endoscopy.[^3] European adult guidance from 2025 conditionally allows a no-biopsy diagnosis for adults under 45 with very high tTG-IgA, decided together with a specialist.[^7]

HLA genetic testing is useful for ruling celiac disease *out*, not in. If you don't carry DQ2 or DQ8, celiac disease is very unlikely.[^3] Wheat allergy is tested by an allergist using skin-prick tests and wheat-specific IgE blood tests, sometimes followed by a supervised food challenge.[^9]

### Tests on the Evidence Spectrum

**Gluten immunogenic peptide (GIP) tests** in stool or urine detect recently eaten gluten. They're an emerging tool researchers use to check how well people are sticking to the diet, but they can't diagnose celiac disease, and the U.S. guideline doesn't recommend routine use of home gluten-detection devices.[^3]

**IgG food "sensitivity" panels** are popular in some wellness settings, and some people report that acting on the results helped. Research suggests food-specific IgG mostly reflects exposure, meaning your body has met the food, not that it is harming you, and allergy specialists discourage these tests for identifying food reactions.[^14]

### Expanded Wheat and Food-Reactivity Panels

Some specialty labs offer detailed wheat panels, such as the Wheat Zoomer and similar arrays, that go well beyond standard celiac testing. They measure IgG, IgA, and complement reactions to many gluten and non-gluten wheat proteins, along with markers meant to reflect gut barrier function.[^15] Some also include celiac-related antibodies, HLA genes, and wheat IgE. Practitioners often use them to look for wheat reactivity that standard tests miss.

**Why practitioners and patients use them.** Someone with real symptoms and normal celiac results often leaves the doctor's office with no explanation. A detailed report offers one, and many people feel better after following it. The underlying technology is also credible. Researchers at a major U.S. medical center, working with scientists from the lab that later developed this panel, used a peptide microarray to map gluten fragments in celiac disease and reported high accuracy in telling people with celiac disease apart from controls.[^16]

**What research shows so far.** That validation was for celiac disease, which has a clear reference standard, the biopsy, to test against. Non-celiac sensitivity has no such standard besides the blinded challenge described above.[^2] To show that a panel detects it, researchers would need to show that its results predict who reacts in blinded testing, and we found no published study showing this. The lab states that its tests have not been cleared by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration. Its first food-sensitivity trial, released in 2024 as a not-yet-peer-reviewed preprint, followed 52 people with no comparison group.[^17] Without a comparison group, it's hard to tell whether people improved because of the test or because a thoughtful elimination diet, which usually lowers wheat fructans, tends to help anyway.[^18]

**Where views diverge.** Integrative practitioners emphasize individual patterns and how clients respond. Allergy and gastroenterology specialists caution that antibody results without validated meaning can lead to unnecessary restriction and expense.[^14]

**What would help.** Studies comparing panel results with blinded gluten and fructan challenges.

**What this means in practice.** These panels can be a starting point for conversation and for a structured, dietitian-guided elimination trial. They don't replace standard celiac and allergy testing, any celiac-related result should be confirmed with standard tests, and like other antibody tests they become less informative once someone has stopped eating gluten. Confidence that they diagnose non-celiac sensitivity: low.

**Structured elimination and reintroduction** is reasonable and often useful *after* celiac disease and wheat allergy have been ruled out. Working with a dietitian makes it more informative: remove likely triggers for a few weeks, then bring foods back one at a time and track what happens.[^10]

## Conventional Treatment and Care

For celiac disease and DH, treatment is a **strict, lifelong gluten-free diet**. The diet treats celiac disease; it doesn't cure it, and the immune reaction returns whenever gluten does.[^3] Good care usually includes:

- A registered dietitian who knows celiac disease, ideally soon after diagnosis
- Follow-up blood tests to confirm antibody levels are falling, and sometimes a later check on intestinal healing
- Bone density screening, since bone loss is common at diagnosis
- Checks of nutrient levels such as iron, folate, B12, vitamin D, and zinc; your clinician may recommend iron, vitamin D, or other supplements based on results
- A vaccination review, because some people with celiac disease have a less active spleen (hyposplenism), and pneumococcal vaccination is often suggested
- Dapsone for DH, and epinephrine for people with wheat allergy who are at risk of anaphylaxis
- Mental health support when the diet feels heavy

### The Treatment Pipeline

*Status checked as of September 28, 2026. This area changes quickly, so check for updates before relying on it.*

Researchers are developing medicines to protect against accidental exposure. None is approved, and all are being tested as additions to the diet.

- **Larazotide**, meant to tighten the gut barrier, was stopped in phase 3 in 2022.[^19]
- **TAK-062 (zamaglutenase)**, an enzyme that breaks down gluten, was discontinued in 2025, and **TAK-101**, a "tolerizing" nanoparticle, in 2026.[^20]
- **ZED1227/TAK-227**, which blocks an enzyme that makes gluten more inflammatory, protected the intestine in a 2021 trial.[^4] A larger follow-up trial reported mixed results in 2025, and the drug remains in development.[^20]
- **Latiglutenase**, an enzyme combination, showed some phase 2 benefit; its developer stopped work in 2025, and a successor company plans further trials.
- **KAN-101**, a liver-targeted tolerizing therapy, appeared safe in early trials, with fuller phase 2 results still awaited.[^21]
- **Anti-IL-15 antibodies** target a key immune signal. On September 2, 2026, the developer of TEV-53408 reported that it prevented gluten-induced intestinal damage versus placebo in an early (phase 2a) trial.[^22] Other immune-targeting drugs are in earlier trials.

These are hopeful developments, but none replaces the diet. Over-the-counter "gluten-digesting" enzymes, including AN-PEP products, are not a safe substitute for avoiding gluten in celiac disease.[^3]

## Living Well: Nutrition, Lifestyle, and Functional Approaches

### Build on Foods That Are Naturally Gluten-Free

The healthiest gluten-free diet looks a lot like any healthy diet: beans, lentils, vegetables, fruits, nuts, seeds, potatoes, and grains such as quinoa, buckwheat (which isn't related to wheat), millet, sorghum, amaranth, teff, rice, and certified oats if tolerated. Packaged gluten-free breads and snacks make life easier, but many are lower in fiber, B vitamins, and iron and higher in sugar, fat, and salt than the foods they replace.[^23]

Common nutrient gaps on a gluten-free diet include fiber, B vitamins, folate, iron, calcium, and vitamin D.[^23] Having your clinician test your levels and guide you works better than guessing with supplements.

If you eat mostly plants, losing seitan can feel like losing a staple. Good gluten-free proteins include lentils, chickpeas, beans, tofu, tempeh, edamame, quinoa, nuts, seeds, and certified gluten-free plant-based products.

### Diversify Your Grains

Rice tends to take up arsenic from soil and water. An analysis of U.S. national survey data found that people on a gluten-free diet had higher urinary arsenic and higher blood levels of mercury, lead, and cadmium than people not avoiding gluten.[^24] The health meaning of these levels is still being studied, but rotating grains rather than relying on rice at every meal is sensible. The gut microbiome, the bacteria living in your intestine, can also shift on a gluten-free diet.[^23]

### Cross-Contamination: Small Amounts Matter in Celiac Disease

In a classic trial, adults with celiac disease took capsules containing 0, 10, or 50 milligrams of gluten a day for three months. At 50 mg, a tiny fraction of a slice of bread, the intestinal lining measurably worsened; at 10 mg most did well, though one person relapsed.[^25]

At home, use a separate toaster or toaster bags, separate cutting boards and colanders (scratches and holes trap crumbs), and separate butter, jam, and peanut butter containers. When eating out, ask how food is prepared; shared fryers and grills are common culprits. For school, many families create a written plan with staff.

In the United States, the "gluten-free" label may be used only on foods with less than 20 parts per million (ppm) of gluten, which matches the international standard.[^26]

> **Key Fact:** The U.S. and international threshold for labeling a food "gluten-free" is less than 20 parts per million of gluten.[^26]

### When the Issue Is FODMAPs

For people with non-celiac sensitivity or irritable bowel syndrome, a short, dietitian-guided low-FODMAP diet can help identify triggers.[^18] It's meant to be temporary, followed by careful reintroduction.

### Gut Health, "Leaky Gut," and Functional Approaches

Many integrative practitioners focus on intestinal permeability, often called "leaky gut."

*Plausible and supported:* Gluten does increase gut permeability in celiac disease, and the protein zonulin helps regulate the junctions between intestinal cells. Researcher Alessio Fasano, who has financial ties to companies developing permeability-based treatments, has argued that zonulin-driven permeability may contribute to a range of chronic inflammatory diseases.[^27]

*Preliminary:* The idea that permeability drives many conditions beyond celiac disease is still being tested. A 2018 study found that a widely used commercial zonulin blood test did not detect the protein identified as zonulin and may have measured a different one, which makes many published zonulin results hard to interpret.[^28]

*Not supported:* Gut-healing protocols or supplements used *instead of* a gluten-free diet in celiac disease.

Some people with non-celiac sensitivity feel better with broader changes: more whole foods, fewer ultra-processed foods, fewer FODMAPs. That may reflect several changes at once, not gluten removal alone.[^2] Sleep, stress management, and physical activity also influence gut symptoms and are reasonable supports alongside medical care.

### The Emotional Side

A food diagnosis can bring real grief for family recipes, spontaneous meals out, and birthday cake. Anxiety and loneliness at food-centered gatherings are common. Research also links restrictive medical diets with disordered eating, including restricting far beyond what a condition requires.[^29] If fear of food is shrinking your life, tell your care team; it's treatable. Support groups, understanding friends, and a welcoming table make a real difference.

## Different Perspectives on Contested Questions

### 1. Should Everyone Avoid Gluten?

Popular books such as *Wheat Belly* and *Grain Brain* argue that modern wheat harms nearly everyone. Research in people without celiac disease mostly doesn't support this. A large U.S. study followed more than 110,000 health professionals for 26 years and found no link between gluten intake and heart disease; after accounting for refined grains, higher gluten intake was linked to slightly lower risk, likely reflecting whole grains.[^30] Whole grain intake is consistently associated with lower rates of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and early death.[^31]

Many people do feel better off wheat. That experience is real, but it may come from eating fewer ultra-processed foods or FODMAPs rather than from removing gluten. Long-term trials in people without celiac disease would help. Confidence that avoiding gluten benefits the general population: low.

> **Key Fact:** In people without celiac disease, avoiding gluten often means eating fewer whole grains, which are linked to better heart health.[^30]

### 2. Is Non-Celiac Sensitivity Really About Gluten?

A 2011 Australian trial found more symptoms on gluten than on placebo in people with self-reported sensitivity.[^32] The same team's 2013 follow-up, run after participants reduced FODMAPs, found no gluten-specific effect.[^33] A 2018 Norwegian trial found that fructans, not gluten, raised symptom scores.[^18] A 2017 analysis of ten blinded trials found that only about 16% of people with suspected sensitivity reacted specifically to gluten, while about 40% reacted as much or more to placebo.[^34] At the same time, blood-marker studies suggest real immune activity in some people.[^12]

Some researchers emphasize the immune findings; others emphasize fructans and the gut-brain connection. Standard challenge protocols and a reliable biomarker would help. The bottom line: symptoms are real, and triggers vary.

> **Key Fact:** In a blinded trial of people with self-reported gluten sensitivity, fructans, a wheat carbohydrate, caused more symptoms than gluten.[^18]

### 3. Modern Wheat, Ancient Grains, and Sourdough

A study of 60 German wheat varieties from 1891 to 2010, grown side by side, found that gliadins decreased and glutenins increased, with no clear rise in total gluten.[^35] Einkorn, emmer, and spelt all contain gluten and are not safe for celiac disease; whether people with non-celiac sensitivity tolerate them better has barely been studied.

A small study found that bread made with a special laboratory process, using sourdough bacteria plus fungal enzymes that broke gluten down to about 8 ppm, didn't harm people with celiac disease.[^36] Ordinary bakery sourdough is not gluten-free. Long fermentation does lower fructans, which may explain why some people with non-celiac sensitivity tolerate it better.

> **Key Fact:** Spelt, einkorn, emmer, and other "ancient" wheats contain gluten and are not safe for people with celiac disease.[^3]

### 4. Glyphosate and Gluten Disorders

A 2013 hypothesis paper proposed that the herbicide glyphosate contributes to celiac disease.[^37] It offered no new data, and no direct studies have shown that glyphosate causes celiac disease. Rising diagnoses have other plausible explanations, including better testing.[^6] Broader agricultural chemical concerns are legitimate but separate from celiac causation. Confidence: very low.

### 5. Gluten-Free Diets for Other Conditions

For irritable bowel syndrome, there's moderate evidence that some people improve, often through lower FODMAP intake.[^33] For autism, gluten-free/casein-free diets show limited, mixed results without consistent benefit in controlled trials. For Hashimoto's thyroiditis, rheumatoid arthritis, and psoriasis, most studies are small or preliminary, though celiac screening makes sense because these conditions tend to cluster together.[^3] Links with schizophrenia have been reported, but causation is uncertain. In every case, a gluten-free diet is an adjunct to explore with a clinician, not a replacement for treatment.

### 6. Infant Feeding and Prevention

Two large 2014 trials found that the timing of first gluten didn't change risk. In one, 944 high-risk infants received small amounts of gluten or placebo between 4 and 6 months of age, with no difference in outcome.[^38] In the other, 832 infants started gluten at 6 or 12 months; waiting delayed celiac disease but didn't prevent it.[^39] Neither found that breastfeeding was protective. A large study of more than 6,600 genetically at-risk children found that eating more gluten in early childhood was linked with higher risk, an association rather than proof.[^40]

Parents can offer gluten at a normal age in ordinary amounts and seek prompt testing if symptoms appear; they can't control their child's genes.

### 7. Whole Wheat in Plant-Forward Traditions

Many plant-forward and vegetarian-leaning health traditions treat whole-grain bread and wheat-gluten "meats" as staples, and for most people those foods fit well in a healthy diet.[^31] When wheat isn't an option, the underlying principles carry over completely: whole foods over refined ones, plenty of legumes and vegetables, fiber-rich grains, and home cooking. A gluten-free table can honor the same values.

### 8. Taking "Just a Little" Gluten

In celiac disease, damage appeared at 50 mg a day in the trial described earlier, and harm can occur without symptoms, so feeling fine isn't proof of safety.[^25] This is one area where the answer is clear: in celiac disease, "a little" is not fine. People with non-celiac sensitivity often tolerate some wheat, and their thresholds vary.[^2] People with wheat allergy may react to very small amounts and should follow their allergist's guidance.

### Weighing the Choices

| | **Potential benefits** | **Potential risks** |
|---|---|---|
| **Pursuing a gluten-free diet** | Symptom relief; intestinal healing in celiac disease; less itching in DH | Missed diagnosis if not tested first; nutrient gaps; cost; social strain; more heavy-metal exposure if rice-heavy; food anxiety |
| **Not pursuing it** | Flexibility; easier social eating; whole-grain benefits | Ongoing damage and complications in undiagnosed celiac disease; continued suffering in non-celiac sensitivity |

Financial interests exist on every side. The gluten-free food industry benefits when more people avoid gluten. Specialty testing companies sell panels that major medical organizations don't endorse for diagnosis. The supplement market sells gut-healing and enzyme products. Drug developers, and many researchers who consult for them, have a stake in trial outcomes.[^27] Knowing who profits doesn't make a claim wrong, but it's a reason to look closely.

## When to See a Clinician

Please see a clinician promptly if you or your child have:

- **Unexplained iron-deficiency anemia, weight loss, ongoing diarrhea, or poor growth in a child**
- **Any suspicion of celiac disease.** Get tested before changing your diet.
- **A parent, sibling, or child with celiac disease.** Testing relatives is often recommended, especially if you have symptoms.[^3]
- **Hives, swelling, trouble breathing, or vomiting soon after eating wheat, or reactions during exercise after eating.** Anaphylaxis is an emergency: use epinephrine if you have it and call emergency services.
- **An itchy, blistering rash**
- **Symptoms that continue despite a strict gluten-free diet.** Hidden gluten, refractory celiac disease, or a different condition may be the cause.
- **Balance problems, numbness, or tingling**
- **Fractures, osteoporosis, infertility, or recurrent miscarriage**
- **Fear of food that is leading to heavy restriction or weight loss**
- **Pregnancy, or a child's diagnosis, where nutrition needs close monitoring**

## How to Support Someone

If you're supporting a friend, family member, or client, useful support includes encouraging testing *before* diet changes, helping with meal planning, label reading, and setting up a safe kitchen, and offering emotional support, gentle accountability, and connections to registered dietitians. Hospitality matters too: gluten-free options at shared meals, including gluten-free bread or wafers for communion or ceremonial meals, let everyone share the table.

Some things are best left to clinicians: diagnosing conditions, interpreting lab results, recommending specific tests or supplement doses, and encouraging someone to reintroduce gluten against medical advice. If someone shares an expanded food-reactivity report, listen with interest and encourage them to review it with their clinician and a dietitian before restricting further.[^14]

Words that help:

- "It sounds like it might be worth asking your doctor about a celiac blood test before you cut gluten out, because the test works best while you're still eating it."
- "I'd love to cook for you. Can you walk me through what keeps a kitchen safe for you?"
- "A dietitian who knows celiac disease could really help with this. Would you like help finding one?"

## Moving Forward

Whatever brought you here, there is a clear path forward.

**If you've been newly diagnosed with celiac disease:**

- Book a visit with a dietitian who knows celiac disease.
- Clear out or separate the toaster, colander, cutting boards, and spreads.
- Learn the hidden sources of gluten and how to read labels.
- Ask about follow-up blood tests, bone density, nutrient checks, and vaccines.
- Suggest testing to your first-degree relatives.

**If you suspect a problem:**

- Keep eating gluten for now, and ask your clinician about celiac testing.
- If wheat causes hives, swelling, or breathing trouble, see an allergist promptly.
- Keep a symptom and food diary.
- Once celiac disease and allergy are ruled out, try a structured, dietitian-guided elimination and reintroduction.

Food has always been about more than nutrients; it's how we welcome and belong. A diagnosis can change what's on the table without changing who's welcome at it. When families, friends, and communities make room for everyone to eat safely, the shared table becomes a gift again.
