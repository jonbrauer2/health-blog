*A health and wellness handout for clients, families, and community members*

## Introduction: The Quiet Ingredients

Walk into nearly any kitchen in America and you will find two small containers tucked into a cupboard: a bright orange box of baking soda and a tin of baking powder. They have been household staples for over a century. They make biscuits puffy, pancakes fluffy, and muffins tender. Most of us never give them a second thought.

Yet behind their cheerful packaging lies an interesting—and sometimes overlooked—story about chemistry, nutrition, and how everyday ingredients interact with the body. Baking soda is a powerful alkali that can shift blood pH, alter electrolyte balance, and (in larger amounts) cause real medical emergencies. Many baking powders contain aluminum compounds and concentrated phosphate salts that have drawn ongoing scientific scrutiny. And both can quietly destroy several B vitamins right in your mixing bowl.

None of this means you need to throw your kitchen into upheaval. But understanding what these ingredients actually do—biochemically, nutritionally, and clinically—can help you make informed, intentional choices for yourself and your family.

---

## Section 1: Baking Soda Inside the Body

Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃) is, chemically speaking, an alkali. When it meets stomach acid, the two neutralize each other and release carbon dioxide gas—the same reaction that lifts a pancake. The trouble is that, unlike a pancake, the human body has narrow tolerances for pH and electrolyte shifts.

**Acid–base balance and metabolic alkalosis.** Each level teaspoon of baking soda delivers roughly 5 grams of sodium bicarbonate, equal to about 59 milliequivalents (mEq) of bicarbonate.[^1] The U.S. Food and Drug Administration considers up to 200 mEq per day generally safe in healthy young adults and 100 mEq per day in adults over 60—but these are upper limits, not goals.[^1] Beyond those amounts, the kidneys can no longer keep up, and blood pH rises into a state called metabolic alkalosis.

A 2022 case series in *Experimental and Therapeutic Medicine* documented a patient who developed metabolic alkalosis, acute hepatitis, and acute kidney injury after consuming about 20 grams of baking soda for self-treatment of gout.[^1] A more recent case report and systematic review in 2026 described a previously healthy man who escalated to ingesting 480 grams in a single day for "intestinal cleansing"; his blood pH reached 7.54 and serum sodium 162 mEq/L before treatment.[^2] The same review identified 78 cases of sodium bicarbonate toxicity in the medical literature, with chronic ingestion most often causing severe alkalosis and electrolyte imbalance, and acute massive ingestion causing gastric rupture with high mortality.

**Electrolyte fallout.** Excess bicarbonate promotes urinary loss of potassium and chloride, producing hypokalemia and hypochloremia.[^2][^3] The accompanying sodium load can drive hypernatremia and dehydration. Symptoms range from muscle weakness, cramps, and confusion to seizures, dangerous heart rhythms, and—rarely—cardiopulmonary arrest.[^4]

**Cardiovascular implications.** Alkalosis shifts the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the left, meaning red blood cells hold onto oxygen more tightly and release less of it to tissues, including the heart muscle.[^4] Combined with the high sodium load (one teaspoon of baking soda contains about 1,260 mg of sodium—well over half the entire American Heart Association daily target), the cardiovascular stress is real, especially in people with hypertension, heart failure, or kidney disease.

> **Did You Know?**
> One level teaspoon of baking soda contains roughly **1,260 mg of sodium**—about 55 percent of the American Heart Association's 2,300 mg daily upper limit, and 84 percent of its "ideal" 1,500 mg target.[^5]

**Gastric rupture—a rare but documented danger.** When concentrated baking soda meets a stomach full of acid and food, it can release carbon dioxide so rapidly that the stomach over-distends. A 2022 systematic review in the *Journal of Eating Disorders* identified 36 cases of spontaneous gastric rupture, ten of them clearly tied to sodium bicarbonate ingestion. The pooled mortality rate was 41.6 percent.[^6] Several cases have involved people taking baking soda to "settle" a heavy meal, to attempt a home detox before a drug test, or to relieve heartburn.[^7]

## Section 2: The Stomach, Digestion, and the "Acid Rebound" Question

Sodium bicarbonate works as an antacid by neutralizing hydrochloric acid in the stomach, raising gastric pH from around 1 to as high as 5.[^8] That sounds helpful, and for occasional, small doses it can be. But several issues complicate the picture.

**Carbon dioxide and gastric distension.** The neutralization reaction generates CO₂ gas, producing belching, bloating, and, in vulnerable stomachs, mechanical strain.[^9]

**Acid rebound.** The classic "acid rebound"—a paradoxical surge of acid secretion after the antacid wears off—is most strongly documented with calcium carbonate, not sodium bicarbonate.[^10][^11] However, the alkaline tide phenomenon and CO₂-induced gastrin release can still stimulate additional acid secretion, undermining long-term symptom relief.[^10]

**Pepsin, enzymes, and absorption.** Pepsin, the stomach's primary protein-digesting enzyme, requires an acidic environment (pH 1.5–3.5). Repeatedly raising gastric pH impairs protein digestion and reduces the release and absorption of vitamin B12, non-heme iron, calcium, and magnesium—nutrients whose absorption depends on acidic conditions.[^12]

**Ulcers and gastric health.** Although sodium bicarbonate provides momentary relief from heartburn, it does not heal ulcers and is no longer recommended for chronic dyspepsia. Modern guidelines prefer H2 blockers and proton pump inhibitors precisely because antacids only mask symptoms and may delay diagnosis of conditions like *H. pylori* infection or gastric cancer.[^8]

## Section 3: What Baking Soda Does to Vitamins

Even when used in ordinary amounts in baking, sodium bicarbonate alters the chemistry of the dough or batter—and several vitamins are casualties.

**Thiamine (vitamin B1).** Thiamine is famously sensitive to heat and alkaline conditions. It is stable between pH 2 and 4 but degrades rapidly above pH 6.[^13][^14] When alkaline leaveners raise dough pH, thiamine destruction accelerates. In some processed cereal and noodle products, up to about 60 percent of added thiamine can be lost due to combined heat and alkaline pH.[^14] A study in fresh yellow alkaline noodles—where alkaline salts are deliberately added—showed thiamine losses several times higher than in standard salted noodles.[^15]

**Vitamin C.** Ascorbic acid is also unstable in alkaline solutions and is largely destroyed when baking soda is added to vegetables (a once-common practice for keeping them green) or to baked goods.

**Riboflavin (B2) and folate.** Riboflavin is moderately sensitive to alkaline pH, and folate is degraded by both heat and alkaline conditions, although the magnitude of loss in home baking is generally smaller than that of thiamine and vitamin C.

**B12, B6, and niacin.** Vitamin B12 is sensitive to alkali, while niacin and vitamin B6 are relatively stable.[^14]

> **Key Facts: Vitamin Losses in Alkaline Baking**
> - Thiamine (B1) destruction can reach **60% or more** in cereal-based products with alkaline leaveners.[^14]
> - Vitamin C is largely lost when baking soda is added to dough or vegetables.
> - Yeast-leavened sourdough and long-fermented breads can actually **increase** B-vitamin content compared with their starting flour.[^16]

## Section 4: Sodium, Calcium, and Bone Health

Sodium intake interacts with calcium balance in a way few people realize. The kidneys excrete sodium and calcium together; for roughly every 2,300 mg of sodium lost in urine, an additional 30–40 mg of calcium goes with it.[^17] That calcium has to come from somewhere—often, bone.

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis specifically focused on postmenopausal women found that high-sodium diets increased urinary calcium excretion by a pooled mean of 29.38 mg per day (95% CI: 12.31–46.46), with measurable links to bone resorption markers.[^18] Cross-sectional research in young women has tied higher 24-hour urinary sodium to lower hip bone mineral density, particularly in women with lower calcium intakes.[^17]

For a person who relies heavily on baking-soda-leavened biscuits, pancakes, or self-rising flour products, the cumulative sodium contribution can be significant—and the bone consequences are subtle but real, especially when calcium intake is marginal.

## Section 5: What's Inside Baking Powder

Baking powder is essentially baking soda plus one or two acidic salts plus a starch buffer. The acid does the work of releasing CO₂ when the powder gets wet (and, in "double-acting" formulations, again when heated). Different brands use different acids, and that is where the health questions diverge.

**Aluminum-containing baking powders.** Many supermarket baking powders use **sodium aluminum sulfate (SAS)** or **sodium aluminum phosphate (SALP)** as the heat-activated acid. Aluminum-containing baking powders typically contain 0.5–3 percent aluminum by weight.[^19] A teaspoon (about 4 g) of such powder may therefore contain roughly 20–120 mg of aluminum compounds, of which only a fraction is elemental aluminum and only a fraction of that is absorbed.

**EFSA tolerable weekly intake.** In 2008, the European Food Safety Authority set a tolerable weekly intake (TWI) of **1 mg of aluminum per kilogram of body weight per week**.[^20] For a 70-kg adult, that is 70 mg per week. A 2019 German exposure assessment found that average dietary intake already accounts for about half of the TWI, and that infants on specialty formulas and adolescents can routinely exceed it.[^21] The Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives later adopted a slightly higher provisional TWI of 2 mg/kg/week.[^22]

**The aluminum–Alzheimer's question.** This is where mainstream consensus and ongoing scientific debate diverge. The Alzheimer's Association, the World Health Organization, EFSA, the U.S. FDA, and the Alzheimer's Drug Discovery Foundation all conclude that current evidence does not support aluminum as a proven cause of Alzheimer's disease.[^23][^24] At the same time, peer-reviewed research continues to explore biological mechanisms by which aluminum could plausibly contribute to neuroinflammation, amyloid aggregation, and tau pathology.[^25][^26] A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in *Ecotoxicology and Environmental Safety* synthesized 54 studies and found mixed results, with about half showing a positive association and the others showing none.[^27] Earlier meta-analyses had reported a roughly 71 percent increased odds of Alzheimer's with chronic aluminum exposure, though heterogeneity is high.[^28]

The most accurate summary: aluminum is **not** an established cause of Alzheimer's, but it is also not unambiguously safe at high cumulative exposures, and many regulators recommend prudent reduction of avoidable dietary sources.

> **Key Facts: Aluminum at a Glance**
> - EFSA tolerable weekly intake: **1 mg of aluminum per kg of body weight**.[^20]
> - The average European adult already uses about **50 percent of that allowance** from food alone—before adding aluminum-containing baking powder, processed cheese, or antacids.[^21]
> - Aluminum-free baking powders (using monocalcium phosphate and/or cream of tartar instead of SAS) are widely available and bake equally well.

**Phosphate compounds.** Many baking powders—including aluminum-free ones—rely on phosphate-based acids (monocalcium phosphate, sodium acid pyrophosphate). Inorganic phosphate additives are absorbed nearly 100 percent, compared to roughly 40–60 percent for naturally occurring phosphate in whole foods. Large epidemiological and mechanistic studies link high intake of inorganic phosphate additives to increased cardiovascular risk, vascular calcification, and faster progression of chronic kidney disease, even in people with normal kidney function.[^29][^12][^30] For most home bakers, baking powder is a small part of total phosphate exposure—the larger contributors are colas, processed meats, and fast foods—but in a kidney-restricted diet every source counts.

**Cream of tartar (potassium bitartrate).** Cream of tartar, the acid used in many traditional and aluminum-free baking powders, is generally considered safe in normal culinary amounts. However, large doses (e.g., a tablespoon or more taken as a "natural" remedy) deliver significant potassium loads that can be hazardous in people with impaired kidney function.[^31]

## Section 6: When to Use Caution

Some readers can comfortably enjoy occasional biscuits or pancakes with little concern. Others should be more deliberate. The following groups have the strongest reasons to limit—or avoid—frequent use of sodium bicarbonate and conventional baking powders:

- **High blood pressure, or a family history of it.** Each teaspoon of baking soda contributes ~1,260 mg of sodium; conventional baking powder, ~400–500 mg per teaspoon. Sodium-sensitive individuals can see meaningful blood pressure responses.[^5]
- **Chronic kidney disease.** Reduced renal function impairs the excretion of bicarbonate, sodium, potassium, aluminum, and phosphate. Multiple case reports document serious alkalosis even from chronic low-dose use, including from baking-soda-containing toothpaste in people with kidney impairment.[^32]
- **Heart failure or fluid-retention states.** The sodium load drives volume overload.
- **DASH or sodium-restricted diets.** Both ingredients contribute meaningful sodium per serving.
- **Osteoporosis or low bone density.** Sodium-induced calciuria can compound bone loss, especially when calcium intake is low.[^18]
- **Pregnant and lactating women, infants, and young children.** Developing kidneys clear aluminum and sodium less efficiently, and infants on specialty formulas can already approach or exceed the EFSA TWI for aluminum.[^21]
- **Older adults.** The FDA's safe upper limit for sodium bicarbonate drops from 200 mEq/day in young adults to 100 mEq/day after age 60, reflecting age-related declines in renal clearance.
- **People with eating disorders or who use baking soda for self-treatment.** Gastric rupture cases cluster heavily in this group.[^6]

If you take prescription medications (especially those for blood pressure, kidney disease, heart failure, lithium, or certain antibiotics), discuss baking-soda use—particularly as an antacid or "home remedy"—with your physician or pharmacist before adopting it.

## Section 7: Wisdom from an Earlier Era — Historical Health Counsel Examined in Light of Modern Science

Long before nutrition was a recognized scientific discipline, health writer Ellen G. White spoke directly about leavening agents in her counsels to readers in the late 1800s. Her writings, preserved in *Counsels on Diet and Foods*, *The Ministry of Healing*, *Healthful Living*, and the *Testimonies for the Church*, repeatedly criticized **saleratus**—the 19th-century term for sodium and potassium bicarbonate leaveners—and the early baking powders of her day.

Among her best-known statements:

> "The use of soda or baking powder in bread making is harmful and unnecessary. Soda causes inflammation of the stomach, and often poisons the entire system." (*The Ministry of Healing*, p. 300)[^33]

> "Saleratus in any form should not be introduced into the stomach; for the effect is fearful. It eats the coatings of the stomach, causes inflammation, and frequently poisons the entire system." (*Testimonies for the Church*, vol. 2, p. 537)[^34]

> "Hot biscuit raised with soda or baking powder should never appear upon our tables. Such compounds are unfit to enter the stomach." (*The Review and Herald*, May 8, 1883)

She also offered constructive alternatives—graham gems leavened with steam and beaten batter, well-fermented bread, and careful baking technique.

**Historical context matters.** The "saleratus" of 1870–1900 was not always pure sodium bicarbonate. It was often impure potassium bicarbonate, sometimes contaminated with carbonate ash, lye residues, or copper. The first commercial baking powders frequently used **alum (aluminum potassium sulfate or aluminum sulfate)** as the acid, were sometimes adulterated with chalk and gypsum, and were the subject of major public health controversies and legal battles in that era (the "Alum Wars"). White's counsel must be read in that context: she was responding to a class of products quite different from today's purified, regulated, and often aluminum-free baking powder.

**Where modern science aligns with her counsel.**

- *Sodium load and cardiovascular risk*: large-dose or chronic use of baking soda clearly raises sodium burden and blood pressure risk in vulnerable people.[^5]
- *Digestive disturbance and inflammation*: sodium bicarbonate disrupts gastric pH, impairs pepsin activity, and—at higher doses—can cause dramatic gastric distension and rupture.[^6]
- *Vitamin destruction*: alkaline leavening genuinely degrades thiamine, vitamin C, and folate—an effect modern food chemistry has confirmed.[^14]
- *Aluminum concerns*: 19th-century alum baking powders delivered substantial aluminum loads, a concern modern regulators continue to monitor.[^20]
- *Yeast and slow-fermented bread*: White's preference for properly raised, well-baked bread anticipates modern findings that long-fermented sourdough increases B-vitamin content and improves mineral bioavailability.[^16]

**Where modern context differs.**

- Today's purified sodium bicarbonate, used occasionally and sparingly, does not "poison the entire system" the way contaminated saleratus might have.
- Modern aluminum-free baking powder is a substantially different product than the 1880s alum powders, although phosphate and sodium content remain real considerations.
- Hot bread is no longer considered intrinsically dangerous in the way 19th-century writers feared.

For readers who value Ellen White's writings, the central principles—simplicity, careful preparation, and avoidance of irritating chemical leaveners when better methods are available—hold up remarkably well under modern nutritional scrutiny. For secular readers, her counsel reads as an early voice for whole-grain, naturally fermented breads and lower additive loads—positions that align with current dietary guidance from many public health bodies.

## Section 8: What You Can Do — Practical Steps

You do not need to fear your kitchen. You can simply make informed choices.

**1. Reach for yeast-based and naturally leavened breads when possible.**
Active dry yeast, instant yeast, and traditional sourdough cultures use living organisms to leaven dough, with no sodium load and no aluminum. Long fermentation also reduces phytic acid, increases mineral bioavailability, and (in many studies) raises B-vitamin content.[^16]

**2. Use mechanical and steam leavening for variety.**
- Whipped egg whites or aquafaba lift cakes, soufflés, and pancakes beautifully.
- Beaten batters incorporate air physically.
- Steam-leavened popovers, Yorkshire puddings, and cream puffs need no chemical leavener.
- Flatbreads—tortillas, chapatis, lavash, pita—rely on griddle heat and minimal or no leavening.

**3. If you use baking powder, choose aluminum-free.**
Read labels. Avoid "sodium aluminum sulfate" and "sodium aluminum phosphate." Choose products whose acidic components are listed as monocalcium phosphate, cream of tartar (potassium bitartrate), or sodium acid pyrophosphate.

**4. Use the smallest effective amount.**
Most home recipes use more leavener than necessary. Start by reducing baking soda or powder by 25 percent in trusted recipes; you will rarely notice a difference in rise but will reduce sodium and additive load.

**5. Watch for hidden sources.**
Self-rising flour, pancake and biscuit mixes, commercial muffins, processed cheese, frozen waffles, doughnuts, packaged cookies, and many baked desserts contain baking soda, baking powder, or aluminum-based anti-caking agents. Read ingredient lists; words like "leavening," "sodium aluminum phosphate," or "sodium aluminum silicate" are clues.

**6. Don't use baking soda as a daily antacid.**
Occasional culinary amounts are one thing; routine spoonfuls dissolved in water for heartburn are another. If you have frequent reflux, talk with a clinician about safer, more effective options.

**7. Never use baking soda for "detox," drug-test cleansing, gout self-treatment, or to relieve discomfort after a heavy meal.**
These are precisely the scenarios linked to gastric rupture, severe alkalosis, and death in the medical literature.[^6][^2]

**8. Read the bigger picture.**
Most cumulative sodium and phosphate exposure does not come from baking powder—it comes from processed and fast foods. Reducing those will move the dial on cardiovascular and bone health far more than tinkering with your scone recipe.

---

## A Brief Reflection

For those who see the body as a gift to be cared for—whether from a faith perspective of stewardship or simply from a sense of personal responsibility—small daily choices about ingredients are part of a larger pattern of attention. None of us makes flawless decisions every meal. But learning what is in our food, why it matters, and what alternatives exist transforms cooking from a routine into an act of intention.

Baking soda and baking powder are not poisons in modest culinary amounts. They are also not neutral. Knowing the difference is the point.
