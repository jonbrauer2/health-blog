The single most important thing to know about gluten is this: if you suspect celiac disease, get tested **before** you stop eating gluten. Celiac disease is a serious, common, and badly underdiagnosed autoimmune condition, and going gluten-free first can hide it from the very tests designed to find it.

## TL;DR

- **Three different conditions hide under one word.** Celiac disease is autoimmune and testable, and it needs strict lifelong gluten avoidance. Wheat allergy is an IgE allergy that can cause anaphylaxis. Non-celiac gluten sensitivity (NCGS) is a real symptom pattern with no biomarker and a still-developing science.
- **Test first, then change the diet — every time.** Celiac blood tests and biopsies only work while a person is still eating gluten. Most people with celiac disease don't know they have it, so this rule protects people.
- **For many people who feel better off wheat, gluten may not be the main trigger.** In blinded trials, fructans (a FODMAP carbohydrate) and other wheat parts often explain symptoms better than the gluten protein itself. That doesn't make the relief any less real. It changes what the most helpful next step looks like.

## 1. Two People, One Sentence, Different Bodies

Picture two people at a church potluck, both politely passing on the dinner rolls. Both say the same thing: "I'm gluten sensitive."

The first person has an immune system that, when it meets gluten, attacks the lining of her own small intestine. A crumb from a shared bread knife can restart that damage. The second person gets bloated and foggy after a big pasta dinner, feels much better since cutting back on wheat, and has never been tested for anything.

Same sentence. Very different biology. And very different stakes.

That gap matters because gluten-free eating has gone mainstream much faster than our understanding of who actually needs it. In the United States, the share of people avoiding gluten *without* a celiac diagnosis more than tripled in just five years, from 0.5% in 2009–2010 to 1.7% in 2013–2014.[^2]\[1\] Meanwhile, a large national study found that of 35 people whose blood showed celiac disease, 29 had no idea they had it.[^1]\[2\]\[3\]\[4\] Worldwide, a 2018 meta-analysis of 96 studies found that about 1.4% of people test positive on celiac blood tests, and about 0.7% have it confirmed by biopsy.[^3]

So we have two problems running at the same time. Many people who *need* a gluten-free diet haven't been diagnosed. Many people *on* a gluten-free diet were never evaluated. This guide is meant to help with both — with clarity, and with respect for everyone at the table.

> **🔑 Key Fact #1: Celiac disease hides in plain sight**
> In a nationally representative U.S. study of 7,798 people, 29 of the 35 found to have celiac disease — about **5 out of every 6** — didn't know they had it.[^1] In a Swedish study, the average person waited **9.7 years** from their first symptoms to diagnosis — and almost 6 years even after first raising it with a doctor.[^4]\[5\] Celiac disease isn't rare. It's rarely *found*.

## 2. Gluten, Wheat, and Three Very Different Conditions

### What gluten actually is

Gluten is not one thing. It's a family of storage proteins found in wheat, barley, and rye. The two main groups are **gliadins** and **glutenins**.[^5]\[6\]\[7\] They give bread its stretch and chew. Wheat also contains many *other* compounds, including starches, fibers, fructans, and defense proteins, that have nothing to do with gluten. That detail becomes very important later.

### Wheat allergy: an allergy, in the classic sense

Wheat allergy is an **IgE-mediated** allergy, the same immune pathway behind peanut or bee-sting allergies. Reactions usually come quickly, within minutes to a couple of hours, and can include hives, swelling, wheezing, vomiting, or **anaphylaxis**.[^6] In one group of wheat-allergic children, more than half had experienced anaphylaxis after eating wheat.[^6]\[8\] A rarer form, wheat-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis, happens only when wheat is eaten within a few hours of exercise. It is often linked to a protein called omega-5 gliadin.[^6]\[8\] Wheat allergy is diagnosed with allergy testing (skin-prick tests, specific IgE blood tests, and sometimes supervised food challenges). Many children outgrow it.[^6]\[8\] It's a real and sometimes dangerous condition. It's just a *different* one from celiac disease.

### Celiac disease: autoimmunity, not allergy

Celiac disease is an **autoimmune** condition. In people with certain genes (HLA-DQ2 and/or HLA-DQ8), gluten sets off an immune attack on the lining of the small intestine.[^7]\[9\]\[10\] It is not an allergy and not a "sensitivity." A strict, lifelong gluten-free diet is not a lifestyle preference for these people. It is their **medical treatment**, and right now it is the only one.[^7]\[11\]

### Non-celiac gluten sensitivity (NCGS): real symptoms, open questions

NCGS describes people who get gut symptoms (bloating, pain, changes in bowel habits) and/or whole-body symptoms (fatigue, headache, "foggy mind," joint pain) after eating gluten-containing foods, **after celiac disease and wheat allergy have been ruled out**.[^5][^8]\[7\]\[12\]\[13\] In 2015, an international expert group published the **Salerno criteria**. This is a standard method for confirming NCGS: a trial of a gluten-free diet, followed by a blinded, placebo-controlled gluten challenge.[^8] The same group was candid: there is **no biomarker** for NCGS, so the diagnosis rests entirely on symptoms under careful testing conditions.[^8]\[13\]

Here's the honest tension. *Why* NCGS happens is still being worked out. That uncertainty has been misused in two directions. Some people use it to wave off real suffering ("it's all in your head"). Others use it to make confident claims about mechanisms that haven't been shown. This guide does neither.

> **🔑 Key Fact #2: Same food, three different immune stories**
> - **Wheat allergy:** IgE antibodies → fast allergic reaction → can cause anaphylaxis.[^6]\[8\]
> - **Celiac disease:** HLA-DQ2/DQ8 genes + gluten → T-cell autoimmune attack → intestinal damage and tTG antibodies.[^7]\[9\]\[10\]
> - **NCGS:** No autoimmunity, no IgE, no intestinal damage like celiac, and no current biomarker. It is defined by symptoms that improve off gluten and return with it.[^5][^8]\[7\]\[13\]
> One grain, three pathways. That's why "I'm gluten sensitive" can mean so many different things.

## 3. Celiac Disease: The Autoimmune Reality

### How the damage happens

Gluten proteins are unusually hard for our digestive enzymes to break down completely, so fairly large fragments (peptides) reach the small intestine. In celiac disease, an enzyme called **tissue transglutaminase (tTG)** modifies these fragments. In people with HLA-DQ2 or DQ8, the modified fragments fit tightly into immune "display" molecules and switch on gluten-reactive T cells.[^7]\[7\] The result is inflammation that flattens the tiny finger-like **villi** that line the gut and absorb nutrients (called villous atrophy). The body also makes antibodies against tTG, which is exactly what the main blood test looks for.[^7]\[9\]

Flattened villi mean less surface for absorbing iron, folate, calcium, vitamin D, and other nutrients. That's why so many signs of celiac disease show up far from the gut.

### Genes are necessary, but not enough

About 30% of people of European ancestry carry HLA-DQ2 alone, and adding DQ8 pushes the share of carriers higher still.[^5]\[7\] Yet only about 1% of people develop celiac disease.[^3]\[14\] So these genes are **necessary but not sufficient**: nearly everyone with celiac disease has them, but most carriers never get the disease.\[15\] What tips someone over — infections, the gut microbiome, timing of gluten introduction, or other factors — is still debated.[^7] Diagnoses are also rising over time, and not just because of better testing: a 2020 meta-analysis estimated that celiac incidence has been increasing by about 7.5% per year over the past several decades.[^12]

### It rarely looks like the textbook

The "classic" picture is diarrhea, weight loss, and a swollen belly. Today, many adults are diagnosed through other routes instead.[^9] These include:

- Iron-deficiency anemia that doesn't respond well to iron pills
- Osteoporosis or early bone loss
- Infertility or pregnancy complications
- Mildly elevated liver enzymes
- Tingling, numbness, or balance problems
- Fatigue, mouth ulcers, or tooth enamel defects
- An intensely itchy, blistering skin rash (see below)[^7][^9]

The ACG guideline describes celiac disease as resembling "a multisystemic disorder rather than an isolated intestinal disease."[^7]\[9\]

### Who's at higher risk

Some groups warrant a lower threshold for testing: people with type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, Down syndrome, Turner syndrome, or IgA deficiency, and especially **first-degree relatives** of someone with celiac disease.[^7]\[7\] A meta-analysis of 54 studies covering 10,252 first-degree relatives found that **7.5%** had celiac disease.[^10] That is roughly 7 to 10 times the general-population rate. Sisters and daughters were at highest risk, at about 1 in 7 and 1 in 8, compared with about 1 in 33 for fathers.[^10]

### When celiac disease shows up on the skin

**Dermatitis herpetiformis (DH)** is celiac disease showing up in the skin. It causes an intensely itchy, blistering rash, usually symmetrical, on the elbows, knees, and buttocks.[^11]\[7\] Obvious gut symptoms are rare in DH, yet three-fourths of patients have villous atrophy in the small bowel, and the rest have celiac-type inflammatory changes.[^11] It is diagnosed with a skin biopsy and treated with a lifelong gluten-free diet (sometimes with medication at first).[^11]\[7\]

> **🔑 Key Fact #3: Most celiac disease doesn't "look like" celiac disease**
> Many adults diagnosed today come to the doctor for anemia, bone loss, fatigue, fertility problems, or a skin rash, not diarrhea.[^9] If you only think of celiac disease when someone has classic digestive symptoms, you'll miss many of the people who have it.

## 4. Getting Diagnosed the Right Way — and Why Order Matters

### The standard testing path

1. **Blood test first.** The recommended first test is **tTG-IgA** plus a **total IgA** level. The total IgA check matters because some people naturally make little IgA, which can make the tTG-IgA test falsely negative. If IgA is low, IgG-based tests are used instead.[^7][^13]\[16\]
2. **Confirm with endoscopy and biopsy.** For adults, the ACG recommends upper endoscopy with multiple small-intestine biopsies to confirm the diagnosis.[^7]\[17\] European pediatric guidelines allow a "no-biopsy" diagnosis in some children with very high tTG-IgA (at least 10 times the upper limit of normal) and a positive confirmatory antibody test on a second blood sample.[^13]\[16\] That decision belongs to the specialist.
3. **Genetic testing is best at ruling celiac disease *out*.** A negative HLA-DQ2/DQ8 result makes celiac disease very unlikely. A positive result tells you very little, because a large share of the population carries these genes.[^7][^15]\[7\]\[18\]

> ### ⚠️ THE CRITICAL CAUTION: Test Before You Eliminate
> **Celiac testing only works while you are still eating gluten.** Once gluten leaves the diet, the gut starts to heal and antibody levels fall. Blood tests and biopsies can then come back **falsely normal**, even in someone who truly has celiac disease.[^7][^13]\[19\]
>
> Getting diagnosed after going gluten-free usually means a **gluten challenge**: eating gluten again, under medical guidance, for weeks.\[19\] In one study of 20 adults with known celiac disease, about 3 grams of gluten a day (roughly one to two slices of bread) for two weeks caused measurable intestinal changes in most, while blood antibodies rose only slightly by day 14 and much more by day 28.[^14] For someone who felt awful on gluten, that's a hard road.
>
> **This is not a soft suggestion. If celiac disease is on the table, test first.**

### What about NCGS?

NCGS is a **diagnosis of exclusion**. Celiac disease and wheat allergy have to be ruled out first, while the person is still eating gluten.[^8]\[7\] After that, the most reliable approach is a structured elimination followed by reintroduction, ideally with a clinician or dietitian. The research-grade version is the blinded Salerno protocol.[^8]\[13\] Without that order, a person may end up on a restrictive diet for life without ever knowing whether they have an autoimmune disease.

> **🔑 Key Fact #4: Going gluten-free first can erase the evidence**
> A negative celiac test in someone who has already cut out gluten **does not rule out celiac disease**.[^7][^13]\[19\] The fix, a supervised gluten challenge lasting weeks,[^14] is uncomfortable and often put off indefinitely. One simple rule prevents the whole problem: **test while still eating gluten.**

## 5. Non-Celiac Gluten Sensitivity: What the Research Actually Shows

### The original picture

Doctors kept seeing patients who were clearly unwell on wheat, clearly better off it, and clearly *not* celiac. Their symptoms were real and often disabling: bloating, abdominal pain, diarrhea or constipation, plus fatigue, headaches, joint aches, and trouble concentrating.[^5][^8]\[7\] Some blinded studies do find a subset of patients who reliably react to wheat and not to placebo.[^23] NCGS earned a place in the medical vocabulary for good reason.

### Then the careful trials got more complicated

Researchers ran **double-blind, placebo-controlled** studies, where neither the participant nor the researcher knows what's in the food. That's the best way to separate what a food does from what we *expect* it to do.

- **Biesiekierski et al., 2013:** 37 people with self-reported gluten sensitivity and IBS first went on a low-FODMAP diet, and symptoms improved for nearly everyone. When gluten was then added back in blinded fashion, gluten-specific effects appeared in only **8%** of participants.[^16]\[20\]\[21\]
- **Skodje et al., 2018:** 59 Norwegians on self-chosen gluten-free diets ate cereal bars with gluten, with **fructan** (a FODMAP carbohydrate found in wheat), or with neither, without knowing which. Fructan caused more gut symptoms than gluten did. Gluten caused no more symptoms than placebo.[^17]\[22\]\[23\]
- **Molina-Infante & Carroccio, 2017:** Across 10 blinded gluten-challenge trials, only **16%** of people with suspected NCGS showed gluten-specific symptoms. Of those, 40% also reacted to placebo as much or more.[^18]\[24\]

Researchers have also pointed out real design problems in these studies: gluten doses that vary a lot, and some "placebos" that themselves contain fermentable carbohydrates.[^19]\[25\] Self-reported gluten sensitivity (as in Skodje's study) is also not the same as specialist-confirmed NCGS, a point the Salerno group raised in response.[^8]\[22\] So the trials don't show that NCGS isn't real. They show that **gluten protein is often not the main culprit**.\[26\]

### Other wheat components under study

**FODMAPs** (fermentable carbohydrates such as fructans) are abundant in wheat, as well as in onions, garlic, and some fruits and legumes. They draw water into the gut and ferment, which can cause bloating and pain in sensitive people. They are completely unrelated to gluten protein.[^17][^22]\[27\]

**Amylase-trypsin inhibitors (ATIs)** are natural pest-defense proteins in wheat. Junker and colleagues showed in 2012 that ATIs activate an **innate** immune receptor (TLR4) on gut immune cells, in cells from both celiac and non-celiac people.[^20]\[6\] Mice without TLR4 signaling were protected from ATI-driven inflammation.[^20]\[28\]\[29\] Later mouse work showed dietary ATIs could worsen intestinal inflammation.[^21]\[30\]\[31\] This is a different pathway from the **adaptive**, T-cell, gluten-specific response of celiac disease.\[28\] It's promising, but most ATI evidence so far comes from cell and animal studies, not human trials.

Experts now often use the broader term **non-celiac wheat sensitivity** and describe several possible triggers — gluten, ATIs, and FODMAPs — that may play different roles in different people.[^22]\[31\]\[32\]

### Why self-reported sensitivity is so much higher than confirmed sensitivity

Several things likely contribute, often together:

- **Real FODMAP sensitivity**, which improves on a gluten-free diet because wheat is a major fructan source[^17]
- **Changes in overall diet quality**: going gluten-free often means fewer pastries, processed snacks, and fast food
- **The nocebo effect**: expecting a food to hurt can create real, physical symptoms[^18]\[25\]\[33\]
- **Attention and expectation** that come with any new health change
- **Genuine gluten- or wheat-specific reactions** in a smaller group[^18][^23]

None of these means people are "imagining" things. Nocebo symptoms are real symptoms. FODMAP pain is real pain.

> **🔑 Key Fact #5: "Gluten-free" may be doing more than its name suggests**
> When you cut out wheat, you also cut out a major source of **fructans** (FODMAPs) and **ATIs**, not just gluten. In a blinded trial, fructan, not gluten, triggered symptoms in people who believed they were gluten sensitive.[^17]\[22\] For many people, the relief is real, but the trigger may be something else in wheat. That's good news: a dietitian-guided low-FODMAP trial may allow a *less* restrictive, more flexible diet than lifelong strict gluten avoidance.

## 6. Living With Celiac Disease: The Gluten-Free Diet as Medical Treatment

For people with celiac disease, gluten avoidance is strict and lifelong. There is **no "cheat day."** Even small, repeated exposures can keep the immune system active and the gut inflamed, with or without noticeable symptoms.[^7]\[7\]

### Cross-contact happens in ordinary places

- Shared toasters, cutting boards, colanders, and wooden spoons
- Deep fryers where breaded foods are also cooked
- Bulk bins, where scoops travel between flours and grains
- **Church potlucks and fellowship meals**: the serving spoon that moved from the pasta salad to the rice dish, the cookie platter touching the fruit tray, crumbs on a shared table
- **Communion bread.** For a person with celiac disease, standard communion bread or wafers contain gluten. Many congregations now offer a separate gluten-free option, kept in its own sealed container and served first or by a separate server. Traditions differ in what they allow, so a quiet, private conversation with church leaders ahead of time usually works best for everyone.

### Hidden sources

Soy sauce (usually brewed with wheat), malt (barley), malt vinegar, some broths and seasoning blends, imitation seafood, beer, some medications and supplements (ask a pharmacist about inactive ingredients), and regular oats, which are often cross-contaminated with wheat.[^7]

### Follow-up is part of treatment

The ACG guideline recommends ongoing follow-up: repeat antibody testing to monitor dietary adherence and healing, checking and correcting nutrient deficiencies, and assessing bone health given the higher risk of osteoporosis.[^7]\[34\]\[35\] It also suggests considering a follow-up biopsy after about two years to check whether the intestine has healed.[^7]\[36\]

### When the diet "isn't working"

Most people improve well on a gluten-free diet. A minority keep having symptoms, which is called **non-responsive celiac disease**. The most common cause is hidden gluten exposure, but other conditions (such as IBS, lactose intolerance, bacterial overgrowth, or microscopic colitis) and, rarely, **refractory celiac disease** can be responsible.[^7]\[9\]\[37\] These people need a **specialist evaluation**, not just advice to "try harder."\[35\]

## 7. The Other Side: Risks of Going Gluten-Free Without a Reason

Undiagnosed celiac disease carries real risks. So does unnecessary restriction. Both deserve honesty.

### Nutritional gaps

In the U.S. and many other countries, refined wheat flour is **enriched** by law with iron and B vitamins: thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, and folic acid. That makes everyday bread, pasta, and cereal a major source of these nutrients. Many packaged gluten-free breads, pastas, and baked goods are made from refined rice, potato, corn, or tapioca starch and are **not fortified to the same standard**.[^24]\[38\] A review of gluten-free diets found them frequently low in fiber, vitamin D, vitamin B12, folate, iron, zinc, magnesium, and calcium.[^24]\[39\] (Vitamin B12 isn't part of standard flour enrichment, but gluten-free diets can still come up short on it.)

### Fiber

Whole wheat, barley, and rye are some of the most common fiber sources in Western diets. Gluten-free substitutes built on refined starches are often much lower in fiber, which matters for digestion, cholesterol, blood sugar, and gut bacteria.[^24]\[39\]

### Cost and access

Gluten-free specialty products cost significantly more. A U.S. study found them **183% more expensive** on average than their wheat-based versions, down from 240% a decade earlier.[^25]\[40\] An earlier Canadian study found a 242% premium.[^26]\[41\] For a lower-income family, especially one feeding children, that's a real burden, and it is one reason people with diagnosed celiac disease sometimes struggle to stick with the diet.

### Social and psychological weight

Restriction affects more than nutrients. It can make family dinners, church fellowship meals, travel, and eating at friends' homes more stressful. It deserves a gentle flag here: for some people, a history of elimination diets can gradually turn into rigid, anxious, or shrinking patterns of eating.\[42\]\[43\] Research links celiac disease with a higher risk of anorexia nervosa, both before and after diagnosis.[^27]\[44\]\[45\] Studies of gastrointestinal conditions report disordered eating in anywhere from 5% to 44% of patients, depending on the group and how it was measured.[^28]\[46\] In adults with celiac disease, the most extremely vigilant eaters reported *lower* quality of life than those who were careful but less anxious.[^29]\[47\] Similar patterns appear in teenagers.[^39]\[42\] (Section 9 covers when to refer.)

### The honest bottom line

For most healthy adults, going gluten-free without a diagnosis isn't dangerous. But it isn't free, and it isn't automatically healthier. It works best as a choice made for a specific reason — ideally *after* celiac testing — not as a default upgrade.

> **🔑 Key Fact #6: "Gluten-free" can mean *fewer* vitamins, not more**
> Ordinary enriched wheat bread comes with added iron and B vitamins, including folic acid, which helps prevent certain birth defects. Many gluten-free breads and pastas don't.[^24]\[38\] Swapping regular bread for a packaged gluten-free loaf can quietly *lower* your intake of iron, folate, and fiber, the opposite of what many people expect.

## 8. Contested Territory: Leaky Gut, Autoimmunity, and "Modern Wheat" Claims

This is where confident claims tend to outrun the data. The goal here is to lay out what's measurable, what's plausible, and what's still unknown.

### Zonulin and intestinal permeability

The cells lining your gut are held together by "tight junctions," which work like adjustable seals. In the early 2000s, Dr. Alessio Fasano's group identified **zonulin**, a protein that loosens these seals and so increases intestinal permeability.[^30]\[7\]\[48\] Their research showed that gliadin (part of gluten) can trigger zonulin release from intestinal cells, and that this effect is stronger and longer-lasting in people with celiac disease.[^30][^31]\[7\]\[49\] More recently, a study following at-risk children found that zonulin levels rose *before* the onset of celiac autoimmunity, suggesting that increased permeability may be an early step in the disease rather than just a result of it.[^32]\[49\]

That's the established core. The broader popular claim — that "leaky gut" is a confirmed root cause of most autoimmune and inflammatory diseases — goes well beyond it. Fasano himself has proposed a model in which increased permeability, together with genetics and immune responses, contributes to many chronic inflammatory diseases.[^31]\[50\] That model is an **active research hypothesis**, not settled science. There's also a measurement problem: researchers, including Fasano, have noted that widely used commercial zonulin blood tests may not measure zonulin as intended.[^31]\[51\]\[52\] So a "zonulin test" from a wellness clinic may not mean what it appears to.

### Gluten and autoimmune conditions beyond celiac disease

People with celiac disease are more likely to have other autoimmune conditions, especially autoimmune thyroid disease and type 1 diabetes.[^7]\[53\]\[54\] That's well documented. Whether people *without* celiac disease benefit from going gluten-free for Hashimoto's thyroiditis, rheumatoid arthritis, and similar conditions is a different question. Many clinicians and patients report improvement, and there are plausible mechanisms. But the controlled evidence is thin. A 2025 systematic review of gluten-free diets in non-celiac Hashimoto's could pool only three small randomized trials (110 people total), and the authors described the results as inconsistent.[^33]\[55\] This is "limited evidence," not "no evidence," and it is not "studied and found ineffective." It is simply not the same level of evidence as the gluten-free diet for celiac disease.

For coaches, that means a client can make an informed choice to try elimination. A reasonable coach can support that trial, **after celiac testing**, with a defined time frame, attention to nutrition, symptom tracking, and coordination with the client's physician. The coach should also be honest that the evidence isn't as strong as it is for celiac disease.

### "Modern wheat is different"

The popular claim is that modern breeding made wheat more inflammatory or harder to digest. Here's what's measurable:

- A U.S. analysis of 20th-century wheat data found **no clear evidence that the gluten content of wheat increased**.[^34]\[56\] The same analysis did note that more *vital wheat gluten* is being added to processed foods, which is an easy-to-miss change in how much gluten people actually eat.[^34]
- A German study grew 60 wheat varieties registered between 1891 and 2010 side by side under the same conditions. Modern varieties had slightly **less** total protein and **fewer gliadins**, with more glutenins. Overall gluten content didn't rise.[^35]\[56\]\[57\]

What isn't yet measurable is whether subtle changes in specific protein fragments, food processing (such as fast-rise bread-making), or how much wheat people eat in total affect sensitive people. Those questions remain open. The "breeding made wheat toxic" version isn't supported by composition data. The broader "our wheat *diet* has changed" version is worth asking about.

### Gluten ataxia versus "gluten brain fog"

**Gluten ataxia** is a recognized, if uncommon, immune-mediated condition in which gluten sensitivity damages the cerebellum, the part of the brain that controls balance and coordination.[^36]\[58\] Many patients have few or no gut symptoms.[^36]\[7\]\[59\]\[60\] In a UK series of 117 patients, brain imaging markers in the cerebellum improved in 62 of 63 patients (98%) who followed a strict gluten-free diet.[^37]\[61\] How common it is remains debated, partly because the antigliadin antibodies used to identify it are not very specific.[^38]\[59\] It is diagnosed by neurologists with specific testing.\[7\]

That is very different from the common, loosely used idea that gluten causes "brain fog" in most people. Brain fog is a real, frequently reported symptom in NCGS and untreated celiac disease.[^5][^8]\[7\] But in the general population, its connection to gluten specifically is still unclear.

> **🔑 Key Fact #7: Zonulin is real science — "leaky gut causes everything" is not (yet)**
> Gluten can increase zonulin release and gut permeability, especially in celiac disease, and rising zonulin may even come before celiac autoimmunity in at-risk children.[^30][^32]\[7\] That's a real, important mechanism. The leap to "leaky gut causes most chronic disease" is an unproven *hypothesis* under active study, and common zonulin blood tests may not measure what they claim.[^31] Plausible mechanism, real research, often stretched too far in popular use.

## 9. Symptom Recognition and When to See a Clinician

### What a coach can appropriately do

| Coaches can… | Coaches should refer for… |
|---|---|
| Explain the difference between celiac disease, wheat allergy, and NCGS | Diagnosing or ruling out any of these conditions |
| Encourage celiac testing *before* any diet change | Ordering or interpreting lab tests or biopsies |
| Support whole-food, balanced gluten-free eating after diagnosis | Planning or supervising a gluten challenge |
| Help with label reading, meal planning, and potluck strategies | Low-FODMAP diet design (best done with a dietitian) |
| Support an informed, time-limited elimination trial *after* testing | Managing symptoms that persist or worsen |
| Notice and gently name worrying eating patterns | Treatment for suspected disordered eating |

### Referral triggers — send to a clinician when someone has:

- **Unexplained weight loss, persistent diarrhea, or any GI bleeding** (including black or bloody stools)
- **Unexplained iron-deficiency anemia**, especially in men, older adults, or women without heavy periods[^7]
- **A first-degree relative with celiac disease**: testing is reasonable even without symptoms[^7][^10]\[62\]
- **Already gone gluten-free but wants a real answer**: they may need a supervised gluten challenge before testing[^14]
- **An itchy, blistering rash** on elbows, knees, or buttocks that could be dermatitis herpetiformis[^11]\[7\]
- **Balance, coordination, or numbness/tingling problems**, especially alongside GI symptoms[^36][^38]
- **Type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, or Down syndrome** plus GI or nutritional concerns[^7]\[7\]
- **Signs that restriction is becoming rigid or fearful**: an expanding list of "unsafe" foods, anxiety or guilt around eating, skipping social meals, weight loss, or a growing focus on body shape. This calls for a gentle referral to a physician and, when appropriate, an eating-disorder-informed professional, not just more nutrition coaching.[^27][^29]

### Special populations

- **Children** with poor growth, delayed puberty, or failure to thrive
- **Pregnant women** with unexplained anemia or GI symptoms
- **Older adults** with unexplained osteoporosis or fractures, who also tend to wait longer for a diagnosis[^4]\[63\]

## 10. Living Well on a Gluten-Free Diet — Practical Guidance

For people who need to avoid gluten (celiac disease or confirmed sensitivity), the goal is simple: **build meals around foods that are naturally gluten-free**, rather than rebuilding a wheat-based diet out of packaged substitutes.

### Build on naturally gluten-free foods

- Vegetables and fruits of every color
- **Legumes**: beans, lentils, chickpeas, and peas, which are rich in fiber, folate, and iron\[38\]
- Whole grains and starches: brown rice, quinoa, millet, buckwheat, sorghum, teff, amaranth, corn, and potatoes\[64\]
- **Oats labeled gluten-free**: oats themselves are generally tolerated by most people with celiac disease, but regular oats are often contaminated with wheat[^7]
- Nuts, seeds, eggs, dairy or fortified alternatives, fish, and poultry, if part of your diet

### Read labels every time

Formulations change. A product that was safe last month may not be today. Look for "gluten-free" labeling (in the U.S., this means under 20 parts per million) and check for wheat, barley, rye, malt, and brewer's yeast.\[65\]

### Shared meals and fellowship settings

- **Bring a dish to share** that you know is safe, and serve yourself first before cross-contact can happen
- **Communicate kindly and directly**: "I have celiac disease, so even crumbs matter for me. Could I get a clean spoon for that?"
- **Keep logistics simple**: separate labeled serving utensils, a "gluten-free end" of the table, and gluten-free communion kept sealed and served separately
- For hosts: a simple label card with ingredients is a real gift to guests

### A hopeful note

The risks in Section 7 come mostly from *over-relying on processed gluten-free products*, not from the gluten-free diet itself.[^24] A gluten-free plate built from beans, vegetables, fruit, whole gluten-free grains, nuts, and seeds can be high in fiber, rich in folate and iron, and entirely satisfying.\[64\]

> **🔑 Key Fact #8: Whole-food gluten-free eating can be excellent eating**
> Lentils, black beans, quinoa, brown rice, sweet potatoes, leafy greens, and nuts are all naturally gluten-free and rich in the fiber, folate, and iron that packaged gluten-free products often lack.[^24] Done this way, a gluten-free diet isn't a nutritional compromise. It can be one of the healthiest ways to eat.

## 11. A Note on Shared Tables and Belonging

Food restrictions, for any reason, can feel isolating, especially in communities where shared meals carry real social and spiritual meaning. Research in celiac disease suggests that the goal is *confident* carefulness, not fearful carefulness: the most anxious, hypervigilant eaters often report lower quality of life.[^29]\[47\]\[66\] Communities can help. A labeled dish, a clean serving spoon, a sealed gluten-free communion option, and a host who asks without making a fuss all tell a person, "You belong here." Hospitality like this supports a person's health. It does not replace accurate diagnosis or medical care, and it doesn't need to. It simply makes the right choice easier to live with.
