## Why Low Blood Pressure Deserves More Attention

You stand up from the couch and the room slides sideways. Little gray sparkles crowd the edges of your vision. You grab the doorframe, wait a few seconds, and the world snaps back into focus. Or maybe it happens on a warm morning while you’re standing for a long stretch — your knees go soft, your ears start ringing, and the next thing you know, kind hands are helping you to the floor while someone fans your face. If either scene feels familiar, you already know something that medical textbooks are slow to say plainly: blood pressure that runs low is not always harmless, and living with it can be genuinely hard.[^1]

We talk about high blood pressure constantly, and for good reason — it’s a leading driver of strokes and heart attacks. But that focus has a shadow side. Low blood pressure, known medically as hypotension, gets treated like the lucky problem, the one you should be glad to have. Many people with chronically low readings are sent home with a pat on the shoulder and the words “you’re fine.” For some, that’s true. For others, it is not, and being told otherwise is exhausting.[^2]

So let’s define our terms. Hypotension is generally described as a reading below 90/60 mmHg, compared with a typical “normal” of around 120/80.[^3] But here is the part that matters most, and the part too often skipped: blood pressure is not one-size-fits-all. Some people feel terrible at readings that a chart calls acceptable, while others cruise through life at 88/55 and never notice. In clinical practice, low blood pressure is usually considered a problem only when it actually causes symptoms — which means your experience is part of the diagnosis, not a footnote to it.[^4]

That brings us to the gap this handout is built to address. The medical literature will tell you, accurately, that low blood pressure is “usually benign” in the general population.[^4] And yet there are real people whose lives are organized around dizziness, fog, and fatigue that flares every time they stand, eat, or get too warm. Both things are true at once. Low blood pressure is often nothing to worry about, and it is sometimes a serious, undertreated source of suffering. This guide takes the second group seriously while giving everyone the tools to understand what’s happening in their body.

## Not All Low Blood Pressure Is the Same

The phrase “low blood pressure” hides several very different conditions under one umbrella. Sorting out which one you’re dealing with is the single most useful step toward feeling better, because the management differs in important ways.

**Orthostatic (postural) hypotension** is the classic “stand up and nearly fall over” type. It’s defined as a sustained drop of at least 20 mmHg in the top number (systolic) or 10 mmHg in the bottom number (diastolic) within three minutes of standing.[^5] Normally, when you rise, gravity pulls blood toward your legs, and your nervous system instantly tightens those vessels and nudges your heart rate up to keep blood flowing to your brain. In orthostatic hypotension, that reflex is too slow or too weak, so your brain briefly runs short on blood and you feel it. This type becomes dramatically more common with age — prevalence is age-dependent, ranging from about 5% in people under 50 to roughly 30% in those over 70   — and it’s tightly linked to falls, hospital stays, and even higher mortality.[^6] Among elders in nursing homes and geriatric wards, prevalence runs as high as 50% and 68%, respectively.[ ^7] Despite how common it is, it’s frequently missed simply because no one checks blood pressure in both lying and standing positions.

**Neurally mediated hypotension and vasovagal syncope** are the fainting types, and they’re the most common cause of fainting overall.[^8] Here the problem isn’t a sluggish reflex but a kind of misfire: after prolonged standing, emotional stress, heat, pain, or the sight of blood, the nervous system suddenly does the opposite of what it should — blood vessels dilate and, often, the heart slows, causing a steep drop in blood pressure and a faint.[ ^9] Interestingly, careful tilt-table studies show that in the large majority of these episodes, the fall in blood pressure comes *first*, before the heart rate drops — which helps explain why pacemakers often don’t prevent these faints.[ ^10] People prone to this often get warning signs: nausea, sweating, tunnel vision, a feeling of warmth.

**Postprandial hypotension** is the after-eating drop, and it is both common and badly under-recognized. It’s defined as a fall of 20 mmHg or more within two hours of a meal.[^11] When you eat, blood pools in your digestive tract to handle the work of digestion; in younger people, the body compensates automatically, but in many older adults that compensation falls short.[ ^12] A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis pooling 13 studies found postprandial hypotension in 40.5% of older adults overall, rising to 49.3% in hospital geriatrics departments.[  ^13] It’s a real and overlooked cause of after-lunch dizziness, falls, and even fainting.

**Chronic low blood pressure without a clear cause** is the group most likely to be dismissed. These are often lean, fit, or athletic people — frequently younger women — whose blood pressure simply runs low all the time. The textbooks call this “constitutional” or essentially benign, and statistically that’s usually right.[^4] But “statistically benign” is cold comfort if you’re the one battling daily fatigue and brain fog. The honest position is this: a low number with no symptoms needs no treatment, but a low number *with* symptoms deserves the same curiosity and care as any other complaint.

**POTS — Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome** — deserves special attention because it’s so often confused with simple orthostatic hypotension and so often missed entirely. The key difference is the heart. In POTS, standing triggers a large, sustained jump in heart rate *without* the qualifying drop in blood pressure that defines orthostatic hypotension. According to the 2015 Heart Rhythm Society Expert Consensus Statement, POTS is defined by an increase in heart rate of at least 30 beats per minute (or at least 40 bpm in people aged 12 to 19) within 10 minutes of standing or head-up tilt, accompanied by symptoms and in the absence of orthostatic hypotension.[ ^14] People with POTS describe a heart that pounds and races the moment they stand, alongside dizziness, fatigue, and fog. Recognition has surged since COVID-19, because a substantial share of people with long COVID develop POTS-like dysautonomia.[^15]

**Secondary hypotension** is low blood pressure caused by something else — and finding that “something else” is the whole point. Causes include dehydration, blood loss, severe infection, heart conditions, hormone problems like adrenal insufficiency or an underactive thyroid, and, very commonly, medications.[^16] New or unexplained low blood pressure should always prompt a look for an underlying cause rather than a shrug.

> **KEY FACT — POTS: A Diagnosis Too Often Delayed**
> POTS overwhelmingly affects women of childbearing age — in one large long-COVID cohort, 91% of those diagnosed were women,  with an average age around 40.[^15] Historically, getting diagnosed took a long time: a December 2013 survey of more than 700 POTS patients by the advocacy and research organization Dysautonomia International found the average diagnostic delay was 5 years and 11 months, with only 25% of patients diagnosed within the first year of symptoms.[ ^17] Even more troubling, before diagnosis, 59% had been told their symptoms were “all in your head,” and 69% had been diagnosed with an anxiety disorder.[ ^17] The good news: driven partly by long COVID and intensive physician education, that delay appears to be shrinking, in some reports to under four years.[ ^18] In a 2025 Karolinska Institutet study published in *Circulation: Arrhythmia and Electrophysiology*, 143 of 467 highly symptomatic long-COVID patients (31%) were diagnosed with POTS — roughly one-third, compared with an estimated pre-pandemic population prevalence of just 0.2% to 1%.[ ^15]

## Symptoms — The Full Picture

If you think low blood pressure just means occasional dizziness, you’re seeing a sliver of it. The fuller picture is what makes this condition so easy to misread.

Yes, there’s lightheadedness and fainting. But the more disabling symptoms are often the quiet ones: a bone-deep fatigue, brain fog, trouble concentrating, and exercise intolerance that makes a flight of stairs feel like a mountain.[^19] People also report headaches, blurred or “graying” vision, nausea, heart palpitations, and a striking inability to stand still for any length of time — the grocery line, the church pew, the museum tour. Symptoms reliably worsen in heat and after meals.[^20] A systematic review of fatigue in orthostatic disorders found that fatigue is not only common but genuinely debilitating, and especially severe in POTS, where “brain fog” significantly harms productivity and cognition.[^21]

Here’s where it gets complicated. These symptoms overlap heavily with chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, depression, and anxiety. Because the symptoms are invisible and the standard quick exam often looks normal, people are frequently told the problem is psychological.[^17] This is a genuine tension, and it deserves an honest answer rather than a slogan. Anxiety is real and can absolutely cause physical symptoms. *And* peer-reviewed research shows that people with POTS are no more anxious than the general public — their racing heart is a physiological response to standing, not a panic attack.[^17] Both can be true; the task is to test carefully rather than to assume. Dismissing a measurable circulatory problem as “just anxiety” wastes years of people’s lives, and the data on diagnostic delay proves it.

The quality-of-life toll is substantial. The 2017 ACC/AHA/HRS syncope guideline notes that the quality of life associated with recurrent syncope is equivalent to that of severe rheumatoid arthritis and chronic low-back pain in an adult population.[ ^9] This is not a trivial complaint, and treating it as one is part of why so many people suffer so long.

## Who Gets Low Blood Pressure, and Why

Low blood pressure has many roads leading to it, and people often travel more than one at once.

**Genetics and constitution** play a real role. Some people are simply built with a lower baseline, and this clusters in families and in lean, athletic body types.[^4] Being fit can lower resting blood pressure as a healthy adaptation — which is wonderful, except when it tips into symptoms.

**Dehydration and low sodium intake** are among the most fixable causes, and they hide a surprise we’ll return to below: while the entire culture pushes us to cut salt for heart health, people with hypotension conditions often need *more* sodium and fluid, not less.[^22] Adequate blood volume depends on both.

**Autonomic nervous system dysregulation** is the emerging frontier. The autonomic system is the autopilot that manages blood pressure, heart rate, and blood-vessel tone without your conscious input. When it misfires — from diabetes, neurological disease, post-viral illness, or causes we don’t fully understand — blood pressure control suffers. This umbrella is called dysautonomia.[^23]

**Nutritional deficiencies** matter more than many realize. Vitamin B12 deficiency, in particular, can cause autonomic dysfunction and orthostatic hypotension; case studies and small trials show that replacing B12 can actually restore normal blood-pressure responses, which is why some experts argue that anyone with orthostatic hypotension should be screened for B12 deficiency.[^24] Iron deficiency and anemia reduce the blood’s oxygen-carrying capacity and worsen symptoms, and folate deficiency travels in similar company.[^25]

**Medications** are a leading and frequently overlooked cause. Blood-pressure drugs, diuretics (“water pills”), alpha-blockers, tricyclic antidepressants, nitrates, and certain Parkinson’s medications all can lower standing blood pressure.[^26] Medication-caused (iatrogenic) hypotension is common, especially in older adults — more on this in its own box, because it’s that important.

**Prolonged bed rest and deconditioning** create a vicious cycle: inactivity shrinks blood volume and weakens the heart and leg muscles that pump blood upward, which worsens symptoms, which discourages activity.[^27] This loop is central to POTS and to recovery from it.

**Eating patterns** influence symptoms too. Large meals, meals heavy in refined carbohydrate, irregular meal timing, and alcohol all can trigger or deepen post-meal blood pressure drops.[^12]

**Heat and hot environments** dilate blood vessels and pull fluid out through sweat, which is why hot showers, saunas, and summer afternoons are classic triggers.[^28]

**Underlying medical conditions** round out the list: adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, diabetes with autonomic neuropathy, heart failure, Parkinson’s disease, and various autoimmune conditions.[^16] One term in this space — “adrenal fatigue” — is genuinely contested, and we’ll handle it carefully in its own section, because real adrenal insufficiency is serious and diagnosable while “adrenal fatigue” is not a recognized diagnosis.

> **KEY FACT — Sodium and the Dietary Mismatch**
> For decades the public-health message has been simple: eat less salt. For people with high blood pressure, that’s well supported — in a randomized crossover trial of people with resistant hypertension, a low-sodium diet lowered office systolic blood pressure by 22.7 mmHg compared with a high-sodium diet.[^29] But that very same advice can backfire for someone with orthostatic hypotension or POTS. In a randomized crossover study of women with POTS, a high-sodium diet increased blood volume, lowered the surge of stress hormones on standing, and reduced the excessive jump in heart rate compared with a low-sodium diet.[^30] This is the mismatch wellness-minded readers rarely hear: the salt advice that helps your neighbor with hypertension may be making *you* feel worse. Standard clinical guidance for orthostatic disorders often recommends substantially higher sodium and fluid intake — but because this is the opposite of typical advice, it should be discussed with your provider, especially if you have any heart or kidney disease.[^22]

## Getting Diagnosed — What to Expect and What to Ask For

The frustrating truth is that many people with blood-pressure-related symptoms get worked up for everything *except* blood-pressure dysregulation. Knowing what a thorough evaluation looks like helps you advocate for yourself.

**Technique matters enormously.** A single seated reading at the doctor’s office tells you almost nothing about orthostatic problems. Blood pressure varies by time of day, arm position, recent meals, and posture. To catch orthostatic hypotension, you need readings in different positions.

**Orthostatic vitals** are the cornerstone test, and they are simpler than they sound: blood pressure and heart rate are measured after you’ve rested lying down for five minutes, then again at three minutes (commonly also at one minute) after you stand.[ ^31] Supine-to-standing measurement is more sensitive than sitting-to-standing.  The catch is that this short test is frequently skipped in routine visits, even though it’s the very thing that would reveal the problem.[^31] It is entirely reasonable to ask: “Can we check my orthostatic vitals — lying and standing?”

**Tilt-table testing** is the next step for suspected POTS or recurrent fainting. You’re strapped to a table that tilts you upright while your heart rate and blood pressure are monitored continuously, reproducing the orthostatic stress in a controlled setting.[^14] An “active stand test” in the office can accomplish much of the same.

**Relevant lab work** helps find secondary and reversible causes. Useful tests include sodium and potassium, morning cortisol and aldosterone (for adrenal function), a complete blood count to catch anemia, a thyroid panel, and B12 and folate levels.[^16][^24]

**When to ask for a specialist:** recurrent fainting, a racing heart on standing, or symptoms that don’t respond to first steps warrant referral to a cardiologist or electrophysiologist, ideally one experienced with dysautonomia, and sometimes a neurologist.[^18] Dysautonomia International maintains a physician directory for exactly this reason. If you’ve been treated for anxiety without improvement, that itself is useful diagnostic information worth raising.

## Lifestyle and Nutritional Approaches — The Core of Self-Management

This is where most of the day-to-day improvement happens, and it’s worth saying clearly: for many people, non-drug measures do more good than any pill. The goal is rarely to make your blood pressure “normal” on a chart — it’s to improve how you feel, how long you can stand, and what you can do.[^32] Let’s organize these by how strong the evidence is, without pretending the categories are walls.

### Well-Supported Approaches

**Fluids** come first. Adequate hydration directly expands blood volume. Guidance for orthostatic disorders commonly targets a generous daily fluid intake, and there’s a neat trick worth knowing: rapidly drinking 300 to 500 mL of water (about 16 ounces) raises systolic blood pressure by roughly 35 mmHg and can serve as a rescue measure — the pressor effect begins after 10 to 15 minutes, peaks around 25 to 40 minutes, and largely dissipates within 90 minutes.[ ^33] For postprandial hypotension specifically, drinking a large glass of water *before* a meal has been shown to blunt the after-meal drop.[^34]

**Sodium**, as discussed, is the counterintuitive ally for orthostatic disorders. Increasing dietary sodium expands plasma volume and improves orthostatic tolerance, and it’s a mainstay of POTS and orthostatic hypotension management.[^22][^30] The caution is real, though: this is not advice for people with high blood pressure, heart failure, or kidney disease, and it should be coordinated with your provider rather than self-prescribed in large amounts.

**Compression garments** have solid evidence behind them. Abdominal binders and waist-high compression stockings reduce the pooling of blood in the legs and belly when you stand. Systematic reviews rate compression of the legs and/or abdomen  among the better-supported non-drug strategies, and abdominal compression in particular targets the splanchnic (belly) pooling that drives many symptoms.[^35] One practical note: they should be put on before getting up and not worn while lying down.[^36]

**Physical countermaneuvers** are free, immediate, and genuinely effective. Crossing and tensing your legs, squatting, standing on tiptoes, clenching your buttocks, or making fists all squeeze blood back toward the heart and buy you time when symptoms strike. These have strong evidence across multiple patient groups.[^35] The flip side: straining against a closed throat (like during heavy lifting or hard bowel movements) does the opposite and should be avoided.[^36]

**Exercise and reconditioning** present the central paradox of these conditions: the activity that helps is often the activity that triggers symptoms. The solution is to start in positions that don’t provoke the problem. Structured programs — most famously the protocol developed by Dallas cardiologist Dr. Benjamin Levine, and its pediatric adaptation from the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia — begin with *recumbent* exercise like a recumbent bike, rowing machine, or swimming, where the legs aren’t fighting gravity.[^37] Over months, the program gradually adds upright work. Studies show this kind of training increases heart size, blood volume, and oxygen capacity in people with POTS, and improves quality of life.[^38] Crucially, participants are reassured that the discomfort early on is not harm — the symptoms tend to abate as fitness builds.[^39](Ibid.) (One important exception: where post-exertional malaise from ME/CFS overlaps with POTS, pushing through can be harmful, and pacing becomes essential.)[^37]

**Meal timing and composition** help most with postprandial symptoms. Smaller, more frequent meals; a lighter load of refined carbohydrate per meal; limiting alcohol; and resting after eating all reduce the post-meal drop.[^11][^40]

**Head-of-bed elevation** is one of the few measures that works overnight. Sleeping with the whole head of the bed raised — using bed risers or a wedge so the head sits roughly 6 to 9 inches higher than the feet (stacked pillows don’t count) — reduces nighttime fluid loss and improves morning orthostatic tolerance.[^41] The evidence is modest and somewhat mixed on degree of effect, and to work it generally requires tilting the bed rather than just propping yourself up.[^42]

**Avoiding triggers** ties it together: heat, prolonged standing, large meals, alcohol, and dehydration are the usual suspects, and small adjustments around each add up.[^28]

### Approaches with Emerging or Limited Evidence

**Electrolyte supplementation** (sodium, potassium, magnesium) follows from the fluid-and-salt logic and is widely used, though the rigorous trial evidence for specific products and doses is thinner than the popularity suggests.[^43] Some practitioners recommend electrolyte drinks alongside increased water; discuss the specifics with your provider, particularly the potassium and magnesium components if you have kidney concerns.

**B12 and iron** have a clear rationale where a deficiency is present. If labs confirm low B12 or iron, correcting them can directly improve symptoms; replacing B12 has restored normal blood-pressure responses in deficient older patients.[ ^24] Where levels are already normal, supplementing is unlikely to help.

**Licorice root** is the most powerful — and most hazardous — herbal option here, and it must not be confused with the deglycyrrhizinated form. (See the box below.)

**Adaptogenic herbs** such as Rhodiola rosea and Panax ginseng are explored for fatigue and circulation, and some functional practitioners use them, but honesty requires noting the limits. A systematic review and meta-analysis of ginseng found no significant overall effect on blood pressure, concluding it appears “vascular neutral.”[ ^44] Rhodiola, meanwhile, is reported to have a mild *lowering* effect on blood pressure in some sources, which could be counterproductive for someone with hypotension.[ ^45] These herbs may help fatigue for some people, but they are not reliable blood-pressure raisers, and the evidence is genuinely limited.

**Caffeine** has the best evidence among the everyday options, especially for postprandial hypotension. Multiple small randomized trials in older adults found that caffeine taken with or before a meal blunted the after-meal blood-pressure fall and prevented symptomatic drops in people prone to them.[^46] The effect is short-lived and tolerance can develop, so it’s used as a targeted tool (for example, morning coffee or coffee with a trigger meal) rather than a cure.[^47]

**Tyramine-rich foods** (aged cheeses, cured meats, fermented products) are sometimes recommended by functional practitioners on the theory that tyramine can nudge blood pressure up, but the evidence base for using them to treat hypotension is limited, and they interact dangerously with a class of antidepressants called MAO inhibitors. This one belongs in the “interesting but unproven” column.

> **KEY FACT — Licorice Root: Know Which Kind You’re Holding**
> There are two very different products sharing the name “licorice,” and confusing them can be dangerous. **Regular licorice root** contains glycyrrhizin, a compound that mimics the hormone aldosterone — it makes your body hold onto sodium and water and lose potassium, which raises blood pressure. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that glycyrrhizic-acid-containing products significantly increased both systolic and diastolic blood pressure.[^48] That BP-raising effect is precisely why some practitioners use it for low blood pressure — but it’s also why it can be genuinely risky: fatal heart events have been reported from heavy licorice candy consumption, and effects can appear at intakes as low as 100 mg of glycyrrhizin daily.[^49] **Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL)** has the glycyrrhizin removed; it’s used for heartburn and stomach soothing and has essentially no effect on blood pressure.[^50] So: DGL is for reflux and is safe for blood pressure; regular (glycyrrhizin-containing) licorice raises blood pressure and is the form discussed for hypotension. Anyone with high blood pressure, heart or kidney conditions, or who is pregnant should avoid the BP-raising form entirely, and even those considering it for low blood pressure should only do so with medical supervision.[^50]

## Medical Treatments — What Physicians May Offer

When lifestyle measures aren’t enough, several medications can help. None of these are do-it-yourself; they generally require a knowledgeable physician, and several involve real trade-offs.

**Fludrocortisone** is a synthetic steroid that expands blood volume by making the kidneys hold onto sodium and water.[^51] It’s included in treatment guidelines largely on expert opinion and long use, though its high-quality trial evidence is more limited than its popularity suggests.[^52] Side effects to watch for include low potassium, ankle swelling, supine high blood pressure, and, in frail elders, fluid overload.[^51]

**Midodrine** tightens blood vessels by stimulating receptors on their walls, raising standing blood pressure. It’s FDA-approved for orthostatic hypotension and carries some of the strongest evidence among these drugs.[ ^53] Because it can raise blood pressure while you’re lying down, it’s timed for daytime hours and avoided near bedtime. It’s also used in POTS.[^54]

**Droxidopa** is a precursor that the body converts into norepinephrine, the chemical messenger that tells vessels to constrict. It’s FDA-approved specifically for *neurogenic* orthostatic hypotension — the kind seen in Parkinson’s disease and related conditions — and systematic reviews give it and midodrine the strongest recommendation levels among the drug options.[^55]

**Low-dose beta-blockers in POTS** present an apparent paradox: why give a heart-slowing drug to someone whose problem is a racing heart? That’s exactly the point — in POTS, a low dose of propranolol can blunt the excessive standing heart rate and improve symptoms, though higher doses are poorly tolerated.[^56] Reviews find beta-blockers among the better-supported POTS treatments.[^57]

**Pyridostigmine** boosts the nervous system’s “rest and regulate” signaling and can lower the standing heart rate in POTS; intriguingly, it’s also the POTS medication with the most evidence for improving exercise capacity, which most others don’t address.[^58] **Ivabradine** slows the heart rate without affecting blood pressure — a useful property for someone whose blood pressure is already low — and around 60% of POTS patients improved on it in open-label study,  though it’s not available everywhere.[^57]

The honest summary on POTS drugs: there are no large, well-powered trials to crown a clear first-line treatment. The most promising agents are beta-blockers, midodrine, pyridostigmine, ivabradine, and increased salt and fluid, but the evidence rests on many small studies.[^57] This is a field where specialist judgment and individual trial-and-error matter.

> **KEY FACT — Medication-Induced Low Blood Pressure Is Surprisingly Common**
> One of the most overlooked causes of low blood pressure is the treatment of *high* blood pressure. Older adults, in particular, can be treated so aggressively that their pressure overshoots downward, especially when standing. A retrospective study found a 23% increased prevalence of orthostatic hypotension in older patients taking even one of several common culprit medications.[ ^59] Recognizing this, the 2023 European Society of Hypertension guidelines state that reducing blood-pressure treatment “can be considered in patients aged 80 years or older with a low systolic blood pressure (less than 120 mmHg) or in the presence of severe orthostatic hypotension or a high frailty level.”[ ^60] In the OPTIMISE randomized trial of 569 patients aged 80 or older, blood-pressure medication reduction could be sustained over 12 weeks in 66% of participants, with 86.4% in the reduction group keeping systolic blood pressure under 150 mmHg versus 87.7% in usual care  — meeting the trial’s threshold for noninferiority.[^61] The takeaway: if your low blood pressure is new, or if you take multiple blood-pressure medications and feel dizzy, ask your provider for a medication review. (One caveat: the long-term cardiovascular safety of stopping these drugs is still being studied, so this is a conversation to have with your doctor, not a solo decision.)[^61]

## Contested and Functional-Medicine Territory

Some of the most-searched topics in this space live in a gray zone. Pretending they’re either fully validated or pure nonsense doesn’t serve you. Here’s an honest map.

**Adrenal fatigue versus adrenal insufficiency.** These sound similar and are completely different. *Adrenal insufficiency* — including Addison’s disease and secondary forms — is a real, diagnosable, and potentially life-threatening condition in which the adrenal glands don’t make enough cortisol; it can cause low blood pressure, profound fatigue, salt cravings, and weight loss, and it’s diagnosed by an endocrinologist using standard hormone tests.[^62] *Adrenal fatigue*, by contrast, is the idea that chronic stress “exhausts” the adrenal glands into underperforming. A 2016 systematic review concluded there is “no substantiation that adrenal fatigue is an actual medical condition,” and no endocrinology society recognizes it.[^63] And yet — and this matters — the symptom cluster people describe under that label (fatigue, low blood pressure, salt cravings, morning sluggishness) is genuinely real and genuinely distressing. The constructive path is to take the symptoms seriously while testing properly: those exact symptoms can point to real conditions like adrenal insufficiency, B12 deficiency, thyroid disease, or a dysautonomia, all of which are diagnosable. For a health coach, the appropriate role is clear: validate the experience, and refer for proper testing rather than diagnosing “adrenal fatigue” or recommending unproven adrenal “support” protocols.

**Dysautonomia as an umbrella term.** Dysautonomia is a legitimate medical term for dysfunction of the autonomic nervous system, covering POTS, neurally mediated syncope, orthostatic hypotension, and more.[^23] It’s also increasingly used in functional and integrative circles, sometimes loosely. The distinction worth holding onto is between *self-diagnosed* dysautonomia and *clinically assessed* dysautonomia confirmed with objective testing like tilt-table or autonomic studies. The term being trendy doesn’t make it fake — but it does make careful evaluation more important, not less.

**Long COVID and POTS.** This is moving fast, and the appropriate posture is humility plus validation. It’s now well established that a significant share of people with long COVID — roughly 30% in several cohorts — develop autonomic symptoms or frank POTS.[^64] The proposed mechanisms include low blood volume, deconditioning from prolonged illness, lingering inflammation, and autoimmunity,  in which the body produces antibodies that interfere with autonomic receptors.[^65] None of these is fully proven, and treatments aren’t yet standardized.[^65] What’s not in doubt is that the experiences are real and measurable. The research is emerging rapidly; expect the picture to keep sharpening.

**Plant-based diets and hypotension.** Here’s a nuance wellness readers rarely encounter. The high-fiber, low-sodium, plant-rich eating patterns that are so effective at *preventing* high blood pressure can, in a constitutionally low person, nudge an already-low pressure lower — especially if sodium is restricted hard and meals are large and carbohydrate-heavy.[^29][^22] This isn’t an argument against plant-based eating, which has many benefits; it’s a reminder that the same dietary lever moves different people in different directions. If a “heart-healthy” diet overhaul leaves you dizzier than before, that’s worth discussing rather than ignoring.

## When to See a Doctor — Clear Referral Triggers

Self-management is powerful, but some situations call for a professional, sometimes urgently. Be direct with yourself about these.

A *first episode of fainting* always deserves evaluation — fainting can be benign, but it can also signal a heart-rhythm or structural problem that needs to be ruled out.[^9] Low blood pressure accompanied by *chest pain, shortness of breath, or confusion* is an emergency and should not wait. *Recurrent falls*, especially in older adults, warrant a workup that includes checking orthostatic vitals and reviewing medications.[^6] *Low blood pressure in pregnancy* should be discussed with your provider. *Suspected adrenal insufficiency* — the combination of salt cravings, profound fatigue, low blood pressure, and unexplained weight loss — needs prompt testing, because untreated it can become dangerous.[^62] *POTS symptoms* — a heart that races on standing along with dizziness and fatigue, particularly in a young woman — deserve referral rather than another anxiety prescription.[^17] And any low blood pressure that *worsens despite lifestyle measures*, or that is *new and unexplained*, should be investigated for a secondary cause.[^16]

> **KEY FACT — Emergency Care Versus Routine Evaluation**
> **Call emergency services or go to the ER now** if low blood pressure comes with chest pain, severe shortness of breath, confusion or trouble staying awake, a first-ever faint with injury, signs of internal bleeding (black or bloody stools, vomiting blood), or a high fever with a racing heart and clamminess that could signal shock — a life-threatening drop in blood flow to the organs.[^16] **Schedule a routine but prompt evaluation** for repeated dizziness on standing, fainting spells without alarming features, after-meal lightheadedness, a racing heart on standing, or symptoms that interfere with daily life. When in doubt about whether something is an emergency, err toward being seen — especially for chest pain, breathing trouble, or confusion.

## Practical Daily Living Tips

The difference between a hard day and a manageable one often comes down to small habits. Here are the ones that tend to help most, offered as gentle scaffolding rather than rules.

**Mornings are the riskiest stretch**, because you’re at your most dehydrated and your blood pressure tends to dip after a night lying flat. Build in a buffer: before you even sit up, drink a glass of water you’ve left on the nightstand. Then sit on the edge of the bed for a moment, let your body adjust, pump your ankles a few times, and only then stand. Rising in stages — lying, to sitting, to standing — gives your circulation time to catch up.[^36]

**Hydrate steadily through the day** rather than gulping it all at once, and keep electrolytes in the picture if your provider agrees. Many people find it helps to front-load fluids in the morning and early afternoon and ease off in the evening to avoid nighttime trips to the bathroom.[^33]

**Plan around meals** if afternoons knock you flat. Smaller, more frequent meals with less refined carbohydrate, a glass of water beforehand, and a little rest afterward can blunt the post-lunch slump. A cup of coffee with a trigger meal is a legitimate tool for some people.[^34][^46]

**Respect the heat.** Hot showers, saunas, hot tubs, and summer afternoons all dilate your vessels and can drop your pressure. A cooler shower, a shower chair for safety, and seeking shade and air conditioning on hot days are simple protections.[^28]

**Know your in-the-moment moves.** When symptoms hit, get low — lie down and elevate your legs if you can, or sit and put your head toward your knees. Cross and tense your legs, clench your muscles, and reach for salt and fluid.[^35][^36] Carrying a small water bottle and a salty snack turns a potential faint into a brief pause.

**Move in ways that help rather than harm.** Start with recumbent or water-based exercise, build slowly, and treat the early discomfort as part of the process rather than a stop sign — while staying alert to the difference between ordinary training fatigue and the post-exertional crash that signals you’ve done too much.[^37](Ibid.)

**Sleep with the head of the bed raised** if mornings are your worst time; tilt the whole bed rather than stacking pillows.[^41]

**Communicate clearly with your provider.** Keep a simple log of symptoms, readings if you have a home monitor, and triggers. Ask specifically for orthostatic vitals. Bring your full medication and supplement list. And if you feel dismissed, it is reasonable to ask, “Could there be an underlying autonomic issue we haven’t explored?”  or to request a referral to someone experienced with these conditions.[^18]

## A Closing Word on Being Believed

There’s a particular loneliness in carrying a problem no one can see. Your bloodwork comes back fine, you look healthy, and yet standing through a long line or a long service leaves you gray and shaking. Being told you’re “fine” when your body is clearly telling you otherwise is its own kind of wound — and the data on how long people wait to be properly diagnosed shows it’s a common one.[^17]

If you’re supporting someone in this situation, the most healing thing you can offer is simple belief. Pull a chair over before they have to ask. Keep water and a salty snack within reach. Understand that resting is not laziness and pacing is not weakness — they’re the tools that let a person with a low-pressure, easily-overwhelmed system stay in the room and stay in their life. Invisible conditions are real conditions, and a community that takes them seriously can change someone’s whole experience of being unwell. Rest, in this light, isn’t a retreat from a full life; it’s part of how a person with this kind of body builds one.
