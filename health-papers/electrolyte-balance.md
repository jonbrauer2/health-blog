## You May Already Know Something Is Off

You wake up with a headache that won’t quit. Your muscles cramp in the middle of the night. You’re tired even after a full night’s sleep. You reach for more water — because water fixes everything, right? — and somehow you still don’t feel right.

These are some of the most common complaints people bring to wellness coaches, nutritionists, and primary care physicians. And while the causes are many, one frequently overlooked contributor is an imbalance in the body’s electrolytes.

Electrolytes are minerals that carry an electrical charge when dissolved in water — and your body is mostly water. They regulate virtually every process that keeps you alive: your heartbeat, your muscle contractions, the fluid that cushions your cells, the chemical balance of your blood. When they’re in proper balance, you barely notice them. When they’re off — even slightly — the effects can ripple across your whole body.[^1]

Most people only hear about electrolytes in the context of sports drinks or heat exhaustion. But electrolyte imbalance is far more common than that, and it affects far more than athletes. Older adults, people on certain medications, those following restrictive diets, individuals under chronic stress, and anyone with conditions affecting digestion or kidney function are all at meaningful risk.[^2]

Understanding electrolytes — what they are, what disrupts them, and how to support them through food and lifestyle — is genuinely useful knowledge. This handout is designed to give you that understanding in practical, usable form.

-----

## What Are Electrolytes? (The Basics, Made Interesting)

Here’s the simplest way to think about electrolytes: your body is a giant electrochemical system. Every message your brain sends to your muscles, every heartbeat, every cell that moves water in or out — all of it runs on electrical impulses. Electrolytes are what make those impulses possible.

When an electrolyte mineral dissolves in water, it splits into positively or negatively charged particles called ions. These ions don’t just float around passively — they’re actively moved in and out of cells to generate voltage, trigger reactions, and regulate fluid flow. The concentration of electrolytes on either side of a cell membrane is so tightly controlled that even small disruptions can have noticeable — and sometimes serious — consequences.[^3]

### The Key Players

The seven major electrolytes, and their primary jobs:

**Sodium (Na⁺):** The most abundant electrolyte in the fluid outside your cells. Sodium governs fluid balance and blood pressure, and it’s essential for nerve signal transmission and muscle contraction. Think of sodium as the gatekeeper of your cell’s water.[^4]

**Potassium (K⁺):** Primarily found inside cells, potassium is sodium’s counterpart. The sodium-potassium pump — an active transport system in every cell — maintains the electrochemical gradient that allows nerve impulses to fire and muscles to contract. Potassium is especially critical for heart muscle function.[^5]

**Magnesium (Mg²⁺):** Involved in more than 300 enzymatic reactions in the body. Magnesium is required to activate ATP (your body’s energy currency), regulate nerve function, synthesize protein, and maintain normal heart rhythm. It also acts as a natural calcium channel blocker, helping muscles relax after contraction.[^6]

**Calcium (Ca²⁺):** Best known for bone structure, but calcium also plays essential roles in muscle contraction, nerve signaling, blood clotting, and cell signaling. It works in close coordination with magnesium: calcium initiates muscle contraction; magnesium enables relaxation.[^7]

**Chloride (Cl⁻):** Usually found paired with sodium, chloride helps maintain fluid balance, supports digestion as a component of hydrochloric acid in the stomach, and contributes to blood pH regulation.[^8]

**Phosphate (HPO₄²⁻):** Critical for energy metabolism (as part of ATP), bone and tooth structure, cell membrane integrity, and DNA synthesis.[^9]

**Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻):** The body’s primary buffer against acid-base imbalance. Bicarbonate keeps blood pH within the narrow range necessary for life — approximately 7.35 to 7.45.[^10]

### How the Body Regulates These Levels

Your body doesn’t leave electrolyte regulation to chance. The kidneys are the primary regulators, continuously filtering the blood and adjusting how much of each electrolyte is retained or excreted. Hormones like aldosterone (which signals the kidneys to retain sodium), parathyroid hormone (which regulates calcium and phosphate), and antidiuretic hormone (ADH, which controls water retention) work in concert to maintain balance.[^11]

The gut plays a role too — absorbing electrolytes from food and, when needed, recycling electrolytes back into digestive secretions. And the endocrine system — particularly the adrenal glands — responds to stress, dehydration, and blood pressure changes by adjusting hormonal signals that affect electrolyte handling.[^12]

The analogy that captures this well is a thermostat: your body has set points for the concentration of each electrolyte, and it’s constantly making micro-adjustments to stay near those targets. Exercise hard and sweat out sodium — the kidneys reduce sodium excretion. Eat a high-potassium meal — the kidneys increase potassium clearance. This system is remarkably efficient. But it can be overwhelmed.

> **📌 Key Fact:** Too much of an electrolyte can be just as dangerous as too little. Hypernatremia (excess sodium) and hyperkalemia (excess potassium) are both serious medical conditions. Balance — not just adequacy — is the goal.

## What Disrupts Electrolyte Balance?

Electrolyte imbalance has many causes — from the ordinary to the clinical. Some are within your direct control; others require medical attention. Understanding the full picture helps you identify which factors may be at play.

### Lifestyle and Dietary Causes

**Low-quality diets:** The standard Western diet creates a particular electrolyte pattern: high in sodium (from ultra-processed foods and restaurant meals), but deficient in potassium, magnesium, and calcium (which come primarily from whole plants and dairy). This imbalance has real consequences for blood pressure and cardiovascular function.[^13]

**Inadequate hydration — and overhydration:** Both extremes cause problems. Dehydration concentrates electrolytes in the blood, impairing cellular function. But overhydration — particularly drinking large amounts of plain water rapidly — can dilute blood sodium to dangerously low levels, a condition called hyponatremia. This is most commonly seen in endurance athletes who drink excessive plain water over long events, but it can occur in any setting involving rapid high-volume fluid intake.[^14]

**Vigorous exercise and sweating:** Sweat contains sodium, chloride, potassium, and small amounts of magnesium and calcium. Moderate exercise in temperate conditions typically doesn’t require electrolyte replacement beyond what food provides. But prolonged, intense exercise — especially in heat — can result in significant losses.[^15]

**Fasting, very low-calorie diets, and crash dieting:** Severe caloric restriction reduces both food-based electrolyte intake and shifts fluid balance, increasing urinary electrolyte losses. Refeeding syndrome — a potentially serious condition that can occur when someone who has been severely restricting intake begins eating again — involves dangerous drops in phosphate, magnesium, and potassium.[^16]

**Intermittent fasting and low-carbohydrate eating:** When carbohydrate intake drops significantly, insulin levels fall — and insulin normally signals the kidneys to retain sodium. Lower insulin means more sodium and potassium lost in the urine. This effect is well-documented in ketogenic and low-carbohydrate dietary patterns.[^17]

**Alcohol and caffeine:** Both have mild diuretic effects, increasing urinary fluid and electrolyte losses. Chronic heavy alcohol use is associated with significant magnesium and potassium depletion, which can contribute to muscle cramps, cardiac arrhythmias, and neurological symptoms.[^18]

**High-sugar diets:** Elevated blood sugar increases urinary glucose, which draws water and electrolytes (particularly potassium) into the urine — a process called osmotic diuresis. This is most dramatic in uncontrolled diabetes, but high glycemic load eating patterns may contribute to subtle electrolyte shifts over time.[^19]

### Medical and Pharmacological Causes

**Diuretics:** Loop diuretics (such as furosemide) and thiazide diuretics (commonly used for blood pressure) directly increase urinary excretion of sodium, potassium, and magnesium. They are among the most common pharmacological causes of electrolyte imbalance.[^20] Potassium-sparing diuretics carry the opposite risk: hyperkalemia.

**Laxative overuse:** Diarrhea from laxatives depletes potassium and magnesium rapidly. This is a particular concern in eating disorders where laxative use is frequent.[^21]

**Vomiting and diarrhea:** Both involve substantial electrolyte losses — particularly sodium, potassium, and chloride. Prolonged or severe gastrointestinal losses can lead to clinical hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis (in the case of vomiting) or metabolic acidosis (in the case of diarrhea).[^22]

**Kidney disease:** The kidneys are central to electrolyte regulation. As kidney function declines, the ability to excrete potassium and phosphate is impaired — a key reason why people with chronic kidney disease often need to limit these electrolytes in their diet. Paradoxically, kidney disease can also impair the ability to retain electrolytes that need conserving.[^23]

**Diabetes:** Both type 1 and type 2 diabetes affect electrolyte balance, particularly when blood sugar is poorly controlled. Osmotic diuresis, changes in insulin signaling, and common diabetic medications all have electrolyte implications.[^24]

**Eating disorders:** Purging behaviors — vomiting, laxative abuse, diuretic misuse — can produce severe hypokalemia and other imbalances that are directly life-threatening. Electrolyte abnormalities are among the most serious medical complications of eating disorders.[^25]

**Hormonal imbalances:** Addison’s disease (adrenal insufficiency) reduces aldosterone production, leading to sodium wasting and potassium retention. Hyperaldosteronism (excess aldosterone, often from a benign adrenal tumor) causes the opposite: sodium retention and potassium depletion. Both conditions require medical diagnosis and management.[^26]

**Certain medications:** ACE inhibitors and ARBs (common blood pressure medications) can elevate potassium. NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) can impair kidney electrolyte handling. SSRIs — particularly in older adults — are associated with hyponatremia, likely through a mechanism involving inappropriate ADH secretion.[^27]

### Who Is Most Vulnerable?

**Older adults** face a convergence of risk factors: reduced kidney efficiency, diminished thirst sensation (meaning they may not drink enough even when dehydrated), lower dietary intake, and polypharmacy — the use of multiple medications, many of which affect electrolyte handling.[^28]

**Athletes and active individuals** lose electrolytes through sweat and may not replace them adequately, particularly if relying on plain water alone during long events.[^29]

**Pregnant and breastfeeding women** have increased requirements for calcium, magnesium, and potassium, and their fluid dynamics change substantially.[^30]

**People following heavily plant-based diets** generally enjoy favorable electrolyte profiles — high potassium and magnesium from vegetables, legumes, nuts, and seeds — but may need to pay attention to calcium (if not consuming fortified foods or dairy), iodine (particularly if not using iodized salt), and iron.[^31]

**People in hot climates or doing physical labor** may have large daily sweat losses that exceed typical dietary replacement without intentional attention to electrolyte intake.[^32]

## Recognizing Electrolyte Imbalance: Symptoms by Electrolyte

One of the more striking things about electrolyte imbalances is how many common, everyday symptoms they can produce — fatigue, headache, muscle cramps, brain fog, sleep trouble — that are easy to attribute to stress, poor sleep, or aging. This section walks through the most clinically relevant patterns for each major electrolyte.

A note before we begin: this information is meant to help you recognize patterns worth investigating, not to self-diagnose or self-treat serious imbalances. Significant electrolyte disturbances require laboratory evaluation and, often, medical management. When in doubt, consult a clinician.

### Sodium

Sodium is the electrolyte most tightly controlled by the body — and the one whose disturbances can be most dramatically dangerous.

**Hyponatremia (low sodium):** Symptoms range from mild to life-threatening depending on severity and how quickly sodium drops. Early signs include nausea, headache, fatigue, and difficulty concentrating. As levels fall further: confusion, disorientation, muscle weakness, and in severe cases, seizures and coma. Chronic mild hyponatremia — which can occur with long-term SSRI use or low-sodium diets combined with high water intake — may present as persistent cognitive fog, unsteady gait, and increased fall risk in older adults.[^33]

**Hypernatremia (high sodium):** More commonly caused by dehydration than by excess sodium intake. Signs include intense thirst, dry mucous membranes, agitation, confusion, and in severe cases, seizures. Older adults with impaired thirst sensation are particularly vulnerable.[^34]

> **📌 Key Fact:** Drinking large amounts of plain water during or after intense exercise can actually lower blood sodium — the opposite of what most people expect. Hyponatremia in endurance athletes is well-documented and has caused deaths. The solution is not to drink less, but to ensure adequate sodium intake around prolonged exercise.

### Potassium

Potassium is critical for cardiac rhythm, and its imbalances carry significant heart risk.

**Hypokalemia (low potassium):** Common causes include diuretic use, vomiting, diarrhea, and insufficient dietary intake. Symptoms: muscle weakness and fatigue, cramps and spasms, constipation, palpitations, and in severe cases, dangerous cardiac arrhythmias. Even mild hypokalemia increases the risk of irregular heart rhythm, particularly in people with existing heart disease or those taking digoxin.[^35]

**Hyperkalemia (high potassium):** Most commonly associated with kidney disease, ACE inhibitor or ARB use, and potassium-sparing diuretics. Symptoms can be subtle (muscle weakness, fatigue) until levels are dangerously high — at which point cardiac arrhythmia and cardiac arrest become real risks. Severe hyperkalemia is a medical emergency.[^36]

> **📌 Key Fact:** The ratio of dietary potassium to sodium matters as much as absolute intake. Western diets typically invert the ratio that appears to support cardiovascular health: high sodium, low potassium. Increasing potassium-rich whole foods while moderating sodium appears to meaningfully reduce cardiovascular risk.[^37]

### Magnesium

Magnesium deficiency may be the most common electrolyte insufficiency that most people have never been told about.

**Hypomagnesemia (low magnesium):** Population studies suggest that a substantial proportion of adults — estimated between 10–30% or higher in some populations — do not meet dietary reference intakes for magnesium, with surveys indicating intake below recommended levels in many subgroups.[^38] Symptoms are wide-ranging: muscle cramps and spasms, sleep disruption, anxiety and irritability, fatigue, constipation, headaches and migraines, and heart palpitations. Chronic low magnesium is also associated with insulin resistance, elevated blood pressure, and increased inflammation markers.[^39]

A clinically important wrinkle: the standard serum magnesium test often misses functional deficiency. Less than 1% of total body magnesium is in the bloodstream — most is stored in bone and soft tissue. Serum levels can appear normal even when intracellular magnesium is significantly depleted. Red blood cell (RBC) magnesium testing provides a more reliable reflection of tissue stores.[^40]

**Hypermagnesemia (excess magnesium):** Rare in people with normal kidney function, since the kidneys excrete excess magnesium efficiently. Risk increases substantially in kidney disease or with high-dose magnesium supplementation.

> **📌 Key Fact:** Magnesium is required as a cofactor for vitamin D activation. If you’re supplementing vitamin D but are magnesium-deficient, the vitamin D may not function properly — and high-dose vitamin D can further deplete magnesium. The two work as a team.[^41]

### Calcium

**Hypocalcemia (low calcium):** Symptoms include muscle spasms and cramps (especially in the hands, feet, and around the mouth), numbness and tingling, and in severe cases, tetany (sustained muscle contraction) and seizures. Chronic low calcium, particularly with vitamin D deficiency, contributes to bone loss over time.[^42]

**Hypercalcemia (high calcium):** Often caused by hyperparathyroidism, certain cancers, or excessive calcium/vitamin D supplementation. Classic symptoms are sometimes summarized as “bones, stones, groans, and moans” — bone pain, kidney stones, abdominal pain, and neuropsychiatric symptoms including depression and confusion.[^43]

Calcium works closely with magnesium and vitamin D. Supplementing calcium without adequate magnesium and vitamin D may not produce the benefits anticipated — and high-dose calcium supplementation has raised questions in the research literature regarding cardiovascular risk, discussed further in the supplementation section.

### Chloride and Phosphate

**Chloride** typically mirrors sodium in its balance dynamics. Low chloride (hypochloremia) most often occurs with vomiting (which loses gastric hydrochloric acid), diuretic use, or metabolic alkalosis. It’s rarely a primary target of dietary intervention.

**Phosphate** imbalances are typically encountered in clinical settings: hypophosphatemia occurs with refeeding syndrome, alcoholism, certain antacid overuse, and diabetic ketoacidosis treatment. Hyperphosphatemia is associated with kidney disease and, at high levels, contributes to cardiovascular calcification.[^44]

## Testing and Assessment

### What Standard Blood Tests Show

The most commonly ordered electrolyte panels are the Basic Metabolic Panel (BMP) and Comprehensive Metabolic Panel (CMP), which measure sodium, potassium, chloride, and bicarbonate (as CO₂) in serum. These panels provide clinically useful information and are the standard starting point for evaluating electrolyte status.[^45]

For calcium, phosphate, and magnesium, additional tests may be ordered separately or as part of a broader nutritional workup.

### The Limitation of Serum Testing for Magnesium

Standard serum magnesium measurement is a useful screen, but its limitation is significant: because the body tightly regulates serum levels (drawing on bone and tissue stores when intake is low), serum magnesium can appear within the normal reference range even when total body stores are meaningfully depleted. This has led researchers and clinicians to advocate for **red blood cell (RBC) magnesium testing** as a more reliable indicator of actual tissue magnesium status.[^46]

Some functional and integrative medicine practitioners also use 24-hour urinary magnesium excretion or magnesium loading tests (measuring retention after a standardized dose) to assess functional deficiency. The evidence base for these approaches varies, and they are not universally adopted in mainstream clinical practice — but they reflect genuine attempts to address the known limitation of serum measurement.[^47]

### Urinary Electrolyte Testing

Spot urine and 24-hour urine collection for electrolytes provide information about excretion patterns — particularly useful for evaluating sodium intake (where dietary recall is notoriously unreliable) and potassium and calcium balance. Urinary electrolyte testing is a standard tool in nephrology and is used in research to estimate dietary intake more accurately than self-report.[^48]

### Hair Mineral Analysis

Hair mineral analysis (HMA) involves measuring mineral concentrations in a sample of hair. It is used in functional medicine, naturopathic medicine, and some integrative nutrition practices as a means of assessing mineral status over time (reflecting weeks to months rather than a snapshot of serum levels).

The evidence landscape for HMA warrants an honest look. Mainstream medical organizations, including the American Medical Association, have expressed skepticism, noting concerns about laboratory variability between testing facilities, the influence of external contamination (hair products, water mineral content), and the limited correlation between hair mineral concentrations and serum or tissue mineral status for many elements.[^49]

At the same time, researchers have noted that for some elements — particularly toxic heavy metals like lead, mercury, and arsenic — hair analysis does have a recognized role and is used in occupational medicine and forensic toxicology.[^50] The more contested area is its use for assessing nutritional minerals like magnesium, zinc, and calcium.

For coaches and clients: HMA can be a starting point for conversation, but significant clinical decisions regarding mineral supplementation are better supported by blood-based testing. If a client has had an HMA and it’s raising questions, that’s a reasonable prompt for a conversation with their physician or a registered dietitian — not a reason to self-prescribe high-dose mineral supplements.

### When to Seek Professional Evaluation

For generally healthy individuals with no significant medical history, mild lifestyle-associated electrolyte concerns (e.g., frequent muscle cramps, fatigue) can often be addressed through dietary changes before testing is necessary. However, testing is appropriate when:

- Symptoms are significant, persistent, or worsening
- The person has a relevant medical condition (kidney disease, heart disease, diabetes)
- They are taking medications known to affect electrolytes
- They are considering high-dose mineral supplementation
- Dietary changes alone haven’t produced improvement after a few weeks

## Food First: Restoring Balance Through Diet

Before reaching for a supplement bottle, it’s worth considering what can be accomplished through food — which, in most cases, is quite a lot.

Whole foods provide electrolytes in forms the body recognizes and can regulate efficiently. They come packaged with cofactors, fiber, and phytonutrients that support absorption and overall metabolism. And the evidence consistently shows that dietary patterns — not individual nutrients in isolation — are the foundation of long-term health.[^51]

### Top Food Sources by Electrolyte

**Sodium:** Found abundantly in processed and packaged foods, restaurant meals, and condiments. Whole foods naturally contain modest amounts; the problem in most Western diets is excess, not deficiency. Those who do need sodium support (athletes with large sweat losses, people in heat, certain medical conditions) can get it from salted whole foods, broths, olives, and fermented foods.

**Potassium:** Bananas get most of the attention, but they’re not even in the top tier. Outstanding sources include sweet potatoes, potatoes (with skin), lentils, black beans, kidney beans, leafy greens (especially spinach, chard, and beet greens), avocados, squash, tomato products, salmon, and dried apricots. A diet genuinely rich in vegetables and legumes is a diet rich in potassium.[^52]

**Magnesium:** Dark leafy greens (spinach, Swiss chard), pumpkin seeds (an exceptional source), black beans, almonds, cashews, dark chocolate (70%+), whole grains, edamame, and avocado. Notably, magnesium content in plant foods has declined in recent decades due to soil depletion — a legitimate consideration for those relying on food alone.[^53]

**Calcium:** Dairy products remain among the most bioavailable sources. For those who don’t consume dairy, excellent sources include fortified plant milks, canned salmon and sardines (with bones), tofu (made with calcium sulfate), bok choy, broccoli, kale, chia seeds, and figs. Note that some calcium-rich plant foods (spinach, beet greens) contain oxalates that reduce calcium absorption — variety matters.[^54]

**Chloride:** Comes primarily from sodium chloride (table salt and salted foods). It is rarely deficient in people eating a normal diet.

**Phosphate:** Found in protein-rich foods — meat, fish, poultry, eggs, dairy, legumes, nuts, and seeds. Most people eating adequate protein meet phosphate needs without effort.

### The Potassium-Sodium Ratio

The relationship between these two electrolytes matters as much as their absolute amounts. High sodium intake raises blood pressure in part by promoting fluid retention; adequate potassium counteracts this by promoting sodium excretion and relaxing blood vessel walls. Research suggests that a potassium-to-sodium ratio of approximately 1:1 or higher (by weight) is associated with better cardiovascular outcomes — a ratio that hunter-gatherer diets are estimated to have achieved, and that most Western diets invert dramatically.[^55]

Practical implication: the goal isn’t just to reduce sodium; it’s to increase potassium-rich whole foods alongside modest sodium.

### A Note on Cooking Methods

Boiling vegetables in large amounts of water leaches water-soluble minerals, including potassium and magnesium, into the cooking water. Steaming, roasting, or stir-frying preserves more of these minerals. If you do boil vegetables, using the cooking water in soups or sauces recaptures much of what was lost.[^56]

### Hydration and Electrolytes

For most people in most circumstances, plain water is entirely adequate for hydration. The body is designed to extract electrolytes from food and maintain balance through normal kidney function.

When electrolyte-enhanced hydration becomes relevant:

- Endurance exercise lasting more than 60–90 minutes, particularly in heat
- Work in hot environments with substantial sweating
- Illness involving vomiting, diarrhea, or fever
- Very low-carbohydrate or ketogenic diets (due to increased sodium and potassium excretion)
- Hot weather with significant daily sweat loss

In these situations, mineral-rich water, broths, or diluted electrolyte drinks can be useful. For everyday life, food-based electrolyte intake plus adequate water intake is generally sufficient.[^57]

### An Electrolyte-Rich Day of Eating

Here’s a simple picture of what a day of eating that supports electrolyte balance can look like:

- **Breakfast:** Oatmeal with pumpkin seeds, a sliced banana, and a handful of almonds; or a spinach-mushroom egg scramble with a glass of fortified plant milk
- **Lunch:** A large salad with lentils, avocado, roasted sweet potato, pumpkin seeds, and dark leafy greens; a piece of fruit
- **Dinner:** Salmon or sardines with roasted vegetables (beets, squash, leafy greens) and a small serving of brown rice or whole grain bread
- **Snacks:** Figs, a small piece of dark chocolate, edamame, or a small handful of mixed nuts

No supplements required in this pattern for most healthy adults. The variety does the work.

## Supplementation: What the Research Shows

Supplements have their place — but they work best when they fill genuine gaps that food alone cannot address, rather than substituting for a diet that could provide those nutrients directly.

That said, modern food systems, soil depletion, medication interactions, and medical conditions mean that some people genuinely benefit from targeted supplementation. Here is what the research shows for the major electrolytes.

### Magnesium: The Most Commonly Indicated

Magnesium is the electrolyte most likely to need supplemental support in the modern context, given widespread dietary insufficiency and its depletion by common medications and lifestyle factors.

**Forms matter.** Magnesium supplements come in many forms, and they are not interchangeable:

- **Magnesium glycinate:** Well-absorbed, gentle on the digestive system, well-suited for sleep support and anxiety-related symptoms. Often the first-line recommendation for those who are sensitive to digestive effects.[^58]
- **Magnesium citrate:** Good bioavailability; can have a mild laxative effect at higher doses, which is sometimes intentionally used for constipation support.
- **Magnesium malate:** Used by some for energy support and fibromyalgia-related muscle pain; reasonably well-absorbed.
- **Magnesium threonate:** A newer form designed to cross the blood-brain barrier more effectively; some research suggests cognitive benefits, though evidence is still developing.[^59]
- **Magnesium oxide:** Most common in inexpensive supplements; poorly absorbed compared to other forms. Useful for constipation but not ideal for replenishing tissue stores.

**Typical supplemental dosing** in clinical literature ranges from 200–400 mg/day of elemental magnesium for adults, with the Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) from supplements set at 350 mg/day by the U.S. National Institutes of Health — above which laxative effects become more likely.[^60]

Magnesium supplementation has demonstrated benefit in research for: improving sleep quality, reducing migraine frequency and severity, lowering blood pressure in hypertensive individuals, improving insulin sensitivity, and reducing muscle cramps.[^61]

### Potassium: Proceed Carefully

High-dose potassium supplements are regulated by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration — individual doses are capped at 99 mg precisely because of cardiac risk from excess potassium, particularly in people with impaired kidney function or those on medications that raise potassium levels.

For most people, food-first approaches to potassium adequacy are both safer and more effective than supplementation. Electrolyte blends containing lower doses of potassium (often 200–400 mg per serving) can be appropriate for athletes, people on ketogenic diets, or those with documented losses — but anyone on ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or potassium-sparing diuretics should discuss supplementation with their physician before starting.[^62]

### Sodium: When More Is Actually Needed

The most common sodium problem in developed countries is excess, not deficiency — and the advice to reduce sodium is well-established for most adults.[^63]

However, certain situations genuinely call for increased sodium intake:

- Athletes doing endurance events in heat, where sweat losses can be 1–3 grams of sodium per hour
- People on very low-carbohydrate diets experiencing increased urinary sodium losses
- Individuals with illness causing significant fluid and electrolyte losses
- Those with conditions involving aldosterone deficiency (requiring medical management)

In these contexts, adding sodium through food (broth, lightly salted meals, electrolyte drinks) is appropriate and beneficial. Salt tablets are occasionally used in extreme endurance settings but warrant caution.

### Calcium: Food First, Cofactors Essential

Calcium supplementation has a nuanced evidence picture. While calcium is essential for bone health, high-dose calcium supplements (particularly calcium carbonate, taken without food) have been associated in some studies with increased risk of kidney stones and, controversially, cardiovascular events — a finding that has generated significant research debate.[^64] The current prevailing view from most guidelines is that calcium from food is preferable to calcium from supplements for most people, and that supplemental doses should be modest when needed.

When supplementing:

- **Calcium citrate** is better absorbed than calcium carbonate, especially in those with reduced stomach acid (common in older adults and those on acid-reducing medications), and can be taken with or without food.
- **Calcium carbonate** requires stomach acid for absorption and should be taken with meals.
- **Cofactors matter critically:** Calcium supplements without adequate vitamin D (for absorption) and magnesium (for metabolic function and distribution) are less effective and potentially more problematic. Vitamin K2 also appears to help direct calcium into bone rather than soft tissue.[^65]

**Supplemental dose guidance:** Most adults need 1,000–1,200 mg of total calcium per day (dietary plus supplemental combined). Supplemental doses above 500 mg at a time are absorbed less efficiently.[^66]

### Evaluating Electrolyte Drinks and Powders

The commercial electrolyte market is vast and highly variable in quality. When evaluating a product:

**Look for:**

- Meaningful potassium content (at least 200–400 mg per serving — many products contain very little)
- Meaningful magnesium content (at least 50–100 mg per serving)
- Sodium from recognizable sources (not just table salt derivatives is fine)
- Minimal added sugar (or sweetened with small amounts of natural sweeteners rather than large doses of sucrose or glucose)

**Be cautious of:**

- Products with 20+ grams of sugar per serving (more candy than recovery)
- Products with proprietary blends that don’t disclose individual electrolyte amounts
- Products making aggressive therapeutic claims without clinical evidence
- Artificial colors and sweeteners in products marketed as “natural”

Coconut water merits specific mention: it is a legitimate source of potassium (roughly 400–600 mg per cup) and contains modest sodium and magnesium. It is a reasonable hydration choice for moderate activity. However, its sodium content is lower than clinical oral rehydration solutions, and it should not be relied upon as a replacement for medical rehydration in serious fluid losses.[^67]

## Contested Topics: An Honest Look

These topics come up regularly in wellness contexts. They deserve direct, honest engagement rather than dismissal or unqualified endorsement.

### Sea Salt, Himalayan Pink Salt, and Celtic Salt vs. Table Salt

Walk into any natural food store and you’ll find shelves devoted to specialty salts — Himalayan pink salt, Celtic grey salt, various sea salts — often marketed as nutritionally superior to iodized table salt.

What does the evidence show? Specialty salts do contain trace minerals — Himalayan pink salt, for instance, contains iron (which gives it its color), potassium, magnesium, calcium, and dozens of other minerals. The amounts are real but small: a typical serving of Himalayan pink salt provides trace quantities of these minerals, but amounts far below what would constitute meaningful nutritional contribution.[^68] To meet daily magnesium needs from Himalayan salt alone, you would need to consume quantities of sodium far beyond any healthy intake.

The more important nutritional question is iodine. Iodized table salt is a primary iodine source in many populations. Specialty salts are typically not iodized. For most people eating iodized salt or consuming dairy, eggs, seafood, or seaweed regularly, iodine intake is adequate. For those following strict plant-based diets who switch entirely to specialty salt, iodine deficiency is a genuine risk.[^69]

**Honest summary:** The evidence for dramatic health superiority of specialty salts over iodized table salt is limited. The trace mineral differences are real but nutritionally minor at typical intake levels. The iodine question is worth taking seriously, particularly for plant-based eaters.

### “Adrenal Fatigue” and Electrolyte Balance

“Adrenal fatigue” is not a recognized diagnosis in conventional medicine. The adrenal glands don’t become “tired” in the way the term implies — and the use of the label has drawn reasonable criticism for potentially leading people away from proper investigation of symptoms.[^70]

At the same time, the underlying physiology invoked in discussions of “adrenal fatigue” is real and clinically recognized: the HPA (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal) axis, which governs the stress response, does show measurable changes under conditions of chronic stress — including alterations in cortisol rhythm, DHEA levels, and, through aldosterone, sodium-potassium balance.[^71]

The important bridge here is aldosterone. Aldosterone, produced by the adrenal glands, signals the kidneys to retain sodium and excrete potassium. When adrenal function is reduced — whether through chronic stress dysregulation, autoimmune adrenal disease, or other causes — sodium wasting and potassium retention can result. This is most clearly seen in Addison’s disease (true adrenal insufficiency), where electrolyte disturbances can be severe and life-threatening.[^72]

People who report improvement with “adrenal support” protocols — which often include increased sodium intake, electrolyte support, and stress reduction — may be experiencing genuine physiological benefit related to these mechanisms, even if the “adrenal fatigue” framework is scientifically imprecise.

**For coaches:** Avoid using “adrenal fatigue” as a diagnostic label. If a client presents with significant symptoms (profound fatigue, postural dizziness, salt craving, darkened skin pigmentation, unexplained weight loss), these warrant medical evaluation to rule out actual adrenal insufficiency — not a coaching conversation.

### Electrolytes on Low-Carbohydrate and Ketogenic Diets

This is one of the better-studied areas at the intersection of diet and electrolytes. The mechanism is straightforward and well-established: insulin signals the kidneys to retain sodium. When carbohydrate intake drops and insulin levels fall, the kidneys excrete more sodium — and with sodium, water, and other electrolytes including potassium and magnesium follow.[^73]

This is the primary explanation for “keto flu” — the headaches, fatigue, muscle cramps, brain fog, and irritability that many people experience in the first one to three weeks of a ketogenic diet. These symptoms are largely electrolyte-related and often substantially relieved by proactive sodium, potassium, and magnesium supplementation.[^74]

What the research supports: intentional electrolyte replenishment is generally recommended for people on ketogenic diets, particularly in the adaptation phase. Higher sodium intake (3–5 grams per day is commonly referenced in clinical ketogenic diet protocols), along with food-based potassium and supplemental magnesium, can meaningfully reduce adaptation-phase symptoms.[^75]

**Caution:** Aggressive electrolyte supplementation without medical supervision carries risk, particularly for people with kidney disease, heart conditions, or those on medications affecting potassium. If someone is on a ketogenic diet for medical reasons (epilepsy, cancer adjunct, metabolic disease), electrolyte management should be supervised by a clinician.

### Sports Drinks, Coconut Water, and “Natural” Electrolyte Claims

The commercial sports drink market was built on a genuine scientific premise: electrolyte replacement during endurance exercise improves performance and safety compared to plain water alone.[^76] The evidence for this is solid in appropriate contexts.

Where the evidence thins is in the everyday consumption of sugar-sweetened sports drinks by people doing low-to-moderate activity who are not sweating significantly. In this context, the electrolyte benefit is minimal and the sugar load — typically 14–18 grams per 12 oz — is a net negative for most health goals.

The practical guidance: for exercise under 60 minutes at moderate intensity in a temperate environment, plain water is adequate. For longer or more intense activity, a low-sugar electrolyte drink or diluted sports drink provides meaningful benefit.

Coconut water occupies a middle ground: genuinely good potassium content, minimal processed ingredients, naturally low sugar compared to commercial sports drinks — but lower sodium than clinical rehydration standards and not ideal for severe fluid losses.[^77]

## Special Populations and Cautions

### Kidney Disease

This deserves explicit emphasis: people with kidney disease — even early-stage chronic kidney disease — are at meaningful risk from electrolyte supplementation without medical supervision. The kidneys’ reduced capacity to excrete potassium and phosphate means that amounts safe for healthy people can accumulate to dangerous levels. Potassium supplements, high-potassium “electrolyte boosts,” and phosphate-rich supplements should not be used by people with kidney disease without clinician guidance.[^78]

### Heart Conditions

Potassium, magnesium, and calcium balance directly influence cardiac rhythm. People with arrhythmias, heart failure, or those taking digoxin or antiarrhythmic medications are particularly sensitive to electrolyte fluctuations. Anyone with a heart condition should discuss electrolyte supplementation with their cardiologist or primary physician before making changes.[^79]

### Medications That Affect Electrolytes

Coaches working with clients on the following medications should be aware of electrolyte implications and encourage periodic monitoring:

- **Loop diuretics** (furosemide, bumetanide): deplete potassium, magnesium, sodium, calcium
- **Thiazide diuretics** (hydrochlorothiazide): deplete potassium, magnesium, sodium; can raise calcium
- **ACE inhibitors / ARBs:** can raise potassium — caution with potassium supplementation
- **Corticosteroids:** can deplete potassium and calcium
- **SSRIs:** associated with hyponatremia, particularly in older adults
- **NSAIDs:** can impair kidney handling of potassium and sodium[^80]

### Eating Disorders

Electrolyte imbalances associated with eating disorders — particularly hypokalemia from purging — can be life-threatening and require medical evaluation and management. This is not a coaching conversation. A wellness coach who identifies signs of an active eating disorder should facilitate a warm referral to appropriate medical and mental health care. Electrolyte support, without addressing the underlying behavior, is not adequate treatment.[^81]

## When to Seek Medical Care

### Seek Emergency Care Immediately For:

- Confusion, disorientation, or altered level of consciousness
- Irregular heartbeat or palpitations that are new or unusual
- Significant muscle weakness or inability to use a limb normally
- Seizures
- Extreme fatigue or collapse
- Signs of severe dehydration: no urination for many hours, very dark urine, rapid heart rate, dizziness on standing

These can indicate serious electrolyte disturbances or other medical emergencies. Do not wait to see if they resolve.

### Schedule a Clinician Appointment For:

- Persistent muscle cramps that don’t improve with dietary changes over 2–3 weeks
- Ongoing fatigue, brain fog, or significant sleep disruption
- Blood pressure readings that are consistently outside the normal range
- Frequent headaches or migraines, especially if new or worsening
- Known kidney disease, heart disease, or diabetes — before making any dietary changes or starting supplements
- Regular use of diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, SSRIs, or other medications listed above
- Suspected or known eating disorder
- Pregnancy, particularly with unusual symptoms

## Practical Takeaways: Small Steps, Real Benefit

You don’t need to overhaul everything at once. Electrolyte support, for most people, comes down to consistent attention to a few foundational habits.

### A Self-Assessment Starting Point

Ask yourself:

1. Am I eating a variety of whole vegetables, legumes, and whole grains daily? (Potassium and magnesium)
1. Am I eating 2–3 servings of calcium-rich foods daily?
1. Is most of my sodium coming from whole foods and moderate amounts of salt at home, rather than processed food and restaurant meals?
1. Am I drinking enough water — but not excessive amounts of plain water during or after intense exercise?
1. Am I on any medications that affect electrolytes?

If most answers are yes, you’re likely in reasonable shape. If several are no, food-first adjustments can make a meaningful difference.

### Simple Daily Habits

- Include a leafy green with at least one meal per day
- Add a serving of legumes (lentils, beans, edamame) most days
- Use pumpkin seeds or almonds as a regular snack
- Check your salt: if you’re on a plant-based diet, make sure some of your salt is iodized
- If you’re exercising intensely in heat, don’t rely on plain water alone

### Reading Supplement Labels

When evaluating a magnesium supplement:

- Look for the *elemental* magnesium content, not just the weight of the compound (e.g., “500 mg magnesium glycinate” contains far less than 500 mg of actual magnesium)
- Choose glycinate, citrate, or malate for absorption; avoid oxide if replenishment is the goal
- Start at the lower end of dosing and increase as tolerated

When evaluating an electrolyte drink or powder:

- Compare potassium content: anything under 100 mg per serving is largely symbolic
- Check total sugar per serving
- Verify the company discloses individual ingredient amounts rather than hiding behind a “proprietary blend”

### A Closing Thought

The body’s capacity to maintain balance — given the right inputs — is remarkable. Trillions of cells, coordinated by chemistry that took billions of years to develop, are working right now to keep you in precisely the equilibrium you need. Supporting that system doesn’t require perfection. It requires consistency: eating real food in variety, drinking adequate water, paying attention to what your body signals, and knowing when those signals warrant professional evaluation.

The minerals in your food are not abstract chemistry. They are the literal building blocks of how you move, think, feel, and heal. Treating them with care is treating yourself with care.
