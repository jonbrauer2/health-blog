## TL;DR

- Sugar cravings are driven by your physiology — blood sugar swings, reward chemistry, stress hormones, lost sleep, gut bacteria, and an engineered food supply — not by a personal failing.
- The average U.S. adult eats about 17 teaspoons of added sugar a day, roughly double or triple the limits set by the American Heart Association, and high intake is linked to heart disease death, fatty liver, and other harms.
- You have real, doable tools: protein and fiber at meals, a 10-minute walk after eating, better sleep, stress care, and a handful of supplements with modest-but-real evidence.

## Key Findings

- **“Sugar addiction” is a genuine scientific debate, not a settled fact.** Animal studies show addiction-like behavior (bingeing, withdrawal, craving), but only under intermittent access; human evidence is far thinner, and the picture points more toward the rewarding pull of sweet, highly palatable foods than to sugar acting like a classic drug.
- **Blood sugar “dips” predict hunger.** In a 2021 study using continuous glucose monitors, people with the biggest post-meal glucose dips felt 9% hungrier and ate about 312 more calories over a day.
- **The liver is sugar’s quiet target.** Fatty liver disease now affects about 30% of adults worldwide, and the fructose half of sugar is a key driver.
- **Functional tools vary in strength.** Post-meal walks and protein/fiber are well-supported; berberine and cinnamon have real but modest blood-sugar effects; chromium is weak and inconsistent; L-glutamine for cravings is mostly anecdotal.

## 1. The 3 p.m. Pull (Introduction)

You know the moment. It’s mid-afternoon, the meeting won’t end, and suddenly the vending machine down the hall is the most interesting thing in the building. Or it’s 9 p.m., dinner is long over, and your feet carry you to the kitchen for something sweet you didn’t plan and aren’t even hungry for. Or there’s a birthday cake at the office, and a slice is on your plate before you’ve decided anything.

Here’s the first thing worth knowing: that pull is not a flaw in your character. It’s biology doing exactly what biology evolved to do. Your brain is wired to seek out quick energy, and modern food is engineered to light up that wiring. Feeling the craving doesn’t mean something is wrong with you — it means your nervous system, hormones, and even the bacteria in your gut are all working in a world very different from the one they were built for.

The scale of that world is striking. In 2017–2018, U.S. adults aged 20 and older averaged 17.1 teaspoons of added sugar a day — about 19 teaspoons for men and 15 for women  [^1]. That’s roughly two to three times what major health bodies suggest, and it adds up to around 60 pounds of added sugar per person per year — the equivalent of six 10-pound bowling balls  [^2]. We are, collectively, swimming in the stuff — and most of us never decided to be.

This document isn’t here to scare you, and it isn’t here to tell you that everything is fine in moderation and you should stop worrying. It’s here to equip you. We’ll walk through why cravings happen in the body, what excess sugar actually does over time, where the science is solid and where it’s still arguing with itself, and — most importantly — what you can actually do that works.

> ### 📌 KEY FACT BOX #1: How Much Is Too Much?
> 
> - **American Heart Association limits:** no more than **6 teaspoons (25 g)** of added sugar per day for most women and **9 teaspoons (36 g)** for most men   [^3].
> - **World Health Organization:** keep “free sugars” under **10% of total calories**, and ideally under **5%** — about **6 teaspoons (25 g)** a day for additional benefit  [^4].
> - **Reality:** U.S. adults average about **17 teaspoons (around 71 g)** daily  — roughly **double to triple** these targets [^1].

## 2. What Sugar Actually Is (and Isn’t)

“Sugar” is a slippery word, so let’s sort it out. Chemically, the sugars in your diet are mostly simple carbohydrates: glucose, fructose, and the duo of the two bonded together, sucrose (ordinary table sugar). Your body runs on glucose. That part is non-negotiable — your brain alone burns through a remarkable amount of it every day.

The useful distinction is not “sugar = bad” but *where the sugar lives*. **Naturally occurring sugars** come packaged inside whole foods — the fructose in an apple, the lactose in milk, the sugars woven into whole grains. They arrive bundled with fiber, water, protein, and nutrients that slow digestion and blunt the blood sugar rise. **Added sugars** (also called “free sugars”) are those put into foods during processing or cooking, plus the sugars in honey, syrups, and fruit juices  [^4]. These hit your system fast, without the brakes.

This is why an apple and a glass of apple juice behave so differently in your body even with similar sugar content — the fiber in the whole fruit slows everything down  [^1].

But it’s worth being honest: the picture is more nuanced than “fruit sugar good, table sugar bad.” A molecule of fructose is a molecule of fructose whether it comes from agave or an apple. What changes the story is the *dose*, the *speed*, and the *company it keeps* — the fiber, the food matrix, and how much you eat at once. Honey, agave, coconut sugar, and maple syrup are all still added sugars. We’ll return to whether they’re meaningfully “healthier” later (short version: not really).

## 3. The Physiology of Sugar Cravings: Why Your Brain Wants What It Wants

Cravings aren’t one thing. They’re the output of several systems running at once. Here are the big ones.

### The dopamine reward loop — and the “sugar is like cocaine” claim

When you eat something sweet, your brain releases dopamine  in a region called the nucleus accumbens — the same reward circuitry that responds to other pleasurable things [^5]. This is real and well-documented. Sweet taste signals “energy ahead,” and your brain rewards you for finding it. That’s not a malfunction; it kept our ancestors alive.

The headline claim — “sugar is as addictive as cocaine” — comes mostly from rat studies. In the work of Avena, Rada, and Hoebel, rats given *intermittent* access to sugar developed behaviors that look like addiction: bingeing, signs of withdrawal (anxiety when sugar was removed), craving, and cross-sensitization to drugs of abuse [^5]. Their brains showed changes in dopamine and opioid receptors. It’s genuinely striking research.

But here’s the nuance the headlines skip: those effects appeared mainly under a specific feeding schedule — limited, intermittent access, essentially “skipping breakfast” then bingeing on sugar [^6]. When rats have free access, the addiction-like pattern largely fades. And critically, humans almost never eat sugar in isolation the way lab rats do. We’ll dig into this debate more in Section 5, because it deserves an honest hearing from both sides.

### The blood sugar roller coaster

Eat a fast-digesting sugary food and your blood glucose climbs quickly. Your pancreas answers with insulin to bring it back down. Sometimes it overshoots, and glucose dips *below* where it started — a “sugar dip” a couple of hours later. That dip is a craving generator.

The strongest evidence here comes from a 2021 study published in *Nature Metabolism* that put continuous glucose monitors on 1,070 people and tracked their meals and hunger  [^7]. People with the biggest post-meal glucose dips at 2–3 hours — the “big dippers” — reported **9% more hunger**, waited about **half an hour less** before their next meal, and ate about **75 more calories** in the hours after breakfast and roughly **312 more calories over the whole day** than “little dippers” eating identical meals  [^7]. The dip, it turned out, predicted hunger better than the peak did.  (Worth noting: the study was funded by and partly conducted by ZOE, a company selling nutrition products, so it’s fair to read it with that in mind  — but the mechanism is biologically sound and consistent with other work [^7].)

### Cortisol and stress

Stress and sugar are old friends. When stress turns chronic, your adrenal glands release cortisol, which increases appetite and specifically nudges you toward calorie-dense “comfort” foods high in sugar and fat [^8]. There’s a feedback loop too: those foods seem to temporarily dampen the body’s stress response, which is exactly why they feel comforting — and why the pattern repeats [^9]. Comfort food, it turns out, really does comfort, at least in the short term. The catch is that the relief is brief and the loop reinforces itself.

### Sleep deprivation

Lose sleep and your food choices change measurably — not because you’ve gotten “weaker,” but because your brain chemistry shifts. In controlled studies, sleep-restricted adults crave and choose more calorie-dense, high-fat, high-sugar snacks [^10]. One mechanism: sleep loss raises endocannabinoid levels (the same system cannabis acts on), which boosts the pleasure of eating; another study showed sleep loss elevates a compound (2-OG) tied to changes in food selection [^11]. Even one night of partial sleep can reduce insulin sensitivity, leaving more sugar circulating [^10]. Conversely, *adding* sleep reduces appetite and the desire for sweet and salty foods [^12].

### The gut microbiome

This is newer, more speculative territory — but fascinating. The trillions of microbes in your gut may influence what you crave. Different bacteria prefer different fuels, and some research suggests they can send signals up the vagus nerve (the information highway connecting gut to brain) that nudge your food choices toward what *they* need [^13]. Germ-free mice prefer more sweets and grow more sweet-taste receptors than normal mice [^13]. People who are “chocolate desiring” show different microbial breakdown products in their urine than “chocolate indifferent” people eating identical diets [^13]. This is a young science — we know correlation more than causation — but the gut-brain conversation around cravings is real and being actively mapped [^14].

### The hunger hormones

A quick tour: **ghrelin** is your “I’m hungry” hormone, rising before meals. **Leptin** signals fullness from your fat stores. **GLP-1** is released after eating and tells your brain you’ve had enough (it’s the target of the new weight-loss drugs). Sleep loss tips this balance — suppressing leptin while raising ghrelin [^11]. Sugar and refined carbs, eaten alone, produce a quick spike-and-crash that can leave these signals out of sync, so you feel hungry again soon. Protein, fat, and fiber work partly by engaging these satiety hormones more steadily.

> ### 📌 KEY FACT BOX #2: Is Sugar Addictive? Two Honest Sides
> 
> **The case for:** Animal studies (Avena and colleagues) show rats on intermittent sugar develop bingeing, withdrawal, craving, and brain changes in dopamine and opioid systems resembling those seen with drugs of abuse [^5]. A rat model meets several DSM-5-style criteria for dependence [^6].
> **The case against:** A major review concluded there is “little evidence to support sugar addiction in humans,” and that the addiction-like behavior in animals appears only with *intermittent access*, not from sugar’s chemistry itself [^15]. Other researchers argue the pull is about *palatability and reward* of sweet foods, not metabolic dependence [^16].
> **Where that leaves us:** The brain reward response to sugar is real. Whether it rises to clinical “addiction” in humans is genuinely unsettled — and “sugar addiction” is not a diagnosis in the DSM. Both the strong-claim and skeptical camps agree reducing excess added sugar is worthwhile.

## 4. What Excess Sugar Does in the Body

Let’s move from wanting to consequences — carefully, because this is an area where claims often outrun evidence.

### Metabolic effects

When you regularly flood your system with fast sugar, cells can grow less responsive to insulin — insulin resistance — which sits upstream of type 2 diabetes. Sugar-sweetened beverages in particular are consistently linked to weight gain and higher diabetes risk in large studies [^1]. The evidence that sugary drinks raise these risks is strong; the precise degree to which sugar *causes* (versus travels with) metabolic disease in every individual is still being refined, because diet is tangled up with calories, weight, and lifestyle.

### The liver and fructose

Here’s the surprising one. Glucose can be used by nearly every cell, but fructose is processed mainly in the liver. When fructose arrives in large amounts, the liver converts much of it to fat through a process called de novo lipogenesis — and that fat can accumulate in the liver itself [^17]. This is now understood as a major driver of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), recently renamed MASLD [^17].

### Cardiovascular effects

The landmark study here is Yang and colleagues, published in *JAMA Internal Medicine* in 2014. Following a large national sample, they found that people who got a high share of their calories from added sugar had a significantly higher risk of dying from cardiovascular disease [^18]. Those who consumed roughly 17% to 21% of their calories from added sugar had a **38% higher risk** of cardiovascular death than those near 8%, and people above 21% had more than double the risk (a hazard ratio of about 2.03)  — and the association held even after adjusting for overall diet quality and other risk factors [^18]. High sugar intake also raises triglycerides and contributes to inflammation, both of which feed heart disease risk [^3].

> ### 📌 KEY FACT BOX #3: Sugar and the Liver
> 
> Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (now MASLD) has become **the most common chronic liver condition in the world, with a global prevalence of about 30%** of adults — and roughly **38% in the most recent U.S. and global estimates (2016–2019), up more than 50% from about 25% in 1990–2006**  [^19]. The fructose portion of added sugar is a key driver, because the liver turns excess fructose into fat [^17]. The surprising part: you don’t have to drink alcohol to give yourself a fatty liver — soda can help do the job.

### Inflammation

Diets high in added sugar, especially from sugary drinks, are associated with markers of low-grade chronic inflammation [^17]. This link is reasonably well-supported for high-sugar diets, though the size of the effect from sugar *specifically* (versus excess weight or overall diet) is where careful researchers add caveats.

### Brain and mental health

This is emerging and genuinely interesting. In the Whitehall II study of British civil servants, men in the highest tertile of sugar intake from sweet foods and drinks (more than about 67 grams a day) had a **23% higher odds** of developing a common mental disorder over five years  (odds ratio 1.23), independent of health behaviors, socio-demographics, diet, body fat, and other diseases  [^20]. Meta-analyses link sugary-drink consumption to higher depression risk [^20]. But — and this is important — nearly all of this research is correlational. We can’t yet say sugar *causes* depression; mood affects eating, and many factors travel together. The honest read: a real and repeated association, with causation still open.

### Dental health

This one is settled. Sugar feeds the bacteria in your mouth that produce acid and erode enamel. The WHO’s sugar guidelines are partly built on strong evidence that dental decay rises with free-sugar intake above 10% of calories [^4]. Brushing helps; less frequent sugar exposure helps more.

### Cancer associations

Here we tread carefully. Large cohort studies have found associations between sugary-drink intake and certain cancers — for instance, a meta-analysis linked each extra 250 mL/day of sugar-sweetened beverages to a 17% greater risk of breast cancer and a 10% greater risk of colorectal cancer, though the certainty of the evidence was rated low  [^21]. The Nurses’ Health Study II found higher sugary-drink intake associated with early-onset colorectal cancer in younger women  [^22]. These are *associations*, not proof of cause. Sugary drinks travel with obesity, which independently raises cancer risk, and untangling the threads is hard. The reasonable takeaway: a plausible, repeatedly observed link — not a verdict.

### Skin and glycation

Popular in skincare and functional medicine is the idea that sugar “ages” your skin. The mechanism is real: glycation, where sugar molecules bind to proteins like collagen and elastin without an enzyme’s help, forming advanced glycation end-products (AGEs)  [^23]. AGEs stiffen and weaken collagen, reducing the skin’s elasticity, and high-sugar diets increase the body’s AGE production  [^23]. Because collagen is long-lived, it’s especially vulnerable to this slow cross-linking  [^24]. How much your daily sugar habit visibly ages your face versus sun, genetics, and time is harder to pin down — but the underlying chemistry is well-established, not fringe.

## 5. The Controversial Corner

Some topics deserve a frank conversation rather than a tidy answer. Here are four.

### Is sugar truly addictive?

We previewed this, but it’s worth sitting with. The animal evidence is real and the brain-reward overlap with drugs is real [^5]. Yet a careful review found “little evidence to support sugar addiction in humans,” noting that the rat bingeing only appears under intermittent access and may reflect the behavior pattern, not sugar’s chemistry [^15]. Some scientists argue what looks like “addiction” is really the powerful reward of sweet, palatable food combined with restrained-then-released eating [^16]. Functional and integrative practitioners often report patients who describe genuine loss of control around sugar and benefit from structured reduction — clinical observations worth taking seriously even where trial data are thin. Mainstream researchers tend to emphasize that “addiction” isn’t a formal diagnosis for sugar. Both can be true: the experience of feeling “hooked” is real, even if the textbook label is contested.

### Artificial and non-sugar sweeteners

Do they help you cut cravings, or keep the sweet habit alive? The evidence is genuinely mixed. The big recent development: in 2023 the WHO advised against using non-sugar sweeteners for weight control, citing reviews suggesting no long-term benefit for body fat and possible associations with higher risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease with long-term use [^25]. But this was a *conditional* recommendation based on low-certainty evidence, and some researchers (including at Harvard) pushed back, noting that swapping sugary drinks for sweetened ones is linked to less weight gain in some large studies [^26]. Aspartame, sucralose, stevia, and monk fruit each have their own evidence trails; none is clearly “the answer,” and the honest position is uncertainty.

### Is fructose worse than glucose?

Fructose behaves differently because the liver handles most of it, and in large amounts it drives liver fat and raises triglycerides more than glucose does [^17]. That’s the kernel of truth behind the “fructose is uniquely bad” narrative. But context matters enormously: the fructose in a piece of fruit, surrounded by fiber and water and consumed in modest amounts, is a very different exposure than the fructose in a liter of soda. The villain isn’t the molecule so much as the dose and delivery.

### “Natural” sweeteners

Honey, maple syrup, coconut sugar, agave — they carry a health halo, and they do contain trace minerals and antioxidants that refined sugar lacks. But metabolically, they’re still added sugars, and agave is actually very high in fructose [^17]. The evidence doesn’t strongly support treating them as meaningfully “healthier” in any quantity that matters. If you prefer the taste of honey, enjoy it — just don’t count it as a free pass.

> ### 📌 KEY FACT BOX #4: The WHO 2023 Sweetener Advisory — What It Does and Doesn’t Mean
> 
> In May 2023, the WHO recommended **against using non-sugar sweeteners** (aspartame, sucralose, stevia, saccharin, and others) as a tool for weight control or to reduce disease risk [^25].
> **What it means:** Don’t rely on diet sodas as a weight-loss strategy; the long-term benefit isn’t there, and there may be downsides.
> **What it doesn’t mean:** It’s not a declaration that sweeteners are dangerous at normal intake — it’s a *conditional* recommendation built on *low-certainty* evidence, and it does **not** apply to people with existing diabetes [^25]. The WHO’s bottom line: the best move is reducing your overall taste for intense sweetness, using whole foods like fruit.

## 6. Working With Your Biology: Practical Approaches

Here’s the encouraging part. Because cravings are physiological, you can change the physiology. None of this requires deprivation or perfection — it requires a few reliable levers.

### Eat to flatten the roller coaster

- **Front-load protein and fat.** Both slow stomach emptying and steady the glucose curve, which means a smaller dip later — and a smaller dip means less hunger and fewer cravings, as the continuous-glucose-monitor research showed [^7].
- **Lean on fiber and lower glycemic load.** Fiber slows sugar absorption; choosing whole foods over refined ones flattens the spike-and-crash. This is the everyday version of what the apple-versus-juice comparison teaches [^1].
- **Mind the timing.** Pairing a sweet food with a meal (rather than eating it alone on an empty stomach) blunts its glucose impact.

### Move — even a little, even slowly

This is one of the most powerful and underused tools. A 2025 study in *Scientific Reports* found that a **10-minute walk immediately after eating** lowered the post-meal blood sugar peak — to about 164 mg/dL versus about 182 mg/dL for those who rested [^27]. Both a 10-minute and a 30-minute walk beat sitting, but the short walk taken *right after* eating was uniquely good at shaving the peak [^27]. Resistance training, separately, improves insulin sensitivity over time. The message: you don’t need a gym session — you need to not sit still right after eating.

### Protect your sleep

Since sleep loss reliably ramps up cravings for high-sugar, high-fat food [^10], guarding sleep is craving management. Aim for the 7–9 hours adults generally need. Even modest improvements help: adding sleep has been shown to reduce appetite and the desire for sweets [^12]. If you’ve ever noticed you crave junk harder after a bad night, now you know it’s chemistry, not character.

### Tend to stress

Because cortisol drives sugar-seeking [^8], lowering chronic stress lowers a craving trigger. Mindfulness, slow breathing, and time in nature are all reasonable, low-risk tools with growing support as ways to take the edge off stress-driven eating. The goal isn’t to eliminate stress — it’s to give your body other ways to settle than reaching for sugar.

### Feed your gut well

Given the emerging gut-craving link [^13], a diverse, fiber-rich diet with fermented foods (yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut) is a sensible bet. Fiber diversity feeds a broader range of beneficial microbes. This is a “promising and low-risk” area rather than a guaranteed fix — but eating this way helps your whole body regardless.

### Functional and integrative options — with honest labels

- **Cinnamon.** Real but modest. Systematic reviews find cinnamon can lower fasting blood glucose and HbA1c in people with type 2 diabetes, though results vary a lot between studies and the ideal amount is unclear  [^28]. Think “small helper,” not “treatment.”
- **Berberine.** The most clinically interesting of the bunch. Meta-analyses find berberine lowers fasting glucose, HbA1c, and post-meal glucose, with effects comparable to some standard medications, working through an enzyme called AMPK [^29]. The caveats are real: most studies are small, short, and conducted in Chinese populations, and it interacts with medications. Interesting, not magic.
- **Chromium.** Weaker and inconsistent. Some meta-analyses show modest glucose improvements in people with diabetes;  a well-known earlier analysis found no effect on glucose or insulin in people *without* diabetes  [^30]. The craving and weight-loss claims are largely unsupported.
- **L-glutamine.** Here honesty matters most. The idea that L-glutamine reduces sugar cravings comes mainly from a popular book and clinical observations, not controlled trials. **No published randomized trial has tested L-glutamine with sugar-craving reduction as a defined outcome** [^31]. The small diabetes trials often cited measured blood sugar, not cravings, and even those results were inconclusive  [^31]. It’s biologically plausible and some practitioners swear by it — but the evidence base for the craving claim is essentially anecdotal, and that’s the truth readers deserve.

> ### 📌 KEY FACT BOX #5: The 10-Minute Walk
> 
> A 2025 trial in *Scientific Reports* found that walking for just **10 minutes immediately after** consuming glucose cut the blood sugar peak to about **164 mg/dL**, compared with about **182 mg/dL** when resting [^27]. Walking immediately after eating beat walking 30 minutes later for shaving the peak. Translation: the dishes can wait — take a lap around the block first.

> ### 📌 KEY FACT BOX #6: Berberine — The Most Interesting Functional Option
> 
> A meta-analysis pooling 46 studies and more than 4,000 people with type 2 diabetes found berberine **as effective as metformin** at lowering blood glucose, with slightly better results on HbA1c, fasting glucose, and post-meal glucose [^29]. Another meta-analysis found it reduced fasting glucose, HbA1c, and 2-hour glucose without raising the risk of low blood sugar [^29]. **Caveats:** studies are mostly small and short; berberine interacts with many medications and should never be combined with prescription blood-sugar or blood-thinning drugs without medical guidance.

## 7. When to Get Help — Not Just Change Your Diet

The approaches above are for generally healthy people. Some situations call for a professional in the loop. This isn’t fine print — it’s important.

- **Diagnosed diabetes or pre-diabetes.** Diet and movement are powerful here, but changes can shift your blood sugar and medication needs. Coordinate with your clinician before making big changes — especially before trying berberine or cinnamon, which can lower glucose further.
- **Taking blood-thinning medication.** Some supplements, including berberine and cinnamon (especially cassia cinnamon, which contains coumarin), can interact. Check first.
- **A history of disordered eating.** Framing foods as off-limits, or pursuing strict sugar reduction, can be a trigger for some people. If this is you, please work with a therapist or registered dietitian rather than going it alone.
- **Hypoglycemia.** If you’re prone to low blood sugar, cutting carbohydrates rapidly without supervision can cause problems. Go slowly and with guidance.
- **Cravings that feel obsessive or are tied to mood.** If cravings dominate your thoughts, or come with significant anxiety or depression, that’s worth a conversation with a physician or mental health professional. It’s not a personal-strength issue — it’s a health issue.
- **Pregnancy.** Blood sugar management in pregnancy (including gestational diabetes) needs clinical supervision. Don’t start supplements without your provider’s okay.

> ### 📌 KEY FACT BOX #7: See a Doctor, Not Just a New Diet, If…
> 
> - You have diabetes, pre-diabetes, or take medication for blood sugar or blood thinning
> - You’re pregnant or trying to become pregnant
> - You have a history of an eating disorder
> - Your cravings feel compulsive, distressing, or are paired with low mood or anxiety
> - You experience shakiness, dizziness, or other symptoms of low blood sugar
> 
> When in doubt, a registered dietitian or your physician can personalize this far better than any general guide.

## 8. A Note on the Bigger Picture

Step back for a moment. If you’ve ever felt that resisting sugar takes more effort than it should, you’re right — and it’s not because you’re weak. The modern food environment is deliberately engineered to produce cravings. Ultra-processed foods are designed by teams of scientists to hit the exact combinations of sweetness, fat, salt, and texture that override your body’s natural fullness signals. You are, quite literally, up against product design.

That reframes the whole conversation. The most important variables in your relationship with sugar aren’t about trying harder. They’re about your environment (what’s in your kitchen and your line of sight), your sleep, your stress, and your biology. Change those, and the cravings quiet down on their own. Keep a few easy swaps stocked, walk after meals, sleep enough, and the daily battle gets smaller.

And small, sustainable changes beat dramatic overhauls almost every time. A dramatic 30-day sugar purge that you can’t maintain does less for you than swapping one sugary drink for sparkling water, every day, for a year. Your taste buds adjust — foods you once found pleasant start to taste cloying, and naturally sweet things taste sweeter [^1].

Finally, a gentle, universal thought: there’s something steadying about treating your body as worth caring for — not out of guilt or punishment, but out of a kind of respect. You only get the one. Eating with a little more awareness — noticing when you’re actually hungry, savoring the treat you genuinely want, letting go of the one you don’t — isn’t deprivation. It’s paying attention. And attention, it turns out, is one of the kindest things you can offer yourself.
