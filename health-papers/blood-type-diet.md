If you've spent any time around wellness culture in the last few decades, you've probably heard some version of it: Type O's should eat like cave dwellers, heavy on meat and light on grains. Type A's do best as vegetarians. Type B's can handle dairy that would make everyone else miserable. Type AB's get to split the difference. The idea has a name — the blood type diet — and it has sold millions of books, launched a small industry of blood-type-specific supplements, and quietly shaped the eating habits of people who have never read a single page of the original research behind it.

Here's the thing worth knowing up front: there isn't much original research behind it. But there's a genuinely fascinating body of real science about what your blood type does mean for your health — some of it settled decades ago, some of it discovered during the COVID-19 pandemic. This piece walks through both. We'll look at where the blood type diet came from, why it's appealing, what actually happens when scientists put it to the test, and then spend real time on the part that gets skipped in most takedowns of this diet: the legitimate, well-documented ways your ABO blood type does shape your health. That part is more interesting than the diet ever was.

## The Idea and Where It Came From

The blood type diet was introduced to the world in 1996 by Peter D'Adamo, a naturopathic physician, in a book called *Eat Right 4 Your Type*.[^1] The book built on ideas his father had explored earlier and became a *New York Times* bestseller, eventually selling more than seven million copies and spawning a dozen follow-up titles on everything from arthritis to menopause, each one filtered through the same blood-type lens.

The core claim goes like this: each ABO blood type — O, A, B, and AB — evolved during a different chapter of human history, shaped by whatever our ancestors were eating at the time. Type O, the theory holds, is the oldest blood type, dating to a hunter-gatherer era, so people with it are supposed to thrive on high-protein, meat-heavy diets. Type A supposedly emerged as humans settled into farming communities, so A's are said to do best on a mostly vegetarian diet. Type B is linked to nomadic herding populations and paired with a dairy-friendly diet. Type AB, the rarest and most recently evolved type, gets a blend of the A and B recommendations.

Layered on top of this evolutionary story is a mechanism: D'Adamo proposed that eating foods containing lectins — proteins found throughout the plant kingdom — that are "wrong" for your blood type causes those lectins to clump your blood cells together, a process called agglutination, triggering inflammation and, over time, disease.

> **KEY FACT:** Type O is often called the "universal donor" blood type because red blood cells without A or B antigens can be given to patients of any blood type in an emergency. That's a real and clinically important fact about blood type — it just has nothing to do with what you should eat for dinner.

It's easy to see why this caught on. It offers something most diet advice doesn't: a story. Instead of generic advice to eat more vegetables, you get a personalized narrative connecting your body to your ancestors, wrapped around a test you may have already had done — your blood type is often printed on a card in your wallet or noted in your medical chart. That combination of identity, ancestry, and a seemingly objective biological marker is a powerful pitch. Decades later, despite everything researchers have since learned, the diet remains part of the cultural conversation about nutrition, and search interest in it never really goes away.

## The Actual Science of Lectins — and Where the Theory Breaks Down

To evaluate the blood type diet fairly, you have to start with lectins, because the whole theory rests on how they supposedly behave in your body.

Lectins are a large family of proteins, found concentrated in legumes, grains, nuts, and seeds, that share one defining trait: they bind to specific carbohydrate molecules.[^2] That carbohydrate-binding ability is exactly what makes them relevant to blood type in the first place, because the A and B antigens that define your blood type are themselves carbohydrate structures sitting on the surface of your red blood cells. In sufficient concentration, certain lectins genuinely can cause red blood cells to clump together — agglutinate — when mixed directly in a test tube. Raw lima beans and raw kidney beans are the classic examples: their lectins will visibly agglutinate blood in a laboratory dish, and some of that agglutination does show a preference for certain blood types over others.[^2] This is the seed of truth that the blood type diet theory grew from, and it's worth taking seriously rather than waving away.

Here's where the theory runs into trouble: what happens in a petri dish with concentrated, purified, raw lectin extract dropped directly onto blood cells is a completely different situation from what happens when you eat a serving of beans. Three things break the chain between "lectins can agglutinate blood in a lab" and "the food you eat is agglutinating your blood cells right now, based on your blood type."

First, cooking matters enormously. Most lectins are heat-sensitive, and normal cooking — boiling, baking, pressure-cooking — deactivates the large majority of the lectin activity in foods people actually eat.[^3] The dramatic lab demonstrations use raw legume extracts specifically because cooking destroys the effect researchers are trying to study.

Second, digestion matters. Lectins are proteins, and like other dietary proteins, they're substantially broken down by stomach acid and digestive enzymes before whatever remains could plausibly reach your bloodstream in a form still capable of binding intact blood-type antigens. The pathway from "lectin in your salad" to "lectin molecule intact and circulating in your blood, reactive with your specific ABO type" involves a lot of steps that the original theory simply skips over.

Third — and this is the detail that undoes the blood-type specificity of the whole idea — the lectins that do show any blood-type-related agglutination activity turn out to be the exception, not the rule. The large majority of lectins found in food react with red blood cells regardless of ABO type; only a small subset show any type-specific preference at all, and that subset is concentrated almost entirely in raw legumes that few people eat uncooked.[^2] A comprehensive review of the lectin literature commissioned as part of a formal assessment of the blood type diet found no controlled evidence that dietary lectins cause blood-type-specific agglutination inside the body under normal eating conditions.

> **KEY FACT:** The blood type diet's origin story also gets the history backward. D'Adamo's theory treats type O as humanity's oldest, original blood type. But phylogenetic analysis of the ABO gene across humans and closely related primates points the other way — blood group A appears to be the more ancestral type, with O emerging later through mutation.[^4] The evolutionary narrative that supposedly explains why each blood type needs a specific diet doesn't match the genetics it's built on.

None of this means lectins are irrelevant to health. Some people do notice digestive discomfort from foods high in raw or undercooked lectins, and that's a real, individual response worth paying attention to. What the science doesn't support is the specific claim that your ABO blood type determines which lectins are "compatible" with your body.

## What Happens When Researchers Actually Test the Diet

For years, the blood type diet existed almost entirely as a theory — plausible-sounding to a lay reader, but never actually tested against real health outcomes in real people grouped by blood type. That changed with a systematic review published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* in 2013, which set out to answer a very specific question: among people grouped by blood type, does following the diet matched to their type actually improve their health compared to not following it?[^5] The researchers searched the major medical databases and found 1,415 potentially relevant articles. After screening, exactly one study was rigorous enough to include in an analysis — and even that one didn't directly test the blood-type-diet hypothesis. The reviewers' conclusion was blunt: no evidence currently exists to validate the health claims associated with blood type diets, not because the claims had been tested and failed, but because almost nobody had tested them at all.

The following year, in 2014, researchers at the University of Toronto closed that gap directly. Published in *PLOS ONE*, the study analyzed data from close to 1,500 adults, genotyping each participant's actual ABO blood type and separately measuring how closely their existing diet matched each of the four "blood type" eating patterns.[^6] This design let the researchers ask the crucial question head-on: do the benefits of eating the "type A diet," say, show up specifically in people who actually have type A blood, the way the theory predicts?

> **KEY FACT:** The 2014 University of Toronto study found that following any of the four blood-type diets was linked to real improvements in cardiometabolic markers — better BMI, waist circumference, blood pressure, and triglycerides. The catch: those benefits appeared in everyone who followed a given eating pattern, completely independent of the person's actual ABO genotype.[^6] A person with type B blood got just as much benefit from the "Type A diet" as an actual type A person did.

That's the whole story of the blood type diet in a single finding. The diets themselves aren't harmful in outline — three of the four are built around largely whole, minimally processed foods, and eating that way tends to improve health markers for almost anyone, regardless of blood type. What the study found no support for is the specific, defining claim of the theory: that matching the diet to your blood type adds any measurable benefit beyond what you'd get from simply eating more whole foods. The researchers' own conclusion was direct — the findings do not support the blood-type-diet hypothesis, and the theory behind it is not valid as originally proposed.

This pattern — real benefit from the diet, zero connection to blood type — helps explain something that might otherwise seem confusing: why do so many people say they genuinely feel better after switching to their "blood type diet"? That experience is real, and it deserves a real explanation rather than dismissal. Several honest explanations fit the evidence. All four blood-type diets push people to cut way back on ultra-processed foods, added sugar, and refined carbohydrates — changes that reliably improve energy and digestion for most people no matter their genetics. The diets also introduce structure and food awareness, which on their own tend to reduce mindless eating and often lead to modest calorie reduction. And expectation effects are genuinely powerful in nutrition research; when people believe a change will make them feel better, and they're paying closer attention to their body, they often do feel better — a real, well-documented phenomenon, not something to wave away as "just placebo." None of this requires blood type to be doing anything at all. A separate line of research makes the same point from another angle: a 2023 randomized trial using identical twins — one twin assigned a vegan diet, the other an omnivore diet, holding genetics constant — found meaningful cardiometabolic improvements in the vegan-assigned twins within just eight weeks, driven by diet quality, not by any blood-type-related mechanism.[^7]

## Where Blood Type Genuinely Does Matter for Health

Here's the part of this story that tends to get lost, either buried under enthusiastic diet marketing on one side or dismissive debunking on the other: your ABO blood type is a real, biologically active marker connected to several health conditions. It just isn't connected to which foods you should eat. Understanding the real science here is more interesting than the diet ever was.

**Cardiovascular disease and clotting.** Blood type affects levels of a clotting protein called von Willebrand factor, and through it, Factor VIII. People with non-O blood types (A, B, and AB) tend to run higher levels of both, which translates into measurably different risk. A systematic review and meta-analysis of vascular disease pooled data across dozens of studies and found non-O blood types carry meaningfully higher risk of venous thromboembolism — blood clots in the veins — along with more modest associations with arterial conditions like heart attack and stroke.[^8] A separate meta-analysis focused specifically on venous thromboembolism, covering more than 10,000 cases, found non-O blood type nearly doubled the odds compared to type O, making it one of the most common inherited risk factors for this condition.[^9]

**Cancer risk.** Blood type A has been consistently linked to a modestly elevated risk of several cancers. A large genetic study and meta-analysis covering more than 33,000 gastric cancer cases across dozens of studies found blood group A carried about a 19% higher risk of stomach cancer compared with blood group O, with blood group AB also somewhat elevated.[^10] A separate case-control study of over 1,000 gastric cancer patients found a similar pattern and traced part of the mechanism: people with blood type A were significantly more likely to be infected with *Helicobacter pylori*, the bacterium responsible for most stomach ulcers and a major driver of gastric cancer.[^11] Pancreatic cancer shows a comparable pattern. A study combining data from Shanghai with a meta-analysis of the broader literature found blood type O carried a meaningfully lower pancreatic cancer risk than non-O types,[^12] a finding consistent with an earlier large cohort study that identified ABO blood type as one of the few reliable inherited risk markers for this notoriously hard-to-predict cancer.[^13]

> **KEY FACT:** People with blood type O appear to have roughly a 20 to 25% lower relative risk of pancreatic cancer compared with non-O blood types — one of a small handful of genetic markers researchers have reliably connected to this particular cancer.[^12]

**COVID-19.** This is the most recent chapter in ABO research, and it emerged fast. Within the first year of the pandemic, multiple research groups noticed that blood type A appeared over-represented among COVID-19 patients relative to the general population, while type O appeared under-represented. A large systematic review and meta-analysis pooling data across many studies confirmed the pattern held up under scrutiny: blood type A was associated with meaningfully higher odds of SARS-CoV-2 infection, and blood type O with modestly lower odds, though the relationship with disease severity and mortality proved less consistent across studies than the relationship with initial infection risk.[^14] Researchers have proposed that naturally occurring antibodies against the A antigen, present in people with type O or type B blood, may offer partial interference with how the virus attaches to cells — a plausible, though not fully confirmed, biological mechanism.

**Malaria resistance — a textbook case of evolution in action.** Of everything on this list, the malaria research is the one that most clearly demonstrates real, mechanism-backed evolutionary pressure on blood type, which makes it a useful point of contrast with the blood type diet's invented evolutionary story. Malaria parasites cause some of their most severe damage through a process called rosetting, where infected red blood cells stick to healthy ones, clumping together in ways that block small blood vessels and can trigger coma or death in severe cases. A study of Malian children found that blood group O was present in only about 21% of severe malaria cases, compared to roughly 44–45% of children with uncomplicated malaria or no infection at all — group O was associated with a 66% reduction in the odds of developing severe disease.[^15] The mechanism traces directly back to blood chemistry: red blood cells lacking the A and B antigens support less rosetting, so infected cells clump less and cause less vascular blockage. A later systematic review pooling data across more than 20 studies confirmed the pattern: blood groups A, B, and AB all carry increased odds of severe *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria compared with group O.[^16]

> **KEY FACT:** Malaria has likely helped keep blood type O common in regions where the disease has been endemic for millennia. It's one of the best-documented examples in human genetics of a disease actively shaping which blood types persist in a population — real evolutionary pressure, unlike the invented ancestral-diet story behind the blood type diet.

Taken together, these findings paint a coherent picture. Blood type is a real, biologically meaningful marker — for clotting risk, for certain cancers, for infectious disease susceptibility. What it is not is a nutrition-matching key. The traits that make blood type medically relevant (antigen structure, clotting factor levels, interactions with pathogens and parasites) operate through completely different biology than digestion and nutrient absorption, which is exactly why the diet's central premise doesn't hold up even as the broader science of blood type keeps turning up genuinely interesting results.

## Real Personalized Nutrition vs. Blood-Type Personalization

If the appeal of the blood type diet is personalization — the idea that your unique biology should shape what you eat — it's worth knowing that a real field of science is built around exactly that idea. It's called nutrigenomics, and unlike the blood type diet, it's grounded in identified genes with documented, mechanistic effects on how your body processes specific nutrients.

**Lactase persistence** is probably the best-known example. Most mammals, humans included, stop producing the enzyme lactase — needed to digest milk sugar — after weaning. But a genetic variant near the *LCT* gene allows some people to keep producing lactase into adulthood, letting them digest dairy without discomfort.[^17] This variant is common in populations with a long history of dairy farming, especially in Northern Europe, and rare in populations without that history — a genuine case of a specific ancestral diet shaping a specific gene, which is the very thing the blood type diet claims to explain but doesn't. Genetic testing can now identify which variant a person carries, and it directly predicts something concrete and testable: whether dairy is likely to cause digestive symptoms. Some research even links the lactase-persistence genotype to how strongly dairy intake affects body weight, adding another layer of individualized relevance.[^18]

**APOE genotype** offers a second example, this one tied to cardiovascular health. The *APOE* gene comes in a few common variants, and people carrying the ε4 variant show a notably stronger rise in LDL cholesterol in response to dietary saturated fat than people with other variants. A randomized trial testing genotype-based dietary advice found that ε4 carriers who received advice tailored to their genotype showed different — generally more favorable — changes in blood cholesterol than those given generic advice.[^19] For someone who carries this variant, knowing it is a genuinely useful piece of information for deciding how strictly to manage saturated fat intake.

**CYP1A2 and caffeine metabolism** shows how the same food can carry different risk for different people, depending on a single gene. The *CYP1A2* gene controls how quickly your body metabolizes caffeine. People with the "fast" variant clear caffeine efficiently; people with the "slow" variant metabolize it much more gradually, leaving it in their system longer. A large case-control study found that slow metabolizers who drank four or more cups of coffee a day had a meaningfully increased risk of nonfatal heart attack, while fast metabolizers drinking the same amount showed no such increase — and if anything, a slightly reduced risk.[^20] Same cup of coffee, same amount consumed, genuinely different health consequence, depending on one identifiable gene.

**FTO and weight regulation** rounds out the picture. Variants in the *FTO* gene are linked to differences in appetite regulation and body weight across large populations. Interestingly, research on this gene has found that its effects aren't fixed: one clinical trial found that people carrying the FTO risk variant lost meaningfully more body fat on a higher-protein weight-loss diet than on a lower-protein one, while people without the risk variant showed little difference between the two diets.[^21] That's real gene-by-diet personalization — a specific, testable interaction between genotype and macronutrient composition.

None of these four genes has anything to do with ABO blood type. That's the point. Real personalized nutrition exists, it's mechanistically grounded, and none of it maps onto the four blood groups the blood type diet is built around. If personalization is genuinely what you're after, these are the places where the science actually supports it.

## Contraindications and Safety Concerns

Because three of the four blood-type diets are built around largely whole foods, most people who try the diet won't come to serious harm. But a few specific pieces of the plan deserve direct, clear caution rather than vague hedging.

The Type O protocol's emphasis on high-protein, meat-heavy eating is worth flagging specifically. Major health bodies classify processed meat as a known contributor to colorectal cancer risk, with each daily 50-gram serving associated with roughly an 18% increase in risk, and classify red meat consumption as a probable contributor to colorectal, pancreatic, and prostate cancer.[^22] A dietary pattern that specifically encourages heavy red and processed meat intake, matched to a blood type rather than to a person's actual cardiovascular or cancer risk profile, can push some people toward more of these foods than would otherwise be recommended.

> **KEY FACT:** Each additional 50-gram daily serving of processed meat — about two slices of bacon or one hot dog — is associated with roughly an 18% higher risk of colorectal cancer, according to the international scientific body responsible for classifying cancer risk factors.[^22]

The Type O and Type B protocols' elimination of dairy and, in some versions, most grains, carries a real risk of unnecessary nutrient gaps — calcium, vitamin D, fiber, and B vitamins are the ones worth watching most closely — with no established offsetting benefit tied to blood type itself. For someone managing kidney disease, gout, or existing cardiovascular disease, "eat more meat because you're Type O" is advice that could work directly against their actual medical needs; blood type should never override a real diagnosis when it comes to protein or dietary fat recommendations. There's also a more general concern worth naming plainly: any diet built around rigid categorical rules — foods you can never eat because of a fixed trait like blood type — can, for some people, feed into patterns of restrictive or disordered eating. If a food plan starts to feel less like guidance and more like a source of anxiety or rigid rule-following, that's worth discussing with a professional.

One more practical note: ABO blood typing done outside a clinical setting — some retail or at-home kits — carries its own accuracy limitations. Before making any decisions based on a blood type result, it's worth confirming that result through a clinical lab, particularly given how central it would be to a plan like this one.

## Addressing the Elephant in the Room: Meat-Heavy "Type O" Advice vs. Plant-Forward Eating Patterns

It's worth sitting with a real tension here directly, because many readers come to this topic from communities and personal traditions that have long emphasized eating mostly plants. The Type O protocol's push toward heavy meat consumption runs in the opposite direction from some of the strongest, most consistent long-term evidence in all of nutrition science.

The European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC)-Oxford study, one of the largest and longest-running cohort studies ever conducted on vegetarian and non-vegetarian diets, has tracked tens of thousands of participants for decades. Its most recent major analysis, following participants for 18 years, found that vegetarians had meaningfully lower rates of ischemic heart disease than comparable meat eaters, even after adjusting for smoking, alcohol use, physical activity, and socioeconomic factors.[^23] This finding holds regardless of blood type — the study wasn't looking at ABO groups at all, because ABO status simply isn't part of the mechanism. What predicts better cardiovascular outcomes here is the overall pattern of what's on the plate, not a person's blood type.

This doesn't mean everyone needs to eliminate meat entirely, and it doesn't mean the blood type diet is uniquely dangerous compared to other high-meat eating patterns. But it does mean that "eat more red meat because your blood type says so" sits uneasily next to some of the best long-term evidence available on cardiovascular and cancer outcomes. If you're drawn to more plant-forward eating for reasons connected to your values, background, or family history, that instinct lines up well with a substantial body of long-term epidemiological research — considerably more robust, on this specific question, than anything behind blood-type matching.

## A Path Forward: What Actually Works

If part of what draws people to the blood type diet is the appeal of personalization, structure, and a more mindful relationship with food, the good news is that all three are available without needing your ABO type to have anything to do with it.

A whole-food eating pattern — built around vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and lean protein sources, with limited processed and red meat — has decades of consistent research behind it, none of which requires blood-type matching to work. If you want genuine personalization, the nutrigenomics research above shows there are real, gene-based tools available: knowing your lactase-persistence status, your APOE genotype, or your caffeine-metabolism profile can meaningfully shape specific choices, in ways blood type simply can't. And if you want ongoing, individualized guidance, working with a registered dietitian who can track your actual lab markers — cholesterol, blood sugar, inflammatory markers — and adjust your plan based on your real, measured response over time offers far more precision than a diet built around one static genetic trait.

Practical steps you can act on immediately, regardless of blood type: build meals around vegetables and whole plant foods first; choose whole grains and legumes over refined carbohydrates; limit processed meat and keep red meat intake moderate; and pay attention to how specific foods actually make you feel, adjusting based on your own observed response rather than a blood-type chart. That last piece, ironically, captures the one part of the blood type diet's appeal that holds up completely: paying closer attention to how your body responds to food is genuinely valuable. It just doesn't need your blood type to guide it.

## When to See a Clinician or Registered Dietitian

A few situations call for professional guidance before making any significant dietary change, blood-type-based or otherwise:

- Before starting any elimination-style diet if you're managing kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or have a history of disordered eating.
- If you notice signs that could point to a nutrient deficiency — ongoing fatigue, unusual hair loss, unexplained bruising, or persistent digestive changes.
- If you're considering a supplement marketed around blood type or "lectin-blocking" claims, especially one that makes disease-specific promises.
- If restrictive eating rules of any kind start to feel like a source of anxiety rather than a helpful structure.

A registered dietitian or your physician can help sort out what's actually driving how you feel — whether that's a nutrient gap, an unrelated health condition, or simply a dietary pattern that needs adjusting — far more precisely than a blood-type chart ever could.

## A Closing Thought

There's something very human about wanting a simple, personal answer to a complicated question like "what should I eat?" A blood type is easy to know, easy to explain, and connects to something as elemental as your ancestry — no wonder the story took hold. The real science turns out to be a little less tidy and a lot more interesting: your blood type doesn't hold the key to your diet, but it does carry real information about your risk for clotting, certain cancers, and infectious disease — a quiet, ongoing conversation between your genes and your health that has nothing to do with what's on your dinner plate tonight.
