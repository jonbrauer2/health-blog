## When normal tests don't match how you feel

You finish a small lunch and within an hour your waistband digs in. By dinner, you look like you swallowed a balloon. You've cut out gluten, then dairy, then nightshades. You've tried probiotics, kombucha, and a parade of teas. Your bloodwork looks fine. Your colonoscopy was clean. Your doctor — kind but busy — uses the phrase "probably just IBS" and hands you a prescription that helps a little, sometimes.

If that scene feels familiar, you are not imagining things, and you are not alone. A growing body of gastroenterology research has been circling a condition called **small intestinal bacterial overgrowth**, or SIBO, that may explain a meaningful share of this kind of suffering.[^1][^2] SIBO is more widely discussed today than a decade ago, but it is also more debated. Some specialists believe it is under-recognized; others worry it is over-diagnosed and over-treated.[^3][^4] Both can be partly right.

This handout is for the person caught in the middle of that debate while their pants don't fit. It walks through what SIBO is, what we know about why it happens, how it is diagnosed, the trade-offs of the available treatments, and — critically — what you can reasonably do about it without overreaching. Think of it as a long conversation with a friend who happens to have read the literature and wants you to leave the conversation more informed, less afraid, and clearer about your next step.

---

## What SIBO actually is

**SIBO is an abnormal increase in the number — and often the type — of bacteria living in the small intestine.**[^5] The small intestine is the long, narrow tube where most of what you eat is digested and absorbed. It is supposed to be relatively bacteria-sparse compared with the colon. That matters because the small intestine is where enzymes break down food, where vitamins and minerals cross into the bloodstream, and where the immune system maintains a delicate truce with the microbes passing through.[^6] When too many bacteria set up shop in this stretch of tubing, they ferment food before you can absorb it, produce gas where there should be quiet, and can damage the thin lining responsible for nutrient transport.[^7]

> **Key Fact #1 — Wrong neighborhood, wrong numbers.** A healthy small intestine typically contains fewer than 10³ (one thousand) bacterial organisms per milliliter of fluid. A healthy colon contains roughly 10¹¹–10¹² (hundreds of billions to trillions) per milliliter. When colonic-type bacteria — or simply too many of any kind — settle into the small intestine, even modest overgrowth can produce real digestive trouble.[^7][^8]

SIBO is not the same thing as colonic dysbiosis, the term often used for an imbalanced large-intestine microbiome. The colon is *supposed* to be a microbial city. The small intestine is supposed to be a quiet suburb. SIBO is what happens when the city sprawls upstream.[^9]

Researchers now recognize at least three flavors of overgrowth, and they behave differently. **Hydrogen-dominant SIBO** is driven by bacteria that ferment carbohydrates into hydrogen gas and tends to produce bloating and often diarrhea.[^10] **Methane-dominant overgrowth**, which the 2020 American College of Gastroenterology guideline reclassified as **intestinal methanogen overgrowth (IMO)**, is driven not by bacteria at all but by archaea — chiefly *Methanobrevibacter smithii* — that consume hydrogen and produce methane. Methane physically slows intestinal transit and is strongly linked to constipation.[^9][^11] **Hydrogen sulfide overgrowth (sometimes called ISO)** involves bacteria like *Fusobacterium* and *Desulfovibrio* that produce a rotten-egg-smelling gas associated with diarrhea-predominant symptoms.[^10][^12]

Prevalence numbers are slippery because they depend on how you test. Pooled estimates suggest SIBO affects roughly **5–8% of healthy adults** but appears in **about 31% of people with irritable bowel syndrome** when meta-analyzed across modern case-control studies — and rises as high as **38% or more** when broader breath-test criteria are used.[^13][^14] In short: a real condition, more common in suffering populations, with edges that researchers are still arguing about.

## What causes SIBO

SIBO is rarely about one thing. It is what happens when several of the body's normal defenses against bacterial overgrowth fail at the same time.[^15]

The most consequential of those defenses is a piece of digestive plumbing called the **migrating motor complex**.

> **Key Fact #2 — The housekeeping wave.** Between meals, your small intestine performs a slow, deliberate sweep called the migrating motor complex (MMC). Roughly every 90 to 120 minutes of fasting, a brief storm of strong contractions — Phase III — travels from the stomach down the small bowel, pushing residual food and bacteria toward the colon. It is, quite literally, the gut's self-cleaning cycle, and motilin is its conductor.[^16][^17] Eating switches the MMC off. Grazing all day or snacking every couple of hours means the housekeeping wave rarely runs, and bacteria get a longer rent-free stay than nature intended.[^16]

Disruption of the MMC is a central theme in SIBO. So is anything that slows the gut, removes its acid barrier, or scrambles its anatomy.

**Well-established contributors** include impaired motility from diabetes, hypothyroidism, scleroderma, Parkinson's disease, and opioid use;[^18][^19][^20][^21] **low stomach acid** from chronic proton-pump inhibitor (PPI) use, autoimmune gastritis, or simple aging — meta-analyses show PPI users have roughly twice the odds of SIBO, with risk climbing the longer the medication is taken;[^22][^23] **structural problems** such as small-bowel diverticula, surgical adhesions, blind loops, strictures, and ileocecal valve dysfunction; and **post-surgical anatomy** such as Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, where SIBO prevalence climbs from about 29% short-term to over 50% by three years out.[^15][^24] Aging and immune deficiency also raise risk.[^15]

**Post-infectious mechanisms** deserve their own paragraph. Work from Mark Pimentel's group at Cedars-Sinai has built a compelling case that a meaningful share of IBS — and the SIBO associated with it — begins with an ordinary case of food poisoning. The bacterial toxin **cytolethal distending toxin B (CdtB)**, produced by *Campylobacter*, *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, and certain *E. coli*, can trigger antibodies that cross-react with **vinculin**, a protein in the interstitial cells of Cajal — the pacemaker cells that drive the MMC.[^25][^26] Damage to those cells leaves the housekeeping wave weaker, and overgrowth follows. A validation study in more than 2,300 IBS-D patients showed these antibodies were significantly elevated compared with inflammatory bowel disease, celiac, or healthy controls.[^27] The science is real and the mechanism is plausible, though the commercial blood test built on it has modest sensitivity and remains debated.[^4]

**Emerging or functionally recognized contributors** include chronic psychological stress, which inhibits the MMC through corticotropin-releasing factor signaling and sympathetic activation;[^28][^29][^30] frequent snacking, which prevents the MMC from cycling;[^16] antibiotic-induced loss of colonization resistance;[^31] and diets very low in fiber, which experimentally shift the small-intestinal microbial community toward dysbiosis and impair barrier function.[^32] None of these are *the* cause of SIBO on their own. They are accelerants on an already-piled wood stack.

## How SIBO is diagnosed

There is no perfect test for SIBO. There are useful tests, with real limitations, used by clinicians who reasonably disagree about how to interpret them.[^4][^33]

**Breath testing** is the most common approach. After fasting and a preparation diet, you drink a sugar solution — usually lactulose or glucose — and breathe into a collection device every fifteen minutes for two to three hours. Bacteria in the small intestine ferment the sugar and release hydrogen and methane, which diffuse into the blood and out through the lungs. The 2017 **North American Consensus** set the most widely used thresholds: a rise of **≥20 ppm in hydrogen above baseline by 90 minutes** is considered positive for SIBO, and a **methane level ≥10 ppm at any point** is considered positive for IMO.[^34] The 2020 ACG guideline added formal recognition of IMO and endorsed breath testing as the preferred non-invasive approach.[^9]

Breath testing is honest about being imperfect. A pooled meta-analysis put glucose breath-test sensitivity at about **54%** and specificity at **83%**, with lactulose performing worse on both measures.[^35] One scintigraphy study found that nearly half of "positive" glucose breath tests were actually caused by fast small-bowel transit moving the sugar into the colon early, where colonic bacteria — not small-intestinal ones — produced the gas spike.[^36] That helps explain why the European multisociety guideline and a 2024 critical appraisal from the European and American neurogastroenterology societies have urged caution about over-reliance on breath testing, especially the lactulose version.[^33][^37]

> **Key Fact #3 — Same breath, different verdicts.** Two labs running the same patient's breath sample can reach different conclusions because thresholds, substrate choice, test duration, and pre-test prep are not standardized worldwide. The North American Consensus uses a 90-minute hydrogen cutoff; European guidance favors 60 minutes. Pooled sensitivity for the lactulose breath test is only around **42%**.[^34][^35][^37] A "negative" test does not always mean you are clear; a "positive" test does not always mean overgrowth is the whole story.

**Small-intestinal aspirate and culture** is the traditional gold standard: an endoscope reaches into the duodenum, fluid is sampled, and bacteria are counted. The threshold itself has been re-debated. The older cutoff was ≥10⁵ colony-forming units per milliliter, derived from post-surgical patients; modern work — including a 2024 high-throughput sequencing study — supports the newer **≥10³ CFU/mL threshold** the ACG now endorses.[^9][^38][^39] Aspirate is rarely used in routine practice because it is invasive, expensive, and prone to contamination from passing through the mouth and stomach.[^9]

**Hydrogen sulfide SIBO** is a particular blind spot. Standard two-gas breath testing does not measure H₂S. A newer three-gas device adds H₂S detection, and a 2022 study linked elevated breath H₂S with diarrhea-predominant IBS and specific microbial signatures.[^10][^12] The technology is promising but still maturing, and most of the validation work comes from a single research group with commercial ties — independent replication is limited.[^33]

That brings us to the honest middle of the diagnostic conversation. Mainstream gastroenterology — represented by the AGA and the joint European-American neurogastroenterology critique — argues that SIBO is overdiagnosed, that breath tests too often mistake transit time for overgrowth, and that the label is being attached to too many vague complaints.[^3][^33] Functional and integrative medicine practitioners argue that strict criteria miss real cases of overgrowth in patients who improve dramatically with targeted treatment. Both groups are reading the same data and drawing different lines. A reasonable clinician acknowledges both concerns, considers risk factors carefully, interprets tests in context, and does not treat a number on a printout as the whole patient.[^33]

## Symptoms — and the conditions SIBO tends to travel with

The hallmark symptoms are **bloating that worsens through the day, visible abdominal distension, excessive gas, abdominal pain, and altered bowel habits** — diarrhea in hydrogen-dominant SIBO, constipation in IMO, often alternating in mixed pictures.[^9][^40] Nausea, early fullness, and reflux are common.

Less recognized but real are the **nutritional consequences** of more advanced disease. Bacteria in the small intestine can deconjugate bile acids and impair fat absorption, leading to deficiencies of fat-soluble vitamins A, D, and E. They can also bind vitamin B12 and produce inactive analogues that compete with the real molecule, producing low B12 in the presence of normal or even elevated folate — a classic SIBO laboratory pattern that can cause fatigue, brain fog, neuropathy, and megaloblastic anemia.[^41][^42] Iron deficiency is frequent.[^41] Interestingly, B12 deficiency appears less often in methane-dominant disease, possibly because the underlying microbial community is different.[^43]

SIBO travels in revealing company. It is documented at substantially higher prevalence in **irritable bowel syndrome** (about 31%, with odds roughly four times those of controls);[^13][^14] in **systemic sclerosis** (about a third of patients, with odds roughly ten times controls);[^44] in **hypothyroidism**, particularly Hashimoto's (over half in one landmark study);[^18] in **Parkinson's disease** (about 46% in a recent meta-analysis);[^21] in **diabetes and gastroparesis** (around 29–41%);[^19][^45] and in **fibromyalgia**, where a small but striking early study found abnormal lactulose breath tests in every fibromyalgia patient assessed.[^46]

> **Key Fact #4 — The rosacea connection.** In a landmark 2008 controlled study, **46% of rosacea patients tested positive for SIBO compared with 5% of matched controls**. After ten days of rifaximin, **71% had complete clearing of their facial rosacea**, with remission maintained at nine months and confirmed in a 3-year follow-up.[^47][^48] Subsequent dermatology research has reinforced the link, particularly for the papulopustular subtype.[^49] The mechanism is still being worked out — likely involving bacterial metabolites, immune signaling, and systemic inflammation — but for a person who has been treating their face for years without addressing their gut, it is a connection worth knowing.

The chicken-and-egg question follows naturally. Does SIBO cause these conditions, or do these conditions predispose to SIBO? In most cases, the answer is *both, in a loop*. Hypothyroidism slows the gut, which permits overgrowth, which can in turn worsen fatigue and inflammation. Scleroderma damages the smooth muscle that drives the MMC, allowing bacteria to accumulate. Parkinson's affects the same enteric nervous system that controls motility. Treating SIBO does not cure these underlying conditions, but in many people it meaningfully reduces digestive misery and may improve the underlying condition's course.[^11][^20]

## Conventional treatment

The mainstream approach to confirmed SIBO is reasonably clear, even if the evidence base is uneven.

**Rifaximin** is the most studied antibiotic for hydrogen-dominant SIBO. It is minimally absorbed — under 0.4% reaches the bloodstream — so it works mostly in the gut lumen and has a remarkably favorable safety profile.[^50] The typical regimen is **550 mg three times daily for 14 days**.[^9] A 32-study meta-analysis put overall eradication at about **70.8%**, with symptomatic improvement near 68% and adverse events under 5%; a more conservative RCT-only meta-analysis put breath-test normalization closer to **49.5%**.[^51][^52] Reasonable working estimate: roughly half to two-thirds of patients will respond to a first course.

For **methane-dominant overgrowth (IMO)**, rifaximin alone is much less effective. Combination therapy — typically **rifaximin plus neomycin** — performs substantially better, with one study showing methane eradication in **87% of the combination group versus 33% with neomycin and 28% with rifaximin alone**.[^53] A randomized trial of methane-positive constipation-IBS confirmed the combination's superiority for constipation, straining, and bloating.[^54] The ACG guideline endorses this approach.[^9] Metronidazole is sometimes substituted for neomycin.

> **Key Fact #5 — Coming back is the rule, not the exception.** In the most-cited recurrence study, **27.5% of successfully treated patients had recurrent SIBO by 6 months and 43.7% by 9 months**, with older age, prior appendectomy, and chronic PPI use as predictors.[^55] This is the single most important reason that long-term success depends on addressing *why* the overgrowth happened in the first place — motility, anatomy, acid, lifestyle — rather than just clearing it once and hoping for the best.

Encouragingly, repeat courses of rifaximin appear safe. A large IBS-D trial showed responders could be successfully retreated, and a microbiology substudy found **no clinically meaningful emergence of antibiotic resistance** across multiple courses.[^56][^57] Rifaximin also shows an unusual "eubiotic" profile in studies of the colonic microbiome, sometimes *increasing* beneficial *Bifidobacterium*, *Lactobacillus*, and *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii* rather than depleting them.[^58][^59]

The **elemental diet** is the other primary medical treatment. An elemental formula is a pre-digested liquid containing free amino acids, simple sugars, and fats, designed to be absorbed in the first portion of the small intestine before bacteria can reach it — effectively starving them. The foundational study put 93 patients on Vivonex for two weeks and found **80% normalization of the breath test at day 15, climbing to 85% by day 21**.[^60] A 2025 trial of a more palatable formulation reported similar results.[^61] The drawback is human: drinking only flavored powder for two to three weeks is genuinely hard, socially isolating, and expensive. It is best considered when antibiotics have failed or are contraindicated.

**Prokinetics** are used after primary treatment to reduce relapse by supporting the MMC. Options include the 5-HT4 agonist **prucalopride**, **low-dose naltrexone** (which has a small retrospective evidence base in SIBO), low-dose **erythromycin**, and historically tegaserod.[^62][^63] None has a robust placebo-controlled trial specifically in SIBO recurrence, but the underlying physiology is sound and clinical experience is broadly favorable.

## Lifestyle, nutrition, and functional approaches

This is the section where most of the day-to-day work of living with SIBO actually happens.

**Dietary approaches.** The **low-FODMAP diet**, developed by the Monash University group, restricts fermentable carbohydrates that feed gas-producing bacteria. It has the strongest dietary evidence base for symptom relief in IBS — and by extension SIBO — with controlled feeding trials showing about **70% symptomatic benefit** and meta-analyses confirming significant reductions in IBS symptom severity scores.[^64][^65][^66] Important nuance: low-FODMAP does not *treat* the overgrowth; it reduces the fuel. Long-term strict restriction can reduce beneficial *Bifidobacterium* and calcium intake and is associated with disordered-eating risk, which is why it is meant to be a 4-to-8-week elimination followed by a structured reintroduction phase.[^67][^68]

The **Specific Carbohydrate Diet (SCD)** restricts disaccharides and complex polysaccharides; the **SIBO Bi-Phasic Diet** developed by Nirala Jacobi blends low-FODMAP and SCD principles into a staged protocol commonly used alongside antimicrobials in integrative practice. Both have meaningful clinical followings but limited randomized-trial data specifically in SIBO; SCD has been studied mostly in pediatric Crohn's disease, where it produces clinical and mucosal improvement.[^69]

**Meal spacing and the MMC.** The mechanistic case for leaving roughly **four to five hours between meals**, including an overnight fast of twelve hours or so, is straightforward: the MMC runs only during fasting.[^16][^17] There are no randomized trials testing meal spacing as a SIBO intervention, but the cost of trying is essentially zero and the physiologic logic is solid.

**Herbal antimicrobials.** The anchor study is **Chedid and colleagues (2014)**, a retrospective chart review at Johns Hopkins that compared a four-week course of two herbal protocols — Dysbiocide plus FC Cidal, or Candibactin-AR plus Candibactin-BR (containing concentrated oregano, thyme, berberine, goldenseal, Chinese skullcap, sage, and lemon balm) — against rifaximin for hydrogen-dominant SIBO. Breath-test normalization was **46% in the herbal group versus 34% in the rifaximin group**, statistically equivalent.[^70] Among rifaximin non-responders, 57% later normalized on herbals.[^70]

Individual agents have varying evidence. **Berberine** has good pharmacology data — very low bioavailability keeps it in the gut, where it modulates the microbiome — but it inhibits multiple CYP enzymes and has real drug-interaction risk.[^71] **Oregano oil** (carvacrol and thymol) has broad antimicrobial activity in vitro and small human data for parasitic clearance. **Allicin** from garlic is often favored clinically for methane-dominant disease because of its activity against methanogens, though human SIBO trials are lacking. **Neem**, **wormwood**, and **thyme** appear in many protocols with mostly in-vitro support.

A 2025 Bayesian network meta-analysis of 30 randomized trials and 12 interventions found berberine ranked highest for eradication and rifaximin plus a prokinetic ranked best in patients with overlapping functional GI disorders.[^72] None of this means herbal antimicrobials are casual; some have pregnancy contraindications, several have meaningful drug interactions, and product quality varies widely. Self-prescribing without clinician oversight is not advised.[^71]

**Probiotics — the live controversy.** Practitioners genuinely disagree about whether probiotics help or hurt in active SIBO.

> **Key Fact #6 — Why thoughtful clinicians disagree about probiotics.** A 2017 meta-analysis of 18 studies found that probiotics achieved a SIBO decontamination rate of about **62.8%** and significantly reduced breath hydrogen by roughly 36 ppm, with reduced abdominal pain — but they did not appear to prevent SIBO from developing.[^73] Yet a 2018 study documented that patients with brain fog, gas, and bloating who were taking *Lactobacillus*-based probiotics had higher rates of SIBO and **D-lactic acidosis**; stopping probiotics and treating with antibiotics resolved brain fog in 85%.[^74] Critics pointed out the small uncontrolled sample, undefined probiotic strains, and missing follow-up data.[^75] The honest summary is that probiotics may help some patients with SIBO and harm others, that strain matters enormously, and that *Saccharomyces boulardii* (a yeast, not a bacterium, that cannot produce D-lactate) and certain soil-based spore-forming species are often better tolerated.[^76]

**Nutritional repletion.** Because SIBO can drive deficiencies in B12, iron, and the fat-soluble vitamins, screening through standard bloodwork is appropriate when symptoms or risk factors suggest malabsorption.[^41][^42] Supplementation is reasonable when it is guided by lab values, not by guesswork. High-dose, untested supplementation as a strategy is not a substitute for a workup, and recommending specific doses falls outside what non-clinicians should be doing.

**Stress, sleep, and the gut-brain axis.** The vagus nerve is the body's main parasympathetic highway between brain and gut, and it directly modulates motility, secretion, and inflammation.[^77][^78] Chronic stress activates corticotropin-releasing factor signaling and the HPA axis, which delays gastric emptying, suppresses the MMC, and increases small-intestinal permeability — even in healthy people exposed to acute psychological stress in controlled studies.[^28][^79]

> **Key Fact #7 — Vagal tone is something you can train.** Slow diaphragmatic breathing — typically around six breaths per minute — measurably increases heart-rate variability, a proxy for vagal tone, in randomized trials.[^80][^81] Gut-directed hypnotherapy has one of the strongest evidence bases of any IBS therapy, with **response rates of 70–80% sustained for years** and meta-analyses showing efficacy comparable to the low-FODMAP diet.[^82][^83][^84] Mindfulness-based interventions have shown significant IBS symptom reduction in randomized trials.[^85] The connection between calming the nervous system and improving gut function is not a metaphor; it is mechanistic.

Sleep matters more than most patients realize. Circadian disruption alters microbial composition, weakens the morning high-amplitude contractions that drive bowel movement, and impairs gut barrier function — a chrono-microbiota-motility axis that connects shift work and chronic insomnia to digestive dysfunction.[^86]

## When to see a clinician

Self-management has real limits. Please involve a clinician promptly if any of the following apply: **unexplained weight loss**, **blood in the stool**, **persistent severe abdominal pain**, **vomiting that won't settle**, signs of nutritional deficiency such as numbness or tingling that could indicate B12 deficiency, iron-deficiency anemia, or visible weakness; **symptoms that do not improve** after a reasonable trial of dietary and lifestyle change; suspected underlying conditions such as thyroid disease, autoimmune disease, scleroderma, or diabetes with poor gastric emptying; **any pediatric case**; pregnancy or planned pregnancy; and any situation where you are considering **antibiotic or intensive herbal treatment**, which requires clinician oversight for dosing, interactions, and follow-up.[^9][^71]

A specific flag: if you have been on multiple supplements for months and are still symptomatic, that is not a reason to add a fourth or fifth product. It is a reason to step back and get a proper evaluation. Persistent symptoms that have resisted self-management often turn out to have a treatable underlying cause that no supplement protocol can address.

## What a health coach can appropriately do

The line between education and treatment matters, both legally and for the client's safety.

A health coach can provide **general education** about gut health, the microbiome, the migrating motor complex, and the well-documented effect of stress, sleep, and meal timing on digestion. A coach can **support sustainable lifestyle change**: helping clients establish a realistic meal-spacing rhythm, build a wind-down routine that protects sleep, integrate breathwork or mindfulness practice, and stay consistent with the diet a clinician has recommended. A coach can **help clients prepare for medical visits**, organizing symptom timelines and questions so the limited time with a clinician is well spent. A coach can **recognize the warning signs above and refer confidently**.

A coach should not **diagnose SIBO** or tell a client they have SIBO based on symptoms. A coach should not **interpret breath-test results** or order tests. A coach should not **prescribe or recommend specific antimicrobial protocols**, herbal or pharmaceutical, or assign specific supplement dosages as treatment. A coach should not **adjust medications**, including PPIs or thyroid hormone, no matter how plausible the rationale may seem.

These boundaries are not about timidity. They are about doing the most good with the role you actually have, while leaving the diagnostic and prescribing decisions to people whose training and licensure put them on the hook for those decisions.

## Honoring the body, honoring the suffering

There is a quiet dignity in attending carefully to the body — slowing down to eat, pausing between meals, protecting sleep, breathing on purpose. These small acts are physiologically sound and they are also expressions of a deeper truth: that the body is not a machine to be optimized but a living whole, and that how we treat it tends to matter.

Chronic, invisible illness — the kind that doesn't show up on standard tests, the kind that makes a person feel dismissed in their own appointments — is real. Bloating that no one else can see is still bloating. Fatigue that doesn't track with last night's sleep is still fatigue. People living with SIBO often carry an extra burden of being doubted by the systems that should be helping them. That experience deserves compassion, not dismissal, and a careful, thorough workup rather than a shrug. The integration of body, mind, and spirit is not a slogan; for many people, it is the only frame that does justice to what they are actually living through.

---

## Key takeaways

SIBO is an abnormal expansion of bacterial — or, in the case of IMO, archaeal — populations in the small intestine, where they don't belong in those numbers. It comes in hydrogen-dominant, methane-dominant, and hydrogen-sulfide subtypes that present and respond to treatment differently. It is genuinely more common in people with IBS, rosacea, hypothyroidism, scleroderma, Parkinson's disease, diabetes, and post-surgical anatomy, and it is genuinely over-diagnosed in some practice settings. Breath testing is the most-used diagnostic tool, with meaningful limitations that reasonable clinicians weigh differently. Conventional treatment relies on rifaximin (sometimes with neomycin for methane), elemental diet, and prokinetics; herbal antimicrobials have an evidence-equivalent retrospective base for hydrogen-dominant disease. Recurrence rates of 40–50% within a year are why diet, meal spacing, stress management, sleep, and treating underlying conditions matter so much. If something feels seriously wrong — weight loss, blood, intractable pain, neurological symptoms — see a clinician. If you suspect SIBO, do not self-prescribe; get an evaluation. And if you are a coach, you have important and limited work to do: educate, support, refer.
