Bedwetting is common, it is not anyone's fault, and most children grow out of it. The approaches with the strongest support are calm reassurance, sensible habits during the day, bedwetting alarms and the medicine desmopressin, while home remedies such as apple cider vinegar have never been studied for bedwetting and carry real safety concerns.

## TL;DR

- According to a 2020 Cochrane review, bedwetting (enuresis) affects 15–20% of five-year-olds and 0.5–2% of adults. It runs strongly in families and comes mainly from body processes (how much urine is made at night, how much the bladder holds, and how easily the brain wakes), not from laziness or defiance.
- Enuresis alarms and desmopressin have the strongest evidence. Alarms give more lasting results, and desmopressin works faster but often wears off once it is stopped. Treating constipation and snoring or sleep apnea can help some children a lot, and punishment makes things worse.
- Apple cider vinegar for bedwetting is **not studied**: no clinical trials exist, and it can damage teeth and the esophagus, so it is not recommended as a treatment. No home remedy has been shown to "cure" enuresis. Changes to diet and fluids have **moderate support** as helpful extras for some children, but they do not prevent enuresis for everyone.

## It's 2 a.m., and You're Not Alone

It's 2 a.m. You hear a small voice in the dark — "Mom? Dad? I had an accident." You pull off wet sheets, find dry pajamas, and try to get everyone back to sleep. Maybe your child is dreading a sleepover next month. Maybe an older sibling made a joke at breakfast that stung. Or maybe you're a teenager or adult reading this privately, tired of hiding something you've never talked about.

Here is the most important thing to know first: bedwetting is common, it is not caused by laziness or defiance, and it usually gets better. The 2020 Cochrane review of alarm treatment puts the usual figure at 15–20% of five-year-olds[^1]. That same review estimates that, until the teenage years, about 14% of children who wet the bed become dry on their own each year, even without any treatment[^1]. Bedwetting mostly comes from how the body works at night, not from a child's choices or character[^3].

> **Key Fact:** About 14% of children who wet the bed stop on their own each year until adolescence, even without treatment, according to the 2020 Cochrane review.[^1]

This handout explains why bedwetting happens, which approaches have the best support, how to judge popular home remedies, and when to see a clinician. It is written for parents and caregivers, for teens and adults living with enuresis, and for the friends, mentors, and community leaders who support them.

## What Enuresis Is (and Isn't)

### The official definition

The International Children's Continence Society (ICCS) defines enuresis as intermittent incontinence (wetting) while sleeping, in a child aged five or older[^2]. Before age five, nighttime wetting is considered a normal part of development. "Intermittent" means it happens in separate episodes, as opposed to constant dribbling. Constant leaking can point to a different problem that needs a medical check[^2].

### Primary vs. secondary

- **Primary enuresis** means the child has never been reliably dry at night. This is by far the most common type[^2].
- **Secondary enuresis** means wetting has started again after the child was dry for at least six months[^2]. New wetting after a long dry stretch deserves a check-up, because it can sometimes signal a urinary tract infection, constipation, diabetes, or significant stress[^8].

### Monosymptomatic vs. non-monosymptomatic

- **Monosymptomatic** enuresis means wetting only at night, with no daytime bladder problems[^2].
- **Non-monosymptomatic** enuresis means nighttime wetting *plus* daytime symptoms such as urgency (sudden, strong need to go), going very often, daytime wetting, or trouble emptying the bladder[^2].

Why does this matter? Children with daytime symptoms usually need the daytime bladder problem (and often constipation) treated first. The standard nighttime treatments tend to work less well until that happens[^3].

### How common is it?

Bedwetting becomes less common with each year of age. The 2020 Cochrane review gives commonly quoted rates of 15–20% of five-year-olds, 7% of seven-year-olds, 5% of 10-year-olds, 2–3% of teenagers, and 0.5–2% of adults[^1], and other sources put the figure at about 5–10% of seven-year-olds[^9]. A large U.S. national survey of 8- to 11-year-olds found that 4.45% had enuresis, with boys (6.21%) affected more than twice as often as girls (2.51%)[^7]. Among children who wet only at night, boys outnumber girls by about 1.5 to 2 times[^3].

For some people, it doesn't fully go away. A Dutch survey of more than 13,000 adults aged 18–64 found that 0.5% had wet the bed in the previous four weeks[^5]. Cochrane reviewers estimate that up to 2% of young adults are affected[^6]. A Korean national study found a rate of 2.6% among people aged 16–40 when counting any episode in six months[^10]. These numbers mean that thousands of teens and adults in any large city are quietly dealing with the same thing.

> **Key Fact:** In a Dutch study, half of the men and 35% of the women who wet the bed had never talked with a health professional about it, and only 30% believed it was treatable.[^5]

## Why It Happens: The Three-Part Picture

Researchers now describe bedwetting as coming from a mix of three main factors[^3][^11]. A child may have one, two, or all three.

### 1. Too much urine at night (nocturnal polyuria)

Most people make less urine at night, partly because the body releases more of a hormone called vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone) during sleep. Many children who wet the bed don't get this normal nighttime rise, so their kidneys keep producing daytime amounts of urine[^9][^11]. Some studies also show that these children pass more salt (sodium) and urea — a waste product from breaking down protein — at night, which pulls extra water into the urine[^12][^13]. This is one reason researchers are interested in what and when children eat in the evening.

### 2. A bladder that holds less at night

Some children's bladders hold less than expected overnight or contract too early, a pattern related to an "overactive" bladder[^3]. These children are more likely to also have daytime urgency or frequency.

### 3. Difficulty waking up

A full bladder sends a signal to the brain. In most people, that signal either quiets the bladder or wakes them up. In children with enuresis, the brain often doesn't respond[^11]. Parents often describe their child as "a deep sleeper," but researchers see it differently: the problem is not simply deep sleep, but a brain that doesn't wake up in response to the bladder's signal[^11]. That is why shouting, shaking, or scolding in the morning doesn't help — the child genuinely didn't notice.

### It runs in families

Genes play a big role. When neither parent wet the bed as a child, about 15% of children do. That jumps to about 44% if one parent did, and about 77% if both did[^9]. Identical twins share enuresis far more often than would be expected by chance[^9]. For many parents, this is a relief: if you or your partner wet the bed as a child, your child isn't doing anything "wrong" — they may simply have inherited the pattern.

> **Key Fact:** If one parent wet the bed as a child, their child's chance of bedwetting is about 44%; if both parents did, it rises to about 77%.[^9]

### Conditions that often travel with bedwetting

- **Constipation.** A full rectum sits right behind the bladder and can press on it and irritate it. In a landmark 1997 study of 234 children with chronic constipation (older, but still widely cited), relieving the constipation stopped nighttime wetting in 63% of those who had it and daytime wetting in 89%[^14]. Constipation is easy to miss, because children may still have daily bowel movements.
- **ADHD.** Children with ADHD are about twice as likely to have enuresis, and children with enuresis are about twice as likely to have ADHD[^15]. The U.S. national survey found an odds ratio of 2.88 for ADHD among children with enuresis[^7].
- **Snoring and sleep apnea.** Children whose breathing is blocked during sleep (often by large tonsils and adenoids) wet the bed more often. A 2018 systematic review of 18 studies found that after surgery to remove tonsils and adenoids, bedwetting improved in more than 60% of children with sleep-disordered breathing, with a pooled complete resolution rate of 51% (43% when only lower-bias studies of children aged five and older were counted). The authors noted, though, that the studies were mostly uncontrolled and of low quality[^16]. A secondary analysis of a randomized trial of 393 children aged 5–9 found that bedwetting fell by 11.0% after surgery but by only 0.5% with "watchful waiting," and that the odds of bedwetting in the waiting group were about twice as high (odds ratio 2.0)[^17].
- **Developmental differences and other conditions.** Enuresis is also more common in children with developmental delays and neurodevelopmental conditions[^9].

### Causes of new wetting (secondary enuresis)

When a previously dry child starts wetting again, clinicians look for[^8][^9]:

- Urinary tract infection (UTI)
- Diabetes (type 1 or type 2), which makes the body produce a lot of urine
- Constipation
- Emotional stress or major life changes — a new sibling, a move, divorce, a death in the family, or trouble at school

Rarely, sudden new wetting can be one sign that a child has been harmed or abused[^19]. This is not a reason to panic or assume the worst — stress and constipation are far more common causes. It is simply one more reason why a sudden change deserves a calm, private conversation with a clinician.

## The Emotional Side

Bedwetting is harmless to the body, but it can weigh heavily on the heart. Children worry about sleepovers, camps, and being found out. They feel different from their friends. Teasing from siblings or classmates can hurt deeply[^19].

The good news is that self-esteem tends to recover when wetting improves. In a Swedish study, children with enuresis and daytime wetting had lower self-esteem than their peers before treatment. After six months of treatment, their self-esteem matched the comparison group, and children who became fully dry scored highest[^18]. This matters for the "just wait" debate: waiting is often reasonable, but a child's emotional wellbeing is a real reason to act.

### Punishment makes things worse

Research is clear here. In a study of 65 children with enuresis, those who were punished by their parents had more wet nights per week and more depressive symptoms than those who were not[^20]. In a Brazilian study of 87 children with enuresis, every child had experienced some form of punishment, and physical punishment was more likely when a parent had wet the bed as a child — yet most of those parents were still intolerant of it in their own children[^21]. The UK's national guideline goes further: it advises professionals to consider child maltreatment if parents keep punishing a child for bedwetting after being told it is involuntary[^19][^22].

Put plainly: punishment, shaming, teasing, and forcing a child to wash sheets *as a punishment* are harmful. They add shame and stress without helping the child get dry.

That's different from calm, age-appropriate participation. A seven-year-old who helps strip the bed and put the sheets in the hamper — while you say, "Thanks for helping, we're a team" — is learning responsibility and gaining some control. The difference is in how the child experiences it: helping, not blame.

> **Key Fact:** Children punished for bedwetting had more wet nights and more depressive symptoms than children who were not punished.[^20]

## Getting Checked: What a Clinician Usually Does

Most visits for bedwetting are simple. A clinician will usually[^3][^22]:

- **Take a history:** How often does wetting happen? Were there ever dry periods? Any daytime symptoms, constipation, snoring, or excessive thirst? Any family history?
- **Ask for a bladder diary** (also called a frequency-volume chart): a record of drinks, bathroom trips, and how much urine is passed for a couple of days, plus a note of wet and dry nights. It helps show which of the "three parts" may be involved.
- **Do a physical exam,** including a look at the lower back and a check of leg strength and reflexes.
- **Test a urine sample** to check for infection or sugar (a sign of diabetes).

According to ICCS guidance, most children with enuresis do not need blood tests, X-rays, or special bladder tests unless there are warning signs such as problems passing urine or excessive thirst[^3].

## Treatment Options: What the Research Shows

### Watchful waiting and reassurance

For a young child who isn't bothered, simply waiting is a reasonable choice. Many children become dry on their own[^4], and Canadian pediatric guidance says that for simple bedwetting, education and reassurance are the foundation of care[^8]. Waiting works best alongside good daytime habits and a no-blame attitude at home.

### Basic bladder and lifestyle advice ("urotherapy")

Urotherapy means teaching healthy bladder habits: drinking well during the day, using the toilet regularly, going before bed, and managing constipation. A Cochrane review found that simple behavioral methods like these can help some children, though they are less effective than alarms or desmopressin. Because they are simple and have no side effects, they're usually worth trying first[^23]. It's fair to note that one small randomized trial found basic bladder advice alone did not reduce wetting[^24] — which is why advice is usually a starting point rather than the whole plan.

### Enuresis alarms

An enuresis alarm is a small moisture sensor worn in the underwear or placed on a bed pad. It sounds when the first drops of urine appear. Over weeks, the brain learns to respond to a full bladder — either by waking up or by holding on[^1].

A 2020 Cochrane review of 74 trials involving 5,983 children found that alarms may be more effective than no treatment. Children using alarms were more likely to reach 14 dry nights in a row and more likely to stay dry after treatment ended[^1]. Alarms probably cause fewer side effects than desmopressin and may leave slightly more children dry after treatment stops[^1]. However, the reviewers rated much of the evidence as low quality and called for better trials[^1]. Older Cochrane work found that about half of children stay dry after alarm treatment[^25].

The reality check: alarms take commitment. It often takes several weeks before progress shows, and a parent usually needs to get up to help the child fully wake, use the toilet, and reset the alarm. UK guidance suggests checking for early signs of progress within four weeks and continuing until the child has had two weeks of dry nights in a row[^22]. Reported downsides include the alarm failing to wake the child, false alarms, waking siblings, discomfort, and frightening the child[^1]. Alarms tend to work best for motivated children and families who can handle some broken sleep for a while.

> **Key Fact:** Alarms have the best track record for lasting dryness — but success usually takes several weeks of consistent nightly use and family teamwork.[^1][^22]

### Desmopressin

Desmopressin is a lab-made version of vasopressin, the hormone that reduces nighttime urine production[^6]. It works quickly, often within days, so it can be useful for camps, sleepovers, and trips[^3]. It reduces wet nights while children are taking it, but many children start wetting again after stopping[^6][^25].

The main safety concern is **hyponatremia**, or water intoxication — a dangerous drop in blood sodium that can happen if someone drinks too much while the medicine is working. In 2007, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration removed bedwetting from the approved uses of desmopressin *nasal spray* after reviewing 61 post-marketing reports of seizures linked to low sodium, two of them fatal; 41% of the cases were in patients younger than 17 using the nasal spray[^26]. Tablet and melt forms are still used for bedwetting, but labeling says fluids should be limited from one hour before until eight hours after the dose, and the medicine should be paused during illnesses with fever, vomiting, or diarrhea[^26]. Dosing must come from your clinician.

### Anticholinergic medicines

These medicines relax an overactive bladder. They are mostly used together with desmopressin for children whose bladders are part of the problem, especially those with daytime symptoms[^3]. A common side effect is constipation, which can make wetting worse — so bowel habits need watching[^3].

### Imipramine and related medicines (tricyclics)

These older antidepressants can reduce wetting, but most children relapse after stopping, and an overdose can be fatal, mainly because of effects on the heart[^25]. Today they are later-line options, used by specialists after other treatments have been tried[^3][^22]. If they are ever prescribed in your home, store them locked away from children.

### Combination and step-by-step plans

Treatments are often combined or used in stages. For example, adding an alarm to desmopressin may increase the number of children who become dry and stay dry compared with desmopressin alone[^1]. For stubborn cases, clinicians look again at constipation, daytime bladder habits, and sleep before adding more medicines[^3].

### Teens and adults

If you are a teen or adult who still wets the bed — or if it starts in adulthood — it deserves a proper medical evaluation, not just a quick fix. In adults, wetting has been linked with urgency, sleep problems, and emotional strain, including lower self-esteem[^10]. A clinician will look at possible causes such as sleep apnea, bladder or prostate conditions, diabetes, neurological conditions, and medicines or alcohol that increase urine production[^10]. Help exists, and you deserve it.

## Everyday Habits: Food, Fluids, and Routines

These are the steps families most often ask about. Each one is rated on an evidence spectrum: **well-established**, **moderate**, **emerging**, **limited or mixed**, **not studied**, or **not recommended**.

### Fluid timing — *moderate support (mainly guideline consensus)*

The goal is to *shift* when a child drinks, not to cut back on how much. Encourage plenty of drinks in the morning and early afternoon, moderate amounts after school, and smaller amounts in the last hour or two before bed[^22]. UK guidance stresses that good daily fluid intake matters, and that problems with drinking too much or too little should be sorted out before starting other treatments[^22]. This fits with "drink lots of water": children still need plenty — just earlier. Harshly limiting fluids can backfire by irritating the bladder, making urine more concentrated, and adding to constipation.

### Caffeine and bladder irritants — *limited evidence; reasonable caution*

UK guidance advises avoiding caffeine-based drinks (cola, tea, coffee, energy drinks)[^22]. This is based mostly on expert consensus and what caffeine does to urine production, not on trials in children who wet the bed. Chocolate and fizzy drinks are often listed too, but they have been studied even less. Cutting evening caffeine is low-risk and sensible.

### Evening salt and protein — *emerging*

Because some children with nocturnal polyuria pass extra sodium and urea at night[^12][^13], researchers suspect that salty or high-protein evening meals could add to nighttime urine. This makes sense biologically, but large trials testing lower-salt evening meals in children haven't been done yet. Choosing less-processed, lower-salt dinners is healthy anyway.

### Constipation care — *moderate support; one of the strongest diet-related links*

Treating constipation can end wetting for some children[^14], and guidelines recommend checking for it in every child with enuresis[^3][^22]. Helpful habits include fiber-rich whole plant foods (fruits, vegetables, beans, whole grains), good daytime fluids, and relaxed, unhurried toilet time after meals with feet supported on a stool. If constipation continues, a clinician can advise on further treatment.

### Regular daytime toilet trips and "double voiding" — *limited to moderate support*

Using the toilet regularly during the day (roughly every two to three hours) and right before bed is standard advice in urotherapy[^3][^22]. "Double voiding" — going, waiting a minute, then trying again — can help children who don't empty fully. It is harmless and worth trying.

### Sleep — *emerging*

Regular bedtimes and enough sleep support overall health. Loud snoring, pauses in breathing, mouth-breathing, or very restless sleep are worth mentioning to a clinician, because treating sleep apnea can help with bedwetting[^16][^17].

### Healthy weight — *limited*

Extra weight is linked to snoring and sleep apnea, and some clinics notice that children with more weight respond more slowly to treatment. Research specifically on weight and bedwetting is limited, so focus on whole-family habits rather than a child's size.

### Cow's milk and elimination diets — *limited or mixed (mostly older research)*

Some older studies looked at whether food sensitivities, especially cow's milk, play a role. The Cochrane review of complementary and miscellaneous treatments judged the diet findings unreliable[^27]. Cutting out major food groups can leave growing children short on calcium, protein, and calories. Any elimination diet for a child should be planned with a clinician or registered dietitian.

### Vitamin D, magnesium, and omega-3s — *emerging for vitamin D; limited for omega-3; not studied for magnesium*

Several recent observational studies found lower vitamin D levels in children with enuresis. For example, a Turkish study of 307 children found that vitamin D deficiency was linked with about three times higher odds of bedwetting[^28]. A small Iranian randomized, placebo-controlled trial found improvement with vitamin D, and less with omega-3[^29]. A 2026 Chinese randomized trial found that adding vitamin D to alarm therapy in children with low vitamin D levels modestly improved results[^30]. These findings are promising, but they come from single centers, and a link is not the same as a cause. No clinical studies were identified for magnesium in enuresis. Talk with your clinician about checking vitamin D levels rather than starting supplements on your own.

### Lifting or waking at night — *limited for long-term dryness*

"Lifting" means carrying or walking a sleepy child to the toilet before you go to bed. UK guidance says neither lifting nor waking, whether at set times or at random, promotes long-term dryness, though it can be a practical short-term way to reduce wet beds[^22]. Alarm-based waking is different: the alarm is triggered by the child's own bladder, which helps the brain learn[^1].

### Reward systems — *moderate support (as part of a plan)*

Reward *effort*, not dry nights. A child can't directly control whether they stay dry, so charts that reward only dry nights can make children feel like failures[^22]. Instead, give stickers for drinking well early in the day, using the toilet before bed, helping change sheets, or using the alarm properly[^22].

## Integrative and Complementary Approaches

Many families explore complementary options, and it makes sense to look at them honestly. The main summary is a 2011 Cochrane review (still the most recent Cochrane look at this topic), which found weak evidence for hypnosis, psychotherapy, acupuncture, chiropractic care, and medicinal herbs. Each came from single small trials, some with questionable methods[^27].

- **Acupuncture and laser acupuncture — *emerging, low-quality evidence.*** In the Cochrane review, one trial found acupuncture worked better than sham (fake) acupuncture[^27]. A 2022 meta-analysis of 13 trials (890 children) found benefits for laser acupuncture, moxibustion, and acupoint injection, but rated the evidence "very low" quality. Laser acupuncture and desmopressin showed no clear difference[^31]. In 2018, the UK's guideline review judged acupuncture evidence too small-scale to change recommendations[^32].
- **Chiropractic care — *limited.*** A single small trial found active adjustment beat sham adjustment[^27]. No larger confirming trials were identified.
- **Hypnotherapy and guided imagery — *limited.*** One trial found hypnosis did better than imipramine after treatment ended[^27], but comparisons with alarms are very uncertain[^1]. Hypnosis and guided imagery are low-risk and may help a child feel calmer and more in control.
- **Parasacral TENS — *emerging.*** This uses gentle electrical stimulation through skin pads on the lower back. Most research has been in children with daytime bladder problems. Small trials in children with nighttime-only wetting show mixed results — three found fewer wet nights, one found no benefit[^33] — and UK reviewers considered the evidence too limited to recommend it[^32].
- **Traditional Chinese herbal formulas — *limited, with safety cautions.*** One trial found an herbal formula outperformed desmopressin[^27], but it was small. Herbal products are not regulated like medicines in many countries. Quality, ingredients, and contamination can vary, and herbs can interact with other medicines. Tell your clinician about anything your child takes.
- **Homeopathy — *not studied.*** The Cochrane reviewers found no randomized trials of homeopathy for bedwetting[^27]. This means it hasn't been properly tested — which is different from being tested and found not to work.
- **Pelvic floor and bladder training programs — *limited to moderate, mainly for daytime symptoms.*** These are part of urotherapy and are most useful for children with daytime bladder problems[^3].

### Why stories and studies sometimes disagree

A friend says, "We tried X and it worked in two weeks!" They may be completely sincere. But because about 14% of children become dry on their own each year until adolescence, according to the 2020 Cochrane review[^1], some improvement will follow *any* approach — conventional or alternative. Families also tend to start something new when wetting is at its worst, and bad stretches naturally tend to ease afterward (researchers call this "regression toward the mean"). Extra attention, calmer routines, and quietly drinking less in the evening can help too. This applies equally to an uncontrolled report about an alarm, a medicine, a supplement, or a remedy. That's why controlled trials — which compare one group with another — matter so much.

## Looking Closely at a Popular Claim

*"Enuresis (or bedwetting) can be cured with home remedies such as apple cider vinegar. Diet changes can also prevent nocturnal enuresis."*

This claim is widely shared online. Let's look at each part.

### Part 1: Apple cider vinegar

**What research exists?** A search of PubMed, the main medical research database, found no clinical studies — no trials, case series, or case reports — testing apple cider vinegar (ACV) for bedwetting. The Cochrane review of complementary treatments did not include any ACV studies either[^27]. The honest rating is **not studied**. That is not the same as "studied and shown not to work" — it simply hasn't been tested.

**The reasons people give.** Common explanations are that ACV changes urine acidity, "balances" the body, or calms the bladder. These ideas haven't been tested in bedwetting, and they don't clearly match any of the three known causes (nighttime urine production, bladder capacity, and waking). It's hard to see how a small amount of vinegar would change vasopressin release or the brain's response to a full bladder.

**Why some families see success.** The stories are understandable. Children become dry on their own at about 14% a year until adolescence, according to the 2020 Cochrane review[^1]. Families often start a remedy at the worst point, so improvement afterward is likely anyway. A new bedtime ritual brings attention and calm. And if ACV replaces a larger evening drink, the child may simply be drinking less before bed.

**Safety concerns.** Vinegar is acidic:
- **Teeth:** In lab studies, apple cider vinegar softened human tooth enamel[^34], and other vinegars eroded enamel over hours of contact[^35]. Frequent use can weaken children's teeth.
- **Throat and esophagus:** A 15-year-old who drank a vinegar beverage daily without enough dilution developed ulcers along his esophagus and vomited blood[^36]. ACV tablets have also caused esophageal injury when one lodged in the throat[^37].
- **Potassium:** Long-term heavy vinegar intake was linked in an adult case report to low potassium and bone thinning[^38].
- **Children are at higher risk** because of their smaller size and developing teeth.

**The biggest risk may be delay.** If a family relies on home remedies, a treatable cause — especially of *new* wetting — can be missed. Secondary enuresis can signal diabetes, infection, or constipation[^8]. Excessive thirst and frequent urination need prompt medical attention, not a kitchen remedy.

### Part 2: The word "cured"

No home remedy has been shown to cure enuresis. The accurate way to put it is that most children outgrow bedwetting, and certain approaches — alarms, medicines, treating constipation, treating sleep apnea — can speed up or support that process[^1][^3][^4].

### Part 3: "Diet changes can prevent nocturnal enuresis"

This part is partly true. Diet and fluid changes *can* help — sometimes a lot — especially for children with constipation[^14] or high nighttime urine production[^12][^13]. Useful steps include shifting drinks earlier in the day, avoiding evening caffeine, going easier on salt at dinner, and eating fiber-rich foods. But there is no evidence that diet can *prevent* enuresis in general. Genetics[^9], bladder capacity, and the brain's arousal response[^11] are mostly outside the reach of food choices.

### Confidence ratings

| Part of the claim | Rating |
|---|---|
| Apple cider vinegar treats bedwetting | **Not studied**, with safety cautions — not recommended as a treatment |
| Home remedies "cure" enuresis | **Not supported** |
| Diet and fluid changes help | **Moderate support** as helpful extras for some children — not prevention for all |

> **Key Fact:** No clinical studies of apple cider vinegar for bedwetting were identified — and vinegar has caused tooth erosion and esophageal injury in case reports.[^34][^36]

## Questions Families Often Wrestle With

### "Just wait" or "treat early"?

Both can be right. Waiting is reasonable for younger children (around ages 5–6) who aren't upset about it[^8]. Treatment makes sense when the child is bothered, is missing out on sleepovers or camps, or is around age 7 or older and motivated to try[^22]. UK guidance suggests basing the decision on the child's age, how often wetting happens, and the child's and family's motivation[^22]. Because self-esteem often improves when wetting improves[^18], a child's distress is a good enough reason to act.

### Diapers and pull-ups

Pull-ups protect sleep and dignity, especially for younger children or on trips. UK guidance suggests trying nights without pull-ups for children who are dry during the day, using waterproof mattress covers and absorbent bed pads instead[^22]. Before starting an alarm, pull-ups need to go, because the alarm has to detect wetness quickly.

### Fluid restriction

Timing matters more than total amount. Healthy daytime drinking supports the bladder and bowels[^22]. The exception is a clinician's instructions around desmopressin, where limiting evening fluids is a safety rule[^26].

### "Isn't it psychological?"

For many years, bedwetting was blamed on emotional problems. Today, it is understood to be mostly physical — about urine production, bladder function, and waking — with a strong genetic component[^3][^9]. Stress can still trigger *new* wetting in a previously dry child[^8], and bedwetting itself can cause emotional distress[^18].

### Natural and whole-food approaches

Many health-conscious families value whole-food eating, early bedtimes, time outdoors, and traditional water-based practices (hydrotherapy). Whole plant foods and good hydration support regular bowels, which clearly matters[^14]. Consistent, adequate sleep is good for everyone. For hydrotherapy methods, no clinical research on bedwetting was identified — they are **not studied**, not disproven. These habits can fit comfortably alongside the approaches with stronger support.

### Worries about medicine

It's natural to be cautious about medicines. Desmopressin is generally well tolerated when fluid rules are followed, but low sodium is a real risk, and relapse after stopping is common[^1][^6][^26]. Not treating has costs too: ongoing low self-esteem, avoiding social events, and stress on the family[^18][^19]. The right choice depends on each child and family.

### Following the money

Financial interests exist across the board. Alarms and medicines are sold commercially: the lead author of the 2020 Cochrane alarm review is one of the inventors of an enuresis alarm, and her hospital has received pharmaceutical funding for enuresis education materials[^1]. An earlier ICCS guideline disclosed authors' financial ties to a desmopressin manufacturer[^39]. Supplement and "natural cure" products are also marketed for profit, often with no requirement to show they work. Look at the quality of the evidence, whoever is selling.

## Safety Cautions at a Glance

- **Desmopressin:** Follow fluid limits around the dose. Pause it during vomiting, diarrhea, or fever. Use extra caution with conditions or medicines that raise the risk of low sodium. The nasal spray is not approved for bedwetting in the U.S.[^26]
- **Tricyclics (such as imipramine):** Risk of heart problems and dangerous overdose. Lock them away[^25].
- **Anticholinergics:** Can cause constipation, which may worsen wetting[^3].
- **Acidic home remedies (vinegar):** Can erode teeth and injure the throat and esophagus[^34][^36].
- **Elimination diets:** Risk of poor nutrition in growing children. Plan them only with professional guidance[^27].
- **Herbal products:** Quality varies, and they can interact with other medicines. Tell your clinician.
- **Fluid restriction:** Never restrict fluids in a way that leads to dehydration, especially in hot weather or during sports. Shift the timing instead[^22].

## When to See a Clinician

Seek care if you notice any of the following:

- **Any new wetting after six or more months of being dry** (secondary enuresis)
- **Excessive thirst, frequent urination, weight loss, or tiredness** — possible diabetes; seek care promptly
- **Pain or burning when urinating, fever, or cloudy or foul-smelling urine** — possible infection
- **Daytime wetting, sudden urgency, or a weak or interrupted urine stream**
- **Constipation or soiling**
- **Loud snoring, pauses in breathing, or very restless sleep**
- **Back abnormalities** (such as a dimple, tuft of hair, or lump on the lower back), **leg weakness, or changes in walking**
- **Wetting past age 7, or wetting that causes real distress at any age**
- **Any bedwetting in a teen or adult** without a clear childhood history
- **Behavioral changes or any concern about a child's safety**

These warning signs are based on international and national guidance[^3][^8][^22].

## For Friends, Mentors, and Community Leaders

If you're supporting a family — as a friend, grandparent, teacher, youth leader, camp counselor, or community wellness volunteer — you can make a real difference.

**Helpful roles:** Sharing accurate information, offering emotional support and encouragement, helping families set up daytime drinking and toilet routines, and gently encouraging a visit to a clinician.

**Leave to clinicians:** Diagnosing causes, recommending or adjusting medicines, suggesting supplements, and designing elimination diets.

**Words that help:**
- "Lots of kids deal with this, and it isn't your fault."
- "Your body is still learning — it's not something you're doing wrong."
- "It might be worth talking with your doctor about options. There are some that work really well."
- To a parent: "You're handling this with so much patience. That really matters."

**Camps, retreats, and sleepovers:**
- Quietly tell one trusted leader ahead of time. Never share it with the group.
- Pack pull-ups or absorbent underwear, a sleeping bag liner, and spare clothes in an opaque bag.
- Plan a private way to handle wet bedding — for example, the leader wakes the child a little before the others, or a discreet laundry bag is available.
- Ask the family whether short-term desmopressin has been discussed with their clinician for the trip[^3].

## Grace for the Journey

Whatever your beliefs, many families find comfort in a few simple truths. A child's worth has nothing to do with dry sheets. Home can be a place of safety, where accidents are met with patience instead of shame. Grace — giving kindness that isn't earned — fits bedwetting perfectly, because the child isn't choosing it. And like many hard seasons, this one passes. Patience, hope, and gentle words at 2 a.m. can shape how a child feels about themselves long after the nights are dry.

## Tonight You Can…

1. **Say it out loud:** "This isn't your fault, and we'll figure it out together."
2. **Move drinks earlier:** More in the morning and afternoon, less in the last hour or two before bed.
3. **Skip evening caffeine:** No cola, iced tea, or energy drinks after lunch.
4. **Build a bedtime routine:** Use the toilet as part of getting ready, then again right before lights-out.
5. **Watch for constipation:** Offer fiber-rich foods and relaxed toilet time with feet supported.
6. **Make cleanup easy:** A waterproof mattress cover, a spare set of sheets, and pajamas within reach.
7. **Reward effort, not dryness:** A sticker for drinking early or helping with sheets.
8. **Book a check-up** if any warning signs apply — or simply if you'd like to talk about options.

Most children who wet the bed will one day wake up dry, and many will get there sooner with the right support. Until then, your patience is one of the most powerful tools you have. Dry nights are ahead.
