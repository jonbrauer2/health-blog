## Introduction

Imagine being told that one of the most powerful “prescriptions” for your mental health isn’t a pill at all — it’s what’s on your plate, when you go to bed, how often you move your body, and who sits beside you at dinner. For most of the last century, depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia were treated almost exclusively as problems to be medicated. Medication remains essential and, for many people, life-saving. But a growing body of high-quality science now shows that the brain is an organ deeply embedded in the body and its daily rhythms — and that lifestyle is not a soft add-on but a genuine lever on brain biology.

This guide takes an integrated approach. The core principles of healthy living — nourishing food, restorative sleep, regular movement, clean surroundings, calming the nervous system, and meaningful connection — apply broadly across mental health conditions. Where the differences matter (and they sometimes matter a great deal), you’ll find clearly marked callout boxes. A fasting protocol that helps one person may destabilize another. A supplement that eases depression may be dangerous alongside a prescription. The aim is to be honest about both the promise and the limits of what we know, and to leave you with concrete things you *can* do.

> **A note on language and intent:** This handout uses person-first language (a “person living with bipolar disorder,” not “a bipolar”). It is written to empower, not to replace your clinical team. Nothing here should be used to stop or change psychiatric medication without medical supervision.

## Key Facts

- **Exercise works.** A 2024 systematic review and network meta-analysis of 218 studies (495 arms, 14,170 participants) concluded that walking or jogging, yoga, and strength training are more effective than other exercises for depression, “particularly when intense.”[^1]
- **Diet can move the needle.** In the landmark SMILES randomized controlled trial (n=67), a modified Mediterranean diet produced a large effect on depressive symptoms (Cohen’s d = −1.16); remission was reached by 32.3% of the dietary group versus 8.0% of controls.[^2]
- **Heritability is high but not destiny.** Twin studies estimate heritability around 80% for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, and roughly 35% for major depression — yet identical twins are frequently *discordant*, proving environment and lifestyle matter.[^3]
- **Omega-3s help, in the right form.** EPA-predominant fish oil (≥60% EPA, ~1–2 g EPA/day) reduces depressive symptoms; DHA-predominant formulas do not.[^4]
- **A ketogenic diet shows early promise for serious mental illness.** In a 2024 Stanford single-arm pilot trial, participants with schizophrenia improved an average of 32% on the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale, and 69% of bipolar participants showed clinically meaningful improvement (>1 point on Clinical Global Impression).[^5]
- **Fasting can be dangerous in bipolar disorder.** Ramadan fasting increased relapse risk 2.77-fold in people with bipolar disorder (95% CI 1.23–6.25), with a 33.3% relapse rate among fasters versus 15.3% among non-fasters.[^6]
- **St. John’s Wort works for mild–moderate depression but is risky.** It can match standard antidepressants for mild-to-moderate depression yet can cause life-threatening serotonin syndrome when combined with SSRIs.[^7]

## Understanding the Conditions

### Depression: More Than One Road to the Same Place

Major depressive disorder is not a single thing. Clinically it shows up as persistent low mood, loss of pleasure (anhedonia), changes in sleep and appetite, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and sometimes thoughts of death or suicide. But the *origins* differ from person to person, and this matters for treatment.

- **Situational (reactive) depression** arises in response to loss, trauma, chronic stress, or isolation. Here, psychological and social interventions — therapy, connection, restored routine, purposeful activity — are often central.
- **Metabolic and biological depression** is driven by underlying physiological disturbance: inflammation, insulin resistance, nutritional deficiency, thyroid dysfunction, disrupted circadian rhythm, or the side effects of medications. This is the form most responsive to lifestyle and nutritional medicine.

Most real-world depression is a blend. The practical lesson: look for and correct the biological contributors *while* addressing the psychological and social ones.

### Bipolar Disorder: The Rhythm Disorder

Bipolar disorder involves episodes of depression alternating with periods of mania or hypomania — states of elevated or irritable mood, reduced need for sleep, racing thoughts, grandiosity, and impulsivity. Bipolar I includes full manic episodes; Bipolar II involves hypomania and depression. The defining biological feature is profound vulnerability to circadian and sleep disruption. Anything that disturbs the sleep-wake cycle — travel, shift work, a missed night, even an aggressive lifestyle change — can trigger an episode. This is why several interventions that help unipolar depression require extra caution in bipolar disorder.

### Schizophrenia: A Disorder of Perception and Thought

Schizophrenia affects roughly 1% of people worldwide. It involves “positive” symptoms (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking), “negative” symptoms (flattened emotion, social withdrawal, loss of motivation), and cognitive symptoms (impaired attention, memory, and executive function). It typically emerges in late adolescence or early adulthood. Antipsychotic medication is the cornerstone of treatment, but lifestyle, metabolic health, and social environment substantially shape long-term outcomes.

### Neuroses Versus Psychoses: A Useful Old Distinction

Though no longer formal diagnostic categories, the older split between *neurosis* and *psychosis* still helps people understand their experience. Neurotic conditions (depression, anxiety) involve distress while a person remains in contact with reality. Psychotic conditions (schizophrenia, severe bipolar mania with psychotic features) involve a loss of contact with reality — hallucinations or delusions. The distinction matters practically: psychosis almost always requires medical treatment and supervision, and self-directed lifestyle experimentation is riskier.

### Neurobiology and Genetics

These conditions share overlapping biology. Disrupted neurotransmission (serotonin, dopamine, glutamate, GABA), chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial and bioenergetic dysfunction, and impaired neuroplasticity all play roles. The “metabolic psychiatry” hypothesis — that some serious mental illness stems partly from disturbances in how brain cells produce and use energy — has gained traction and underlies interest in the ketogenic diet.[^8]

Genetically, twin studies estimate heritability at roughly 80% for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder and around 35% for major depression.[^3] Crucially, the heritability captured by common genetic variants is far lower, and identical twins are often discordant — meaning genes load the gun, but environment, lifestyle, and chance pull the trigger. Epigenetic studies of identical twins discordant for psychosis confirm that experience changes how genes are expressed.[^9]

## Lifestyle Interventions

### Nutrition and Diet

**Whole-food, plant-rich dietary patterns.** The strongest dietary evidence supports a whole-food, predominantly plant-based pattern rich in vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and (for non-vegetarians) fish. In the SMILES trial, a modified Mediterranean diet produced a large effect on depression (Cohen’s d = −1.16), with 32.3% reaching remission versus 8.0% in the social-support control group, and a number-needed-to-treat of about 4.[^2] The HELFIMED trial replicated benefits with a Mediterranean diet plus fish oil.[^10] Vegetarian and Mediterranean patterns overlap heavily, and both emphasize the anti-inflammatory, high-fiber, nutrient-dense foods that appear to matter most.

**Food sensitivities and elimination diets.** For a subgroup of people, immune reactions to foods may contribute to symptoms. The most striking evidence is in schizophrenia: about one-third of people with schizophrenia have elevated anti-gliadin (wheat) antibodies of the IgG type, roughly three times the rate in the general population, and these are distinct from celiac disease.[^11] A small inpatient randomized pilot found that removing gluten was associated with improvement in psychiatric and gastrointestinal symptoms in those who were antibody-positive.[^11] The top food allergens worth considering in a supervised elimination trial include wheat/gluten, dairy, eggs, soy, tree nuts/peanuts, shellfish, and corn. Elimination diets should be time-limited, systematic, and ideally guided by a clinician to avoid nutritional gaps.

**Omega-3 fatty acids.** This is one of the best-supported supplements in psychiatry. Meta-analyses show EPA-predominant formulas (≥60% EPA, around 1–2 g EPA/day) reduce depressive symptoms, while DHA-predominant or pure-DHA formulas do not.[^4] Benefits are greater in people also taking antidepressants and in those with inflammatory features.[^12] Effects typically take 8–12 weeks.

**B vitamins.** The one-carbon cycle (folate, B12, B6) is central to neurotransmitter synthesis. Adjunctive L-methylfolate at 15 mg/day improved response in SSRI-resistant depression in two parallel-sequential trials,[^13] and it is the only form of folate that crosses the blood–brain barrier — relevant for people with MTHFR gene variants. Low folate predicts slower and weaker antidepressant response.[^14] However, plain folic acid plus B12 did not improve mood in the large B-PROOF trial of older adults with elevated homocysteine,[^15] so the benefit appears concentrated in those who are deficient or methylation-impaired.

**Vitamin D.** Deficiency is associated with depression, and supplementation helps modestly — most clearly in people who are deficient. A dose-response meta-analysis found each additional 1,000 IU/day of vitamin D3 was associated with improved depressive symptoms, with stronger effects at higher doses and shorter durations.[^16] Testing and correcting deficiency is sensible.

**Magnesium, zinc, and selenium.** These minerals support hundreds of enzymatic reactions and antioxidant defense. Zinc and selenium deficiencies have been reported in schizophrenia, where oxidative stress is implicated. Magnesium is involved in NMDA-receptor regulation and is commonly low in modern diets. While evidence for routine supplementation is mixed, correcting documented deficiencies is reasonable.

**Blood sugar regulation and the gut-brain axis.** Insulin resistance and blood-sugar swings are increasingly linked to mood instability. Stable blood sugar — achieved through fiber, protein, healthy fats, and minimizing refined carbohydrates — supports steadier mood and energy. The gut microbiome communicates with the brain via immune, neural, and metabolic pathways. Multi-strain probiotic trials in depression have shown modest benefits, particularly in increasing beneficial bacteria like *Lactobacillus* and reducing symptoms as an add-on to usual care.[^17] A systematic review concluded that probiotics and synbiotics — but not prebiotics alone — offer modest benefit, while calling for more rigorous trials.[^18] Prebiotic fiber from diverse plants feeds these beneficial microbes.

> **Condition-Specific Note — Ketogenic and metabolic approaches in bipolar disorder and schizophrenia:** A 2024 Stanford pilot trial put 21 adults with bipolar disorder or schizophrenia and metabolic abnormalities on a ketogenic diet (about 10% carbohydrate, 30% protein, 60% fat) for four months. Participants with schizophrenia improved by an average of 32% on the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale, and 69% of bipolar participants showed clinically meaningful improvement (>1-point gain on Clinical Global Impression), alongside reversal of metabolic syndrome.[^5] This is *early, small, single-arm* evidence — promising but not yet proof. A ketogenic diet should only be undertaken with medical supervision, especially because it overlaps with fasting physiology (see fasting cautions below) and can affect lithium levels and hydration.

### Sleep and Circadian Rhythm

Sleep is not optional maintenance — it is active brain repair, and disrupted circadian rhythm is one of the most reliable biological signatures across these conditions.

- **Protect a regular, earlier sleep schedule.** Going to bed and waking at consistent times anchors the body clock. Many people with depression have delayed, fragmented sleep.
- **Get morning bright light.** Light is the master signal that resets the circadian clock. Morning sunlight or a 10,000-lux light box supports mood and sleep timing in non-seasonal and seasonal depression.
- **Practice sleep hygiene.** Dark, cool bedroom; no screens before bed; limit caffeine and alcohol; reserve the bed for sleep.

> **Condition-Specific Note — Light therapy timing in bipolar disorder:** Bright light therapy is an effective adjunct for bipolar depression, but *timing matters and risk is real*. Morning light can trigger hypomania or mania in some people with bipolar disorder. A landmark trial of midday bright light (7,000 lux, around noon) found a significantly higher remission rate (68.2% versus 22.2%; adjusted odds ratio 12.6) with no mood-polarity switches.[^19] In bipolar disorder, light therapy should always be done with a mood stabilizer on board and clinical monitoring. Conversely, in bipolar mania, *reducing* evening light and protecting sleep is protective.

### Physical Activity

**Depression.** Exercise is a frontline intervention. The 2024 BMJ network meta-analysis found walking/jogging, yoga, strength training, and dancing all effective, with larger effects at higher intensities.[^1] A practical prescription: aim for most days of the week, mixing aerobic activity with some strength work, at whatever intensity is sustainable. Even light activity helps; more is generally better, but starting is what matters.

**Schizophrenia.** Exercise improves both symptoms and cognition. A meta-analysis found that around 90 minutes per week of moderate-to-vigorous exercise significantly reduced psychiatric symptoms (SMD ≈ 0.72) and improved functioning and neurocognition.[^20] A separate meta-analysis found aerobic exercise significantly improved global cognition (Hedges’ g ≈ 0.33–0.43), with particularly strong effects on social cognition (g ≈ 0.71) and attention/vigilance (g ≈ 0.66) — an effect size comparable to formal cognitive remediation therapy.[^21] Programs supervised by physical-activity professionals worked best.

**Bipolar disorder.** Exercise supports mood and metabolic health but should be structured to protect sleep and avoid overstimulation during high-risk periods.

**Outdoor/nature therapy and purposeful work.** Time in nature (“green exercise”) and meaningful physical work — gardening, building, caring for animals — combine movement, light, purpose, and often social connection. The sense of accomplishment and contribution is itself therapeutic.

> **A Historical Perspective:** More than a century ago, the health writer Ellen White urged that people struggling with despondency be given useful outdoor work, time in nature, sunlight, and regular hours — counsel once dismissed as quaint. Modern research on exercise, morning light, circadian regularity, and purposeful activity has independently confirmed the core of that advice. *(Included for readers who find this tradition meaningful; the clinical recommendations stand on the peer-reviewed evidence cited above.)*

### Environmental Medicine

**Blood viscosity and schizophrenia.** This is an emerging and genuinely unsettled area. Recent research has found that whole blood viscosity is actually *reduced* in both first-episode and acute exacerbations of schizophrenia compared with healthy controls, possibly reflecting inflammatory changes — the opposite of the older “thick blood” hypothesis.[^22] A 2025 study found that in remitted schizophrenia, certain hemorheological parameters and serum osmolarity correlated with cognitive performance.[^23] The practical takeaway is limited for now — good hydration and cardiovascular health are sensible, but claims that “thick blood causes schizophrenia” are not supported by current evidence.

**Environmental toxins and heavy metals.** Oxidative stress from heavy metal exposure is implicated in schizophrenia. The “copper excess” hypothesis — holding that high copper and low zinc drive psychiatric symptoms — has biological plausibility but lacks robust randomized evidence. Testing for and treating genuine heavy-metal toxicity is appropriate; routine “detox” protocols based on hair analysis are not evidence-based.

**Drug-induced depression.** This is underappreciated and highly actionable. A JAMA study of 26,192 adults found that more than 200 commonly used prescription drugs list depression or suicide as possible side effects, and that the prevalence of depression was 15% among those taking three or more such medications versus 4.7% among those taking none.[^24] Common culprits include some beta-blockers, corticosteroids, hormonal contraceptives, certain anti-seizure drugs, opioids, and proton-pump inhibitors. If depression began or worsened after a new medication, review it with your prescriber.

**Caffeine, alcohol, and other substances.** Caffeine disrupts sleep and can worsen anxiety and trigger mania in vulnerable people. Alcohol is a depressant, disrupts sleep architecture, and interacts dangerously with many psychiatric medications. Cannabis and stimulants can precipitate or worsen psychosis. Reducing these is often one of the highest-yield changes a person can make.

### Hydrotherapy

Water-based treatments have a long history and a modest but real evidence base. A 2024 meta-analysis of 17 randomized trials in 977 adults found hydrotherapy and balneotherapy significantly reduced depression (SMD ≈ 0.53) and anxiety (SMD ≈ 0.46) symptoms as adjunctive treatments.[^25] Practical applications:

- **Warming applications** (hot baths, saunas) can be relaxing and may have antidepressant effects; whole-body hyperthermia has shown preliminary antidepressant signals.
- **Cold applications** (cold showers, cold-water immersion) are stimulating and energizing for some.
- **Contrast therapy** (alternating hot and cold) is invigorating.
- **Neutral baths** (water at body temperature for 20–30 minutes) are deeply calming and have traditionally been used for agitation and insomnia.
- **Wet sheet packs** are a historical sedative hydrotherapy technique.

> **Caution:** People taking medications affecting blood pressure or temperature regulation, and those with cardiovascular disease, should check with a clinician before hot or cold extremes. For agitation in serious mental illness, neutral (body-temperature) applications are safest.

### Herbal Medicine

**St. John’s Wort (*Hypericum perforatum*).** This is the most studied herbal antidepressant. It is effective for mild-to-moderate depression, often comparable to standard antidepressants with fewer side effects in that severity range.[^7] But it carries serious risks: it powerfully induces the CYP450 enzyme system (especially CYP3A4), reducing blood levels of many drugs — oral contraceptives, anticoagulants, HIV and cancer drugs, and immunosuppressants. Combined with SSRIs or other serotonergic drugs, it can cause life-threatening serotonin syndrome. It can also trigger mania in bipolar disorder.

> **Warning — St. John’s Wort:** Never combine St. John’s Wort with prescription antidepressants without medical supervision. It can cause dangerous drug interactions (including serotonin syndrome) and is not recommended in bipolar disorder due to mania risk. Always tell your doctor and pharmacist if you take it.

**Calming herbs.** Valerian (*Valeriana officinalis*) is used for sleep and anxiety; skullcap (*Scutellaria lateriflora*) and passionflower (*Passiflora incarnata*) are traditional nervines with some supportive evidence for anxiety. Oats (*Avena sativa*) are a gentle traditional nerve tonic. These are generally milder and safer than St. John’s Wort but can still interact with sedatives and should be used thoughtfully.

### Mind-Body Approaches

- **Mindfulness and meditation.** Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) is one of the best-supported relapse-prevention tools in depression. An individual-patient-data meta-analysis found MBCT reduced depressive relapse risk over 60 weeks (hazard ratio 0.69, 95% CI 0.58–0.82), with benefits comparable to maintenance antidepressants.[^26] An earlier meta-analysis found a 34% relative reduction in relapse risk.[^27]
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).** A frontline psychotherapy for depression and anxiety, also valuable as an adjunct in bipolar disorder and psychosis.
- **Breathing practices** (slow, diaphragmatic breathing) activate the parasympathetic nervous system and reduce acute distress.
- **Massage** reduces anxiety and can ease the muscular tension that accompanies mood disorders.
- **Routine and regularity.** A predictable daily rhythm of waking, eating, activity, and sleep is quietly one of the most stabilizing interventions, especially in bipolar disorder.

### Social and Occupational Therapy

Human connection is medicine. Isolation worsens every condition discussed here; belonging protects.

- **Family-based versus institutional care.** Family psychoeducation is among the most effective psychosocial interventions for schizophrenia. A network meta-analysis found family interventions substantially reduced relapse, with family psychoeducation alone being especially effective (odds ratio 0.18 versus treatment as usual).[^28] A 2024 Cochrane update found family-based interventions may reduce short-term relapse (RR 0.66), though at low certainty.[^29] The Finnish Open Dialogue model has reported encouraging long-term outcomes (reduced hospitalization, lower antipsychotic use at long-term follow-up),[^30] but only from observational, non-randomized studies — promising rather than proven.
- **Meaningful work and service to others.** Purposeful activity and contributing to others’ wellbeing build self-worth and structure. Service and volunteering are consistently linked to better mental health.
- **Community.** Faith communities, peer-support groups, clubs, and friendships all provide the social scaffolding that supports recovery.

> **Condition-Specific Note — Niacin and the orthomolecular history in schizophrenia:** In the 1950s–60s, psychiatrist Abram Hoffer proposed high-dose niacin (vitamin B3) for schizophrenia. Multiple follow-up studies failed to replicate benefit, and a 1973 American Psychiatric Association task force found significant methodological flaws in the original work. High-dose niacin is *not* a substitute for antipsychotic medication. There remains legitimate scientific interest in niacin-response abnormalities as a biological marker in schizophrenia, but this is a research question, not a treatment recommendation.

## Controversial Topics

### Sexual Behavior and Mental Health

This area is often clouded by stigma, so it deserves clinical clarity. Population studies show an association between sexual activity and mood: in large analyses of US adults (NHANES), weekly sexual activity was associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms (odds ratio ≈ 0.60) compared with infrequent activity,[^31] and low frequency was associated with higher odds of depression in women (OR ≈ 1.37).[^32] These studies are cross-sectional, and the relationship is almost certainly bidirectional — depression lowers libido and many antidepressants reduce sexual function, while satisfying intimacy may support mood.[^33] Regarding masturbation specifically, rigorous mental-health outcome data are sparse; most evidence is either neuroendocrine (orgasm produces a sustained prolactin surge and oxytocin release)[^34] or survey-based. There is no credible evidence that ordinary masturbation causes mental illness. Where distress occurs, it is typically driven by guilt or cultural shame rather than physiological harm, and that distress — not the behavior itself — is what may warrant compassionate clinical attention.

### Substance Use and Mental Illness

Substance use and mental illness frequently co-occur and worsen each other. Cannabis use, especially high-potency products in adolescence, is associated with increased risk of psychosis. Alcohol worsens depression and sleep. Stimulants can trigger mania and psychosis. Treating both conditions together (integrated dual-diagnosis care) produces better outcomes than treating either alone.

### Medication: When Necessary, When to Reconsider

Medication is essential and often non-negotiable in severe depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia. The goal of lifestyle medicine is not to replace medication but to help people get the best possible outcome, sometimes allowing dose reduction *under medical supervision*. Reconsidering medication is appropriate when a drug isn’t working, causes intolerable side effects, or may itself be contributing to symptoms — but changes should always be made collaboratively with a prescriber, never abruptly or alone.

### Fasting: Benefits and Serious Risks

Fasting and time-restricted eating are popular and may offer metabolic benefits, but they carry real psychiatric risk.

> **Warning — Fasting in bipolar disorder and schizophrenia:** Fasting disrupts sleep, circadian rhythm, hydration, and medication timing — all of which can destabilize mood. A prospective study found Ramadan fasting increased relapse risk 2.77-fold in people with bipolar disorder (95% CI 1.23–6.25), with 33.3% of fasters relapsing versus 15.3% of non-fasters.[^6] Fasting is generally **contraindicated or requires close supervision** in unstable mood disorders, psychosis, eating disorders, and for people on lithium (dehydration raises toxicity risk), clozapine (orthostatic hypotension), or diabetes medications. If fasting is undertaken, eating windows of 6–10 hours appear safest, with stable medication and protected sleep.

### Dietary Extremes and Orthorexia

Enthusiasm for healthy eating can tip into orthorexia — an unhealthy obsession with “pure” or “perfect” eating that itself causes distress, social isolation, and malnutrition. Restrictive dietary extremes can also mask or worsen eating disorders. The goal is nourishment and flexibility, not perfection. If food rules are generating anxiety or shrinking your life, that’s a sign to step back and seek guidance.

## When to Seek Help

> **Red flags requiring urgent professional help:**
> 
> - Thoughts of suicide or self-harm, or thoughts that life isn’t worth living
> - A specific plan, means, or intent to harm oneself — **this is an emergency**
> - Hallucinations, delusions, or loss of contact with reality
> - Manic symptoms: little need for sleep, racing thoughts, reckless behavior, grandiosity
> - Inability to care for oneself — not eating, drinking, or maintaining basic safety
> - Rapid worsening of mood or functioning

**Suicide risk indicators** include hopelessness, talking about being a burden, withdrawal, giving away possessions, increased substance use, and dramatic mood changes. Take all such signs seriously. In the US, call or text **988** (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline). Elsewhere, contact local emergency services.

**Hospitalization** may be necessary when someone is at imminent risk of harming themselves or others, is experiencing severe psychosis or mania, or cannot maintain basic safety. This is a protective, often temporary measure.

**Integrating natural with conventional care.** The strongest approach combines the best of both: medication and therapy where needed, built on a foundation of lifestyle medicine. Tell every member of your care team about all supplements and herbs you take — interactions are real. Never stop psychiatric medication suddenly or without medical guidance; doing so can trigger relapse or withdrawal.

## Quick Reference Summary

> **Foundations for everyone:**
> 
> - Eat a whole-food, plant-rich (Mediterranean-style) diet; stabilize blood sugar
> - Consider EPA-predominant fish oil (~1–2 g EPA/day) and correct vitamin D, B12, and folate deficiencies
> - Protect regular, earlier sleep; get morning bright light (midday in bipolar disorder)
> - Move most days — aim for ~90+ minutes/week of moderate activity, more if able
> - Reduce caffeine, alcohol, and other substances; review medications that may cause depression
> - Practice mindfulness or CBT; protect daily routine
> - Stay connected — family, community, purposeful work, service
> 
> **Use extra caution (get medical supervision) with:**
> 
> - **Fasting/keto** — risky in bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, eating disorders, on lithium/clozapine
> - **St. John’s Wort** — dangerous with SSRIs; mania risk in bipolar disorder
> - **Light therapy** — use midday and a mood stabilizer in bipolar disorder
> - **High-dose niacin** — not a substitute for antipsychotic medication
> - **Any supplement** — tell your care team; interactions are real
> 
> **Seek help now if:** thoughts of suicide, psychosis, mania, or inability to stay safe.

-----

A diagnosis of depression, bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia is the beginning of a story, not its end. The science is increasingly clear that the choices you make each day — what you eat, how you sleep, how you move, how you connect — genuinely shape the trajectory of these conditions. None of it replaces good medical care, and none of it guarantees an outcome. But taken together, these are real, evidence-based tools that put meaningful power back in your hands. That is reason for grounded, realistic hope.
