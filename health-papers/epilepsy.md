It's the third Sunday of the month, and the fellowship hall smells like coffee and casseroles. Kids chase each other between folding tables. Then, from the corner by the dessert table, a chair scrapes back hard, and a plate hits the floor.

Marcus is on the ground, his arms jerking. Someone reaches to hold him still, to put something between his teeth. But his sister, who has watched this happen since he was nine, is already there. She moves the chair aside, slides her sweater under his head, and checks her watch. She doesn't restrain him, and she doesn't put anything in his mouth. She just stays, and waits, and after ninety seconds it's over. Marcus is confused and tired; his sister helps him sit up, and someone else quietly starts refilling coffee cups. This is Tuesday. This is Marcus's life, and he is doing fine.

If you've never been close to someone with epilepsy, a moment like this can feel frightening. If you have, you know it's usually far less dramatic than it looks, and that the people living with it are managing a chronic condition — the same way someone manages diabetes — with good medical care, daily habits, and people around them who know what to do.

This handout is for people living with epilepsy, the families and friends who love them, and the caring people in congregations and community roles who want to support them well. We'll cover what epilepsy actually is, what helps, what the evidence says about popular natural approaches, and — because it matters enormously — where real caution isn't optional.

## What Epilepsy Is (and Isn't)

A seizure is a sudden, temporary electrical storm in the brain — a burst of activity that disrupts normal signaling for seconds to minutes. Almost anyone can have a seizure under the right circumstances: a high fever, a head injury, dangerously low blood sugar, alcohol withdrawal. Having *a* seizure doesn't mean you have epilepsy.

Epilepsy is an ongoing tendency to have seizures. The definition neurologists use worldwide says a person has epilepsy if they've had two unprovoked seizures more than a day apart, or one unprovoked seizure with a high chance of recurrence, or a diagnosed epilepsy syndrome.[^1] It's not about a single event, but about a brain prone to these storms recurring.

It's more common than most people realize. The World Health Organization estimates around 50 million people worldwide live with epilepsy.[^2] In the United States, the most recent nationwide count found about 3.4 million people — 3 million adults and 470,000 children — with active epilepsy.[^3] Over a lifetime, the Institute of Medicine estimates 1 in 26 people in the U.S. will develop epilepsy.[^4] Odds are, you already know someone.

Here's the hopeful part: WHO reports that up to about 70% of people with epilepsy could become seizure-free with proper diagnosis and treatment.[^2] For most people, epilepsy is very manageable.

Neurologists recently updated how they classify seizures. As of 2025, the ILAE groups seizures into four categories: focal (starting in one brain area), generalized (involving both sides from the start), unknown onset, and unclassified.[^5] Focal seizures can happen with full awareness — a strange smell, déjà vu, a tingling sensation — or with impaired awareness, where someone seems to "check out" or fumbles with their clothing. Generalized seizures include the dramatic tonic-clonic type (stiffening, then rhythmic jerking), absence seizures (brief blank staring, common in children), myoclonic jerks, and atonic seizures, where sudden loss of muscle tone causes a fall.

You'll increasingly hear "antiseizure medication" instead of "antiepileptic drug" — a small but meaningful shift, since these medications control seizures but don't cure the underlying condition for most people.[^2]

Epilepsy is a spectrum, not one disease. Some children have childhood absence epilepsy and outgrow it by their teens. Others live with severe, lifelong syndromes like Dravet or Lennox-Gastaut, with developmental challenges and hard-to-control seizures. Most people fall somewhere in between.

## Why Seizures Happen: Causes and Mechanisms

Think of the brain as an electrical grid balancing "go" signals (the chemical glutamate) and "stop" signals (GABA), like circuit breakers. A seizure happens when that balance tips and electrical activity spreads further than it should.

In about half of all cases, the cause is unknown.[^2] Where a cause is identified, it's usually genetic, structural (stroke, tumor, traumatic injury, birth injury), infectious, metabolic, or immune-related.

A few causes connect directly to everyday life. **Neurocysticercosis** — pork tapeworm larvae in the brain — is a leading preventable cause of epilepsy worldwide.[^6] The brain infection comes from ingesting tapeworm eggs shed in an infected person's feces, typically through contaminated food, water, or poor handwashing — not simply from eating undercooked pork.[^6] That means sanitation and handwashing matter regardless of diet.

**Celiac disease** has a documented, uncommon connection to epilepsy, including a specific pattern involving calcium deposits visible on brain imaging.[^7] This doesn't mean gluten causes epilepsy broadly — it's specific to people who actually have celiac disease.

**Pyridoxine-dependent epilepsy** is a rare genetic condition appearing in newborns and infants, caused by a vitamin B6 processing problem.[^8] It's worth knowing this newborn condition is entirely different from an adult trying B vitamins on their own.

**Stroke** is the most common cause of new-onset epilepsy in older adults.[^9] Managing blood pressure and other stroke risk factors protects brain health broadly.

## Recognizing Seizures and Knowing What to Do

Seizures don't always look dramatic. Some are subtle: a blank stare, lip smacking, confusion, an odd smell that seems to come from nowhere. Others are unmistakable: a sudden fall, stiffening, rhythmic jerking.

The response is the same either way, and simple enough for anyone to learn. The Epilepsy Foundation and CDC both teach **Stay, Safe, Side**.[^10]

- **Stay** with the person. Time the seizure. Stay calm.
- Keep them **Safe**. Move away anything they could hit their head on. Never hold them down or restrain their movements.
- Turn them onto their **Side** once jerking stops or if they're not fully alert, so fluid can drain away from the airway.[^11]

> **Key Fact:** Nothing should ever go into the mouth of someone having a seizure — not fingers, not a spoon, not water, not any home remedy. A person having a seizure cannot swallow their tongue, and putting something in their mouth risks choking and injury.

After the seizure, stay with the person as they come back to full awareness, which can take a few minutes and often comes with confusion. Call emergency services if: the seizure lasts five minutes or more; a second seizure starts before recovery from the first; it's a first-ever seizure; it happens in water; the person is injured, pregnant, or diabetic; or they have ongoing trouble breathing afterward.[^11]

A seizure that won't stop, or repeated seizures without recovery between them, is status epilepticus — a medical emergency.[^12] Families of people with harder-to-control epilepsy are often trained to give a rescue medication if a seizure crosses a certain time threshold. If your family has one, follow the specific plan your prescriber wrote — this isn't something to improvise.

One condition deserves compassionate understanding: functional (psychogenic nonepileptic) seizures. These look like epileptic seizures but arise from a different process, often connected to stress or trauma rather than electrical misfiring. Among people admitted to epilepsy monitoring units for hard-to-control seizures, roughly 20 to 40% turn out to have functional seizures rather than, or alongside, epileptic ones.[^13] This is not "faking" — these are real, involuntary events that respond to a different kind of treatment, usually therapy rather than medication. Accurate diagnosis, often through video-EEG monitoring, is what gets someone the right help.[^14]

## Conventional Treatment: What It Offers and Where It Falls Short

For most people, antiseizure medication works. A landmark study found about two-thirds of newly diagnosed patients became seizure-free, most on their first medication.[^15] A thirty-year follow-up found nearly identical results: 64% seizure-free at last assessment.[^16] For most people, epilepsy is very treatable.

But it's not the whole story. That same long-term study found each additional medication tried after the first two failed adds only a small chance of seizure freedom.[^16] This is why the field defines "drug-resistant epilepsy" as continuing seizures despite two appropriately chosen medications.[^17] If that describes your situation, it's a signal to ask about referral to a comprehensive epilepsy center.

Side effects are real. Depending on the medication, people report fatigue, mood changes, cognitive slowing, or weight changes. Some older medications, used long-term, are linked to bone density loss, and phenytoin specifically to gum tissue overgrowth.[^16] These are legitimate concerns worth raising directly with the prescriber — switching or adjusting medication is safer than quietly stopping it.

For the roughly one-third whose seizures don't respond well to medication, other options are often underused. Epilepsy surgery, for carefully selected patients, has strong evidence: a randomized trial found 58% of surgical patients free of awareness-impairing seizures after a year, versus 8% on medication alone.[^18] A trial focused on earlier referral found 73% seizure-free after surgery versus none on medication.[^19] Yet people often live with epilepsy for close to two decades before reaching surgical evaluation.[^20] If two medications haven't worked, ask specifically about a comprehensive epilepsy center.

Other options include neurostimulation devices — vagus nerve stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, deep brain stimulation — typically aimed at reducing rather than eliminating seizures. Newer medications include cenobamate, fenfluramine (for Dravet syndrome), and prescription cannabidiol, covered later. At least one additional investigational medication has completed late-stage trials and is under FDA review, though not yet approved.

Now the hardest but most important part: sudden unexpected death in epilepsy, or SUDEP. National guidelines estimate it affects roughly 1 in 1,000 adults with epilepsy per year, and about 1 in 4,500 children.[^21] The strongest known risk factor is frequent generalized tonic-clonic seizures.[^21] This is precisely why consistent medication use matters: good seizure control is one of the most protective things a person can do.

> **Key Fact:** Frequent generalized tonic-clonic seizures are the strongest known risk factor for SUDEP. Taking antiseizure medication consistently, and never stopping it without medical guidance, is one of the most protective choices a person with epilepsy can make.

Some families also use nighttime monitoring — a listening device or simply someone nearby during sleep — since this could reduce risk for people with frequent nighttime seizures.[^21] It's worth discussing with a neurologist.

## Lifestyle and Nutrition Approaches

**The ketogenic diet** has the strongest evidence of any dietary approach, and it's been used for a century.[^22] The classic version is high in fat and very low in carbohydrates; related approaches, like the modified Atkins diet, aim for similar effects with more flexibility. A Cochrane review found these diets can meaningfully help children with drug-resistant epilepsy, though evidence for adults is less certain.[^22] A children's trial found seizures dropped well below baseline, with over a third of children cutting seizures by more than half, versus 6% of controls.[^23] A modified Atkins trial in adults found no significant overall benefit, though people who completed the full trial saw meaningful improvement.[^24]

The leading theory involves ketones changing brain excitability.[^22] Early research in mice suggests gut bacteria may play a role — a fascinating direction, but not yet established in humans and not something to try to replicate with a supplement.[^25]

These are medical therapies needing a care team, not a home project. Risks include kidney stones, elevated cholesterol, constipation, slowed growth in children, and rare cases of dangerous acidosis; certain metabolic conditions make these diets unsafe.[^22]

> **Key Fact:** The ketogenic diet has been used for epilepsy since the 1920s and has solid evidence in children with drug-resistant seizures. It's a genuine medical treatment — it works best and most safely with a care team involved.

For readers who eat plant-based, there's an understandable tension since classic keto leans on animal fats. This is an area of ongoing exploration rather than settled guidance — worth raising with a dietitian experienced in diet therapy.

Beyond diet: **sleep deprivation** is one of the best-documented seizure triggers. **Stress** is the most commonly self-reported trigger — in one survey, over half of participants reported at least one trigger, with emotional stress and sleep deprivation topping the list.[^26] Mind-body practices like mindfulness and cognitive behavioral therapy show promise, though the evidence is still developing.

**Alcohol** withdrawal can itself provoke seizures, and alcohol can interact with medications. **Physical activity** is generally safe and beneficial; international sports medicine guidelines encourage it, with individualized precautions — never swimming alone, judgment around heights or heavy contact.[^27]

**Photosensitivity** — seizures triggered by flashing lights — affects only a minority of people with epilepsy and isn't a general concern for everyone.

For women, **catamenial epilepsy** clusters seizures around the menstrual cycle. A large NIH trial testing progesterone found no overall benefit, but a possible benefit in a subgroup with a strong perimenstrual pattern.[^28] This is prescriber territory, not something to self-manage with over-the-counter products.

People on certain older medications may need attention to bone health and vitamin D, and women planning pregnancy are often advised to take folic acid — specifics best set by a prescriber.

Finally: **drowning**. People with epilepsy face a substantially higher risk than the general population — one analysis found it elevated fifteen to nineteen times, with bathtubs a common setting.[^29]

> **Key Fact:** People with epilepsy face a much higher risk of drowning, and bathtubs are a common setting. A shower, or a bath only with someone nearby, is a simple habit that protects a life.

## Popular Natural Remedies: Exploring the Evidence

Online health communities are full of testimonials about home remedies for epilepsy. These deserve a fair, specific hearing, because the people sharing them are genuinely trying to help. Here's what the research shows, by evidence level.

**Some research support, though only alongside medical care:**

Omega-3 fatty acids have a mixed record. A Cochrane review found the evidence inconclusive,[^30] while a small controlled trial found low-dose fish oil modestly reduced seizures, though higher doses didn't help.[^31] Claims that fish oil worsens seizures aren't supported by evidence — but the case for benefit is genuinely mixed.

Magnesium is layered. IV magnesium sulfate is the standard treatment for eclamptic seizures in pregnancy — a specific, hospital-supervised use.[^32] Low magnesium can provoke seizures, but trial evidence for routine oral supplementation specifically for epilepsy is limited. Oral Epsom salt, taken as a laxative, can cause diarrhea (which can reduce medication absorption) and can be dangerous with kidney disease.[^32] Epsom salt baths don't hold up well: meaningful magnesium absorption through skin isn't well supported.[^33] Combined with drowning risk, a shower is safer than a bath regardless of what's dissolved in the water.

**Limited or emerging evidence:**

Coconut oil and MCT oil aren't equivalent. Medical MCT oil is a legitimate part of the ketogenic diet; plain coconut oil has less of the most ketogenic fats, and there are no controlled trials of coconut oil alone. Dramatic online improvement stories are genuine, but hard to interpret: seizures naturally fluctuate, other changes often happen simultaneously, and improvement after an unusually bad stretch is a well-known statistical pattern regardless of cause. These experiences deserve respect — but they can't substitute for treatment.

Melatonin's small trials are too limited for firm conclusions, per Cochrane.[^34] Acupuncture's evidence is insufficient to support use, per another Cochrane review.[^35] Calming herbs like passionflower and valerian may help sleep and stress — real triggers — but lack direct seizure evidence, and their sedating effect can add to medication drowsiness.

**Contested topics:**

Artificial sweeteners come up often. An older study found aspartame appeared to worsen a specific EEG pattern in children with absence epilepsy.[^36] The warning labels on aspartame exist for an unrelated reason: people with phenylketonuria must avoid a component called phenylalanine — that labeling has nothing to do with epilepsy. If you seem to react to a sweetener, a simple trigger diary for your neurologist is reasonable.

MSG taps into real neuroscience — glutamate is the brain's main excitatory signal — but dietary glutamate largely doesn't cross into the brain in healthy adults, limiting how directly it could affect seizures.

Gluten-free diets have clear rationale in confirmed celiac disease. Outside celiac disease, evidence for seizure benefit is limited.

Pyroluria, hair mineral analysis, and "orthomolecular" protocols deserve fair but honest treatment. Pyroluria isn't a recognized diagnosis, and hair mineral analysis is widely considered unreliable for nutritional status. High-dose zinc and B6, sometimes used in these protocols, carry real risks: too much zinc can cause copper deficiency, too much B6 can cause nerve damage.

Mercury amalgam removal and homeopathy have no solid evidence supporting a role in epilepsy. Therapeutic fasting has an interesting history — early observations that fasting reduced seizures helped lead to the ketogenic diet[^22] — but unsupervised fasting risks low blood sugar and disrupted medication timing.

**Where stronger caution is genuinely warranted:**

Stopping or replacing antiseizure medication with a natural remedy is the most dangerous item on this list. Uncontrolled generalized tonic-clonic seizures are the strongest known SUDEP risk factor, and abruptly stopping medication can trigger status epilepticus.[^21] If someone wants to reduce or stop medication, for any reason, that conversation belongs with the prescriber — not a solo decision. Tapering is sometimes appropriate, but only under medical supervision.

Putting anything in the mouth during a seizure — cayenne in water, a spoon — risks choking and injury. Borax, historically tried as a seizure treatment, is toxic and was abandoned for good reason; it has no place in seizure management. An infant having seizures needs emergency medical care, not a home remedy.

Certain herbs and essential oils, especially concentrated, may lower the seizure threshold: sage, rosemary, eucalyptus, camphor, hyssop, fennel, thujone-containing plants like wormwood, and ginkgo. The concern mainly involves concentrated oils or extracts, not ordinary cooking amounts.

Herb-drug interactions are common and underestimated. St. John's wort can lower blood levels of several antiseizure medications. Grapefruit interacts with carbamazepine. Kava carries liver toxicity risk. Imported herbal products have occasionally been found contaminated with heavy metals.

> **Key Fact:** St. John's wort, a popular herbal supplement for mood, can lower the blood levels of several antiseizure medications. Always tell your prescriber and pharmacist about every herb and supplement you take.

## Cannabis and CBD: A Contested Topic Handled Carefully

This is understandably sensitive for many faith communities, so let's handle it with care.

Prescription cannabidiol (Epidiolex) is FDA-approved for Dravet syndrome, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, and tuberous sclerosis complex. Its Dravet trial found convulsive seizures dropped substantially more on medication than placebo.[^37] A Lennox-Gastaut trial found drop seizures fell by a median of nearly 44%, versus about 22% on placebo.[^38] A tuberous sclerosis trial found reductions of 47 to 49% depending on dose.[^39] CBD is non-intoxicating — it produces no "high" — distinguishing it entirely from recreational marijuana and its psychoactive component, THC.

Over-the-counter CBD products are far less reliable: one study found fewer than a third of online products accurately labeled, with some containing undetected THC.[^40] CBD, prescription or not, can interact with medications — raising clobazam levels and, with valproate, raising liver enzymes that need monitoring.[^41]

Families of children with severe epilepsy drove much of this research through their own observations, which then led to rigorous trials — a genuine example of lived experience advancing science. For readers with moral reservations about cannabis: prescription CBD is a regulated pharmaceutical, evaluated the same way as any other medication — a different category from recreational use.

## Special Populations

**Pregnancy.** Most women with epilepsy have healthy pregnancies. The exception is valproate, which carries serious risks of birth defects and neurodevelopmental problems, leading UK, EU, and U.S. regulators to issue strong warnings. Some UK and EU regulators have also raised a precautionary concern about valproate taken by fathers before conception, though this is actively debated, with some studies not finding the association.[^42] This is a time-sensitive conversation for the prescriber — never a reason to stop medication abruptly. Folic acid before and during pregnancy is commonly recommended, with specifics set by a prescriber.

**Contraception.** Some antiseizure medications reduce hormonal birth control's effectiveness, and estrogen can lower blood levels of lamotrigine specifically. Worth coordinating between neurologist and whoever manages contraception.

**Children.** A good school seizure action plan, and being supported to participate fully in ordinary activities rather than being overprotected, both matter.

**Older adults.** Epilepsy is often missed here, since seizures can look like confusion or unexplained "spells." Medication interactions are also more common. A new pattern of unexplained confusion in an older family member is worth mentioning to their doctor.

## Mind, Heart, and Community

Depression and anxiety are both common in epilepsy — a review pooling many studies found roughly one in five people experiencing each.[^43] Suicide risk is also elevated. None of this is a personal failing; it deserves the same attention as the seizures themselves.

Epilepsy carries a long history of stigma, including beliefs in many cultures — some still encountered today — that seizures reflect spiritual causes rather than a medical condition.[^2] Addressing this gently and directly helps.

> **Key Fact:** Epilepsy is one of the oldest recorded medical conditions, and it has carried stigma for thousands of years. Today we understand it as a disorder of the brain's electrical activity — not a character flaw, a spiritual failing, or something contagious.

For readers whose faith is central, there's real room here: prayer, hope, and community can be genuine sources of resilience alongside good medical care — not instead of it. A congregation can support someone with epilepsy by learning seizure first aid together, offering rides (since driving restrictions during uncontrolled seizures can be isolating), and making space for full inclusion rather than treating someone as fragile.

Driving restrictions deserve compassion. Laws vary, but most require a seizure-free period before driving resumes, and losing that ability — even temporarily — carries real emotional weight tied to independence.

Consider hosting a seizure first aid training session, which the Epilepsy Foundation offers as a certification.[^10] It's a small investment that pays off the first time it's needed.

## When to Refer to a Clinician

- Any first seizure, or a suspected seizure not yet evaluated
- A seizure lasting five minutes or more, or clusters without recovery between them — call emergency services
- Any change in seizure pattern, frequency, or type
- Seizures continuing despite two appropriately chosen medications — ask about an epilepsy center
- Bothersome side effects, including mood changes or thoughts of self-harm
- Pregnancy, a planned pregnancy, or any change to contraception
- Interest in trying a diet therapy, supplement, herb, CBD product, or fasting
- Any interest in reducing or stopping medication
- New confusion or unexplained falls in an older adult
- Staring spells or unusual episodes in a child

## What Supportive Helpers Can Appropriately Do

If you're supporting someone with epilepsy without being their clinician, there's a real role, alongside clear boundaries.

**Genuinely helpful:** learning seizure first aid; encouraging good sleep and stress management; helping keep a simple seizure and trigger diary for appointments; supporting medication routines; helping prepare questions before a doctor's visit; offering steady emotional support.

**Not appropriate, however caring the intention:** suggesting someone reduce or stop medication; recommending supplement doses; guiding a ketogenic diet without a medical team; interpreting EEGs or lab results; diagnosing seizure type.

Phrasing matters. Instead of a recommendation, try: "Some people find that tracking sleep and stress helps them notice patterns — would you want to try that and share it with your neurologist?" Or, about a natural remedy someone read about: "That's interesting — what does your doctor think about trying that alongside your current treatment?"

## Five Things You Can Start This Week

If you have epilepsy: start a simple sleep and stress log for your next appointment. If two medications haven't controlled your seizures, ask directly about referral to a comprehensive epilepsy center. If someone in your household has epilepsy: learn Stay, Safe, Side this week. If you're part of a congregation: ask whether hosting a seizure first aid training might be worth it. And wherever you are in this journey: know that epilepsy, for most people, is very manageable — with good medical care, steady habits, and a community that shows up.

People with epilepsy build full, rich lives. The right combination of medical care, everyday habits, and people who know what to do when something happens isn't just protective. It's what lets life keep going, one ordinary Tuesday at a time.
