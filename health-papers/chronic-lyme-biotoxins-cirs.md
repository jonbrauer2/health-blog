*A balanced, evidence-based resource for health coaches, wellness practitioners, and church health ministry leaders.*

-----

## How to Read This Guide

Throughout this guide we use a four-level “evidence spectrum” so you can quickly see how strong the science is behind any claim:

- **Gold standard** — supported by randomized controlled trials (RCTs), systematic reviews, or Cochrane reviews.
- **Evidence-supported** — backed by several consistent peer-reviewed studies.
- **Emerging** — early or limited studies suggest a possible benefit, but it is not yet confirmed.
- **Insufficient or contested** — the evidence is weak, conflicting, or comes mostly from one group of researchers.

We avoid framing these topics as simply “scientific versus unscientific.” Real people are suffering with real symptoms, and many honest scientific questions remain open. Our goal is to be truthful about what is well established, what is still being studied, and what has been tested and not supported. This is a teaching handout, not medical advice; every individual’s care should be guided by qualified clinicians.

# PART 1 — UNDERSTANDING

## 1. Introduction: What Is “Chronic Lyme”?

Lyme disease is a bacterial infection caused by *Borrelia burgdorferi* (and related *Borrelia* species), spread to people through the bite of infected blacklegged ticks.[^1] When caught early, it is usually treated successfully with a short course of antibiotics. The hard questions begin when symptoms do not go away.

Mainstream medicine and patient-advocacy groups often use different words for the same painful reality. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) use the term **Post-Treatment Lyme Disease Syndrome (PTLDS)** to describe people who were treated for documented Lyme disease but still have fatigue, widespread pain, or “brain fog” lasting six months or more.[^2] The CDC discourages the term “chronic Lyme disease” because it implies an ongoing active infection when, in their words, the cause “is not currently known.”[^3] Under the IDSA definition cited in the peer-reviewed literature, PTLDS “symptoms occur in 10%–20% of patients previously diagnosed with [Lyme disease] and appropriately treated with a course of antibiotics.”[^2]

The **International Lyme and Associated Diseases Society (ILADS)** sees it differently. ILADS argues that some patients may have a persistent infection, relapse, or untreated co-infections, and it defines “chronic Lyme disease” as an ongoing infection that may warrant longer, individualized treatment.[^4] This disagreement is not just about words; it shapes how patients are diagnosed, treated, and whether insurance pays for care.

Lyme disease is common and growing. Over 89,000 cases were reported to the CDC in 2023, but the CDC estimates that “nearly 476,000 people in the U.S. are diagnosed and treated for Lyme disease each year” — a figure that likely includes some people treated on suspicion who do not actually have Lyme.[^5] Roughly 90% of reported cases come from 15 high-incidence states in the Northeast, mid-Atlantic, and upper Midwest.[^6] Whatever we call the lingering form, the suffering is real, and people deserve compassion and honesty.

## 2. Biotoxins: Sources, Mechanisms, and Health Effects

A **biotoxin** is a poisonous substance produced by a living organism. In the context of chronic illness, the biotoxins most often discussed are:

- **Mold and mycotoxins** — toxic chemicals made by certain fungi (such as *Aspergillus*, *Penicillium*, *Fusarium*, and *Stachybotrys*), especially in water-damaged buildings.[^7]
- **Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae)** — which can produce toxins such as microcystins.
- **Dinoflagellates** — marine organisms that produce ciguatoxin and similar compounds.
- **Actinobacteria and bacterial endotoxins** — found in damp indoor environments.
- **Tick-borne pathogens** — bacteria and parasites discussed elsewhere in this guide.

How might biotoxins cause harm? Laboratory and animal research points to several mechanisms. Mycotoxins can trigger the release of pro-inflammatory signaling molecules (cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF), activate immune cells called microglia in the brain, and contribute to **neuroinflammation**, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction.[^7][^8] A scoping review found that common mycotoxins can damage the nervous system through disrupted neuronal signaling, oxidative stress, and inflammation.[^9]

Here honesty matters. Most of the strongest evidence about mycotoxin harm comes from **dietary (eaten) exposure** in animals and from high-dose laboratory studies, not from breathing indoor air. The landmark 2004 Institute of Medicine (now National Academy of Medicine) report *Damp Indoor Spaces and Health* found **sufficient evidence** linking damp indoor environments to upper-respiratory symptoms, cough, wheeze, and asthma symptoms in sensitized people — but it did **not** find good evidence that inhaled mold causes the wide range of systemic illnesses sometimes attributed to it.[^10][^11] So: the respiratory and allergic effects of damp buildings are **evidence-supported**, while broad claims of “toxic mold” causing multi-system illness through inhalation remain **contested**.

-----

## 3. CIRS: A Comprehensive Overview

**Chronic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (CIRS)** is a model developed in the 1990s by physician **Dr. Ritchie Shoemaker**.[^12] The theory holds that in genetically susceptible people, exposure to biotoxins (most often from water-damaged buildings) sets off a chronic, self-sustaining inflammatory response that the body cannot switch off, producing a multi-system, multi-symptom illness.[^12]

Shoemaker’s diagnostic framework typically involves several tiers:[^13]

- **Exposure history** — evidence of a water-damaged building or other biotoxin source.
- **Symptom clusters** — 37 symptoms grouped into clusters; proponents say eight or more positive clusters suggest high probability of CIRS.[^14]
- **Visual Contrast Sensitivity (VCS) testing** — a vision test of the ability to detect shades of gray, used as a screening tool.
- **Genetic susceptibility** — certain **HLA-DR** immune-system gene types said to impair biotoxin clearance.
- **Biomarkers** — blood tests including TGF-beta1, MMP-9, C4a, MSH (melanocyte-stimulating hormone), and VIP (vasoactive intestinal polypeptide).[^13]

**How strong is the evidence?** This is where intellectual honesty is essential. CIRS is **contested** as a clinical entity. It is accepted by parts of the functional and environmental medicine community but is **not recognized as an established diagnosis by mainstream medical bodies**. UCLA Health states plainly that “CIRS is not widely seen as an established medical diagnosis. Almost everything about it remains the subject of research and ongoing debate. This includes the criteria for diagnosis, biomarkers and ways to treat it.”[^15]

The American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (AAAAI) has stated that “there is no credible evidence to suggest that environmental exposure to molds or their products leads to a state of clinically significant altered immunity,” and that “the practice of performing large numbers of nonspecific immune-based tests as an indication of mold exposure or mold-related illness is not evidence based and is to be discouraged.”[^16] The American College of Medical Toxicology (ACMT) concluded in its position statement that “there are no medical toxicologically-based diagnostic laboratory or treatment modalities in the management of individuals exposed to damp indoor spaces that are supportive of inhalation contact with mycotoxins as a systemic toxicant.”[^17]

A practical point for coaches: **CIRS has no distinct diagnostic code** in the international classification systems (ICD-10-CM or ICD-11). Practitioners who use the label typically borrow the code R65.10, which was actually created for **Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS)** of non-infectious origin — a different, sepsis-spectrum condition.[^18] This means CIRS is not formally recognized in the coding systems used by hospitals and insurers; the borrowed code is a workaround, not formal recognition.

None of this means the patients are not ill. It means the *specific model* — that a defined biotoxin syndrome can be diagnosed by this particular panel of tests — has not been validated by independent researchers outside the originating group.

## 4. The Interconnection: Lyme, Biotoxins, and CIRS

Why are Lyme disease and biotoxin illness so often discussed together? There are a few proposed links, though most are **emerging** or **contested** rather than firmly established.

First, tick-borne infections are themselves sometimes classified as biotoxin sources within the Shoemaker framework, and proponents propose that infection may “prime” the immune system to react abnormally to other biotoxins.[^12] This is a hypothesis, not a proven mechanism.

Second, **co-infections** are genuinely important. A single tick can carry more than one pathogen, so a person may be infected with *Borrelia* plus *Babesia* (a malaria-like parasite), *Bartonella*, *Ehrlichia*, or *Anaplasma* at the same time.[^19] In a 2024 Penn State Health study (Ssentongo et al., *Open Forum Infectious Diseases*) of 3,521 U.S. babesiosis patients, 42% (95% CI 40–43%) were co-infected with at least one other tick-borne disease, most commonly *Borrelia burgdorferi*/Lyme at 41% (95% CI 39–42%).[^20] Co-infections can make illness more severe and recovery longer, and they may explain why some patients do not improve with standard Lyme treatment alone.[^19]

Third, the **HLA-DR genotype** is central to the CIRS theory: Shoemaker proposed that certain immune gene types cannot effectively “present” and clear biotoxins, leaving them to recirculate in the body.[^14] It is true that HLA genes influence immune responses and are linked to various autoimmune conditions. However, the specific claim that particular HLA-DR “haplotypes” predict biotoxin-clearance failure rests largely on the originating group’s own work and has not been independently confirmed.

## 5. Diagnosis Challenges and Controversies

Diagnosing Lyme disease and its aftermath is genuinely difficult, and the controversies here are legitimate.

**Testing limitations.** The standard approach is **two-tier serologic testing**: an ELISA (or similar) screening test, followed by a Western blot for confirmation.[^21] This method is highly **specific** (few false positives) but has poor **sensitivity in early infection**. The CDC itself notes that sensitivity of two-tier testing is only about 30–40% during early infection while antibodies are still developing, rising to 70–100% in disseminated (later) disease.[^22] Because the tests detect antibodies rather than the bacteria themselves, a person tested too soon after a bite may test negative even while infected.[^21] This is a real limitation, not a fringe complaint.

**The seronegative debate.** Some patients have symptoms consistent with Lyme but never test positive. Mainstream medicine cautions that a negative test in someone without a clear exposure usually means another cause should be sought; Lyme-focused clinicians argue that test insensitivity means some true cases are missed.[^21] Both points contain truth, which is why clinical judgment matters.

**Why “PTLDS” vs. “chronic Lyme” matters clinically.** The distinction is not mere semantics. If lingering symptoms reflect a *post-infectious* process (immune, inflammatory, or tissue damage after the bacteria are gone), then more antibiotics will not help and may cause harm. If they reflect *ongoing active infection*, then continued antimicrobial treatment might be justified. The weight of high-quality evidence (see Section 9) favors the post-infectious interpretation for most patients, but the question is not fully closed for every individual.[^2]

**Functional vs. mainstream perspectives.** Mainstream infectious-disease medicine emphasizes randomized controlled trials and standardized testing. Functional and Lyme-literate clinicians place more weight on clinical observation, individual response, and emerging research on bacterial persistence.[^4] Conflicting guidelines most often arise, as ILADS itself notes, “when evidence is weak.”[^4]

**Risk of misdiagnosis and long-term antibiotics.** This is a serious safety concern. Labeling symptoms as “chronic Lyme” can delay the correct diagnosis of other treatable conditions. Prolonged antibiotic therapy — especially intravenous antibiotics — carries real risks, including dangerous bloodstream infections, gallbladder problems, and antibiotic resistance. These risks are why mainstream bodies urge caution.

# PART 2 — MANAGEMENT AND STRATEGIES

## 6. Holistic and Integrative Approaches

“Integrative medicine” combines conventional medical care with evidence-informed complementary approaches — nutrition, stress management, sleep, movement, and mind-body practices — while treating the whole person. For people with persistent, poorly understood symptoms, a whole-person framework is humane and often genuinely helpful for quality of life.

Where is the evidence strongest? It is strongest for foundational lifestyle measures — good nutrition, sleep, gentle activity within tolerance, and psychological support — which improve well-being across many chronic illnesses. Evidence is weaker, or absent, for many specialized “detox” protocols, intravenous infusions, and proprietary supplement regimens marketed specifically for chronic Lyme or biotoxin illness.

The honest middle path is this: integrative *support* of the whole person is reasonable and compassionate, but it should not be confused with *cures*. Coaches and ministry leaders should be especially cautious about expensive, unproven therapies that promise dramatic recovery, and should never encourage clients to abandon evidence-based medical care.

-----

## 7. Nutritional Support and Supplements

**Anti-inflammatory diet and omega-3s.** A diet rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and omega-3 fatty acids is sensible and broadly health-promoting. Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA) have biologically plausible anti-inflammatory effects and reduce some inflammatory markers, though they are not a cure-all.[^23] The large VITAL randomized trial found that omega-3 supplements modestly reduced inflammatory markers mainly in people who ate little fish, and that vitamin D and omega-3 did not dramatically lower systemic inflammation markers across the general population.[^24] So: a whole-food, plant-forward, omega-3-rich diet is **evidence-supported** for general health, while supplements offer **modest** and selective benefits.

**Gut microbiome.** Research has found that people with PTLDS may have a distinct gut microbiome “signature,” with changes such as increased *Blautia* and decreased *Bacteroides*.[^25] This is **emerging** science — interesting and potentially useful as a future diagnostic clue, but not yet a basis for specific treatment.

**Specific supplements with at least some supporting evidence:**

- **Vitamin D** — in the VITAL trial (Hahn et al., *The BMJ*, 2022; 25,871 participants, median 5.3 years’ follow-up), vitamin D supplementation “with or without omega 3 fatty acids, reduced autoimmune disease by 22%” (hazard ratio 0.78, 95% CI 0.61–0.99); correcting a genuine deficiency is worthwhile.[^24]
- **Magnesium** — reasonable for correcting deficiency and supporting sleep and muscle function.
- **Probiotics** — may help protect the gut during and after antibiotic use (evidence-supported for antibiotic-associated diarrhea).
- **N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and glutathione** — NAC is a precursor to the antioxidant glutathione; reviews show it can reduce oxidative-stress markers, and it has established medical uses, though strong evidence for treating Lyme or biotoxin illness specifically is lacking.[^26]

**Supplements with insufficient evidence for these specific conditions:** Many proprietary “binders,” herbal antimicrobial protocols, and high-dose supplement “stacks” marketed for chronic Lyme or CIRS lack rigorous trial support. They should be clearly labeled as **insufficient/unproven** and discussed with a physician, especially because supplements can interact with medications.

## 8. Environmental Modifications

If a home or church building has water damage or visible mold, addressing it is sound public-health practice — independent of any debate about CIRS. The Institute of Medicine and the CDC agree that damp, moldy indoor environments are associated with respiratory and asthma symptoms and that **moisture should be controlled and the source eliminated** as soon as practical.[^10][^11]

Practical, evidence-aligned guidance coaches can share:

- **Find and fix the moisture source** (leaks, flooding, high humidity) — this is the single most important step.[^11]
- **Clean or remove mold-damaged materials.** The EPA generally advises that non-porous surfaces can be cleaned, while porous materials with extensive mold (such as soaked drywall or carpet) often need removal. For larger areas, professional remediation is appropriate.
- **Improve ventilation and control indoor humidity** (ideally below ~50%).
- **Reduce volatile organic compounds (VOCs)** from solvents, fresh paint, and some cleaning products by ventilating and choosing low-VOC products.

A coach can reasonably help a client *notice* moisture problems and connect them with qualified remediation or medical professionals. A coach should **not** diagnose “mold illness,” interpret specialized mold-toxin lab tests, or recommend medical detox protocols.

-----

## 9. Medical Interventions and Therapies

**Standard antibiotic therapy.** For acute Lyme disease, short courses of antibiotics (commonly doxycycline) are highly effective, curing roughly 90% of early infections.[^21] IDSA guidelines support short courses — generally 10–21 days for early disease, with somewhat longer courses for certain neurologic or cardiac involvement.[^4]

**The long-term antibiotic question — what the major trials found.** This is one of the most important evidence stories in this field, and it deserves careful, fair telling. Several U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH)–sponsored randomized trials tested whether *additional* antibiotics help people with persistent symptoms:

- **Klempner et al. (2001, *New England Journal of Medicine*)** — Two trials (in seropositive and seronegative patients) of 90 days of intravenous then oral antibiotics versus placebo found **no significant difference** in outcomes.[^27]
- **Krupp et al. (2003, STOP-LD, *Neurology*)** — Found that 28 days of IV ceftriaxone improved **fatigue** but not cognition or a lab marker of infection.[^28]
- **Fallon et al. (2008, *Neurology*)** — Found that IV ceftriaxone produced **short-term cognitive improvement** that was **not sustained** after the antibiotic was stopped, and treatment carried adverse events.[^29]
- **Berende et al. (2016, PLEASE trial, *NEJM*)** — A European trial found that longer-term antibiotics did **not** improve quality of life beyond shorter-term treatment.[^30]

Taken together (**gold-standard** evidence), these trials show that prolonged antibiotics generally do **not** produce lasting benefit and do carry risks — which is why mainstream guidelines advise against routine long-term antibiotic therapy.[^30] In fairness, Lyme-focused clinicians point out that a few trials showed short-term improvement in fatigue or cognition, and they argue the trials studied narrow patient groups; they call for better-designed studies.[^4] The reasonable bottom line: the science has **not** been “disproven” in the sense of showing antibiotics never help anyone, but the high-quality evidence does **not** support prolonged antibiotic use as a general strategy, and the burden of proof rests on showing benefit that these trials did not demonstrate.

**The Shoemaker Protocol.** This is a sequential, multi-step program beginning with removal from the moldy environment, then **cholestyramine** (a cholesterol-lowering “binder” used off-label to bind biotoxins), treatment of nasal bacteria (MARCoNS), and later steps including **VIP nasal spray**.[^31] Proponents cite two double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover studies of cholestyramine and report improvements in symptoms and brain-imaging measures.[^31] The evidence level here is **insufficient/contested**: the supporting studies are small, come largely from the originating group, and have not been independently replicated, and mainstream bodies do not recognize a validated treatment for systemic inhaled-mold illness.[^16][^17] A 2024 narrative review in *Annals of Medicine & Surgery* (Dooley, Vukelic & Jim) concluded the Shoemaker Protocol was the only CIRS treatment with “documented clinical efficacy,” but that review was authored by CIRS practitioners and rested on a largely self-referential evidence base, with the authors themselves conceding that “peer-reviewed publications other than Shoemaker’s have been minimal.”[^38]

**Low-dose naltrexone (LDN).** Naltrexone at low doses is being studied for chronic pain and fatigue conditions. It has the **strongest** off-label evidence base for fibromyalgia among these uses, with several small randomized trials.[^32] A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis (Kang et al., *Korean Journal of Pain*; 4 RCTs, 222 patients) found that LDN significantly reduced pain versus placebo (mean difference −0.86, 95% CI −1.20 to −0.51, P<0.001), though some secondary measures showed no significant difference.[^33] The evidence remains **emerging** but promising; LDN is generally well tolerated and is a reasonable physician-supervised option to discuss, not a proven cure for Lyme-related illness.

**Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT).** Some patients report symptom relief, and laboratory studies suggest high oxygen can slow *Borrelia* growth, but there is **no good evidence** that HBOT eliminates infection or produces durable benefit in PTLDS; the human evidence is limited and **contested**.[^34]

## 10. Lifestyle Changes for Long-Term Wellness

For people with persistent post-infectious symptoms, the foundations of wellness genuinely help quality of life.

**Sleep and stress.** Protecting sleep and managing stress are reasonable, low-risk, broadly beneficial goals — areas where a health coach can add real value.

**Pacing and energy management.** For people whose symptoms worsen after exertion (a feature called **post-exertional malaise**), pacing — staying within one’s “energy envelope” — is now the recommended approach for ME/CFS-type illness.

**Exercise — an important caution.** Graded exercise therapy (GET) was once widely recommended for chronic fatigue, based largely on the 2011 PACE trial. That trial’s methods and “recovery” claims were later heavily criticized, and in 2021 the UK’s National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) **stopped recommending GET** for ME/CFS and clarified that cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is not a cure.[^35][^36] The lesson for coaches: for clients with post-exertional malaise, do **not** push fixed, escalating exercise; gentle, flexible, symptom-guided movement and pacing are safer.

**Mind-body approaches.** CBT and mindfulness can help people cope with the distress, anxiety, and depression that accompany chronic illness, and a Cochrane review found CBT can reduce fatigue symptoms compared with usual care.[^37] These should be framed as **supportive** tools for coping and quality of life — not as evidence that the illness is “all in the mind.”

## 11. Genetic and Immunological Factors

Genes shape immune responses. **HLA-DR** gene variants are well established as influencing autoimmune disease risk, and within the CIRS model specific HLA-DR types are proposed to impair biotoxin clearance — a claim that remains **contested** and not independently validated.[^14]

Persistent Lyme symptoms have a real biological footprint in research. A Johns Hopkins brain-imaging study (Coughlin et al., *Journal of Neuroinflammation*, 2018) using [11C]DPA-713 PET found elevated microglial activation (a marker of neuroinflammation) in PTLDS patients compared to healthy controls.[^39] Pre-treatment immune differences also appear to influence who recovers.[^2] Cognitive complaints — memory trouble, slowed thinking, “brain fog” — are among the most disabling and most studied features. This research supports the reality of the symptoms while leaving the precise cause (lingering immune activation, autoimmunity, tissue effects, or persistent infection) still under investigation.

## 12. A Note for Health Coaches: Scope of Practice and Referral

Health coaches and ministry leaders occupy a position of trust — and with it, real responsibility.

**What coaches can do:** Offer encouragement and a listening ear; support healthy nutrition, sleep, stress management, and pacing; help clients prepare questions for their doctors; help them notice home moisture or mold problems; and walk alongside them spiritually and emotionally through a frightening, often isolating illness.

**What coaches should not do:** Diagnose Lyme disease, PTLDS, or CIRS; interpret laboratory tests; recommend, start, or stop antibiotics, supplements, or “detox” protocols; or promise cures.

**When to refer:** Encourage clients with possible acute Lyme (especially a bull’s-eye rash or recent tick bite) to see a physician **promptly**, since early treatment works well. Refer persistent or complex cases to an infectious-disease physician; refer suspected environmental or mold concerns to qualified remediation professionals and physicians with relevant expertise. Always refer warning signs — chest pain, fainting, facial droop, severe neurological symptoms — for urgent medical care.

**Validation without overreach.** Perhaps the most healing thing a coach can offer is to say, with integrity: “I believe your suffering is real, I will support you, and I will help you stay connected to good medical care.” That stance honors both compassion and truth.

-----

## A Closing Pastoral Word

People living with persistent symptoms after Lyme disease — or who believe their environment has made them ill — often feel dismissed, exhausted, and alone. Some have spent years and large sums searching for answers. As caregivers in a faith community, we can hold two truths at once: deep compassion for genuine suffering, and honesty about what science does and does not yet know. We can refuse both careless dismissal and false promises. In doing so, we serve people well — in body, mind, and spirit.
