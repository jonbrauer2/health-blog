## A patch you can feel before you see

Run your fingertips slowly across the back of a sun-weathered hand, the edge of an ear, or the bald crown of a scalp, and you may catch the faintest snag of rough skin — like sandpaper hiding under your touch. That tiny patch has a name. It is called an **actinic keratosis**, or AK, and it is one of the most common skin findings on the planet. A 2024 systematic review pooling data from 60 studies estimated the global point prevalence of AK at roughly **14% of adults**, with prevalence rising steeply with age and outdoor sun exposure.[^1] In the United States, AK is the most common dermatologic diagnosis in patients aged 45 and older, with an estimated 58 million Americans affected.[^2] In subtropical Queensland, Australia, the figure climbs higher still — up to 83% of men in their sixties.[^3]

Most people who carry these patches do not know what they are. Some assume the rough spots are simply old age, dry skin, or sun damage that will fade. Others worry they are something far worse. The truth lies in between. AKs are precancerous lesions — early signposts that the cells in your skin have absorbed more ultraviolet light over a lifetime than they could comfortably repair. A small fraction will, if left alone, eventually progress to squamous cell carcinoma, an invasive skin cancer.[^4] Most will not. But because we cannot tell with the naked eye which lesion belongs to which group, every AK deserves attention.

The good news threaded through this guide is that there is a lot you can do — starting today. Understanding what AK is, what it looks like, how it is treated, and how to lower your risk turns a frightening unknown into something practical and manageable.

---

## What actinic keratosis actually is

An actinic keratosis is a rough, scaly patch or thin plaque that forms on skin that has spent decades absorbing sunlight. Dermatologists describe AKs as ranging from barely-there flat pink spots to thick, crusty bumps that look almost like small horns.[^5] Color varies — pink, red, tan, brown, or skin-colored — and lesions often share an erythematous (reddened) base with a dry, adherent scale on top. They tend to appear in predictable places: the face, lips, ears, balding scalp, the V of the chest, the backs of the hands, and the forearms. In other words, wherever the sun reaches most often and longest.[^5]

AKs are easy to confuse with other common skin findings. Seborrheic keratoses look "stuck on," waxy, and well-defined; they are benign and have no precancerous potential. Common warts often show tiny black dots from clotted capillaries. A basal cell carcinoma typically has a pearly, translucent quality with visible small blood vessels, while a squamous cell carcinoma is usually thicker, firmer, and may ulcerate or bleed.[^5] The line between a thick AK and an early in-situ squamous cell carcinoma can be blurry even for pathologists — which is part of why these lesions matter.[^6]

> **Key Fact:** AKs exist on a spectrum, from thin and barely felt to thick and hyperkeratotic. The yearly risk that any single AK will progress to invasive squamous cell carcinoma is small — most estimates put it between **0.025% and 16% per lesion per year**, with rigorous studies suggesting around 0.5% to 2.5% over four years — but the cumulative risk grows when a person carries many lesions over many years. Roughly **65% of all squamous cell carcinomas** appear to arise from previously existing AKs.[^4][^7][^8]

The biology behind AK is, in a real sense, a story of accumulated paperwork errors. Ultraviolet B radiation, the same kind that causes sunburn, is absorbed directly by DNA in skin cells, creating chemical lesions called cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers. Over years of exposure, some of those errors slip past the cell's repair machinery and produce signature UV mutations, especially in a critical tumor-suppressor gene called **p53**.[^9] Roughly two-thirds of AK lesions show p53 mutations, and patches of p53-mutated cells exist quietly in normal-looking sun-exposed skin long before any lesion becomes visible.[^10] This insight gave rise to the modern concept of **field cancerization** — first proposed by Slaughter and colleagues in 1953 and refined genetically by Braakhuis and colleagues in 2003 — which holds that an entire region of chronically sun-damaged skin contains many mutated cells, only a few of which manifest as a clinically visible AK.[^11][^12][^13]

Risk factors line up with that biology. Fair skin (Fitzpatrick types I and II) carries the highest risk, along with light eyes and a history of freckling or burning easily. Cumulative lifetime sun exposure matters more than any single sunburn, though a history of five or more blistering sunburns sharply raises risk. Age over 50, male sex, living closer to the equator, outdoor occupations, and a history of immunosuppression — particularly organ transplantation — all push risk higher.[^1][^14]

## How to recognize an AK on your own skin

The phrase dermatologists use most often is also the most useful: AKs are usually **felt before they are seen**. A thin AK can be nearly invisible — a flat pink area no larger than a pencil eraser — yet your fingertip will catch the scale every time. That gritty, sandpaper sensation is the single most reliable clue available to a non-expert.[^5]

> **Key Fact:** Many AKs are easier to feel than to see. Light fingertip pressure across sun-exposed skin will often catch a rough patch your eyes would miss. If a spot keeps regrowing the same scale week after week in the same place, take note.

Beyond the rough patch itself, certain features signal that an AK may be evolving into something more serious. Dermatologists summarize them with the acronym IDRBEU: **I**nduration (firmness when you press on it), **D**iameter greater than one centimeter, **R**apid enlargement, **B**leeding, **E**rythema (deep redness), and **U**lceration.[^15] Pain or tenderness, persistent itching, and a lesion that simply will not heal also belong on this list. Any of these features warrant prompt evaluation, because they overlap with the warning signs of invasive squamous cell carcinoma.

Self-monitoring matters because change over time is more informative than any single snapshot. A simple habit — examining your skin once a month under good light, with a hand mirror for the back and scalp — lets you notice new spots, vanished spots, or spots that are slowly transforming. Photographs of stubborn lesions taken on a smartphone provide a record you and your clinician can compare later. In the clinic, dermatologists may use dermoscopy (a handheld magnifier with polarized light) to look for the "strawberry pattern" of vessels and follicular plugging characteristic of AK, or perform a small punch or shave biopsy when the diagnosis is uncertain or invasive cancer is suspected.[^16]

## Treatment options worth knowing about

When it comes to treating AK, there is no single best answer. The right choice depends on how many lesions are present, how thick they are, where they sit, the patient's skin type, immune status, tolerance for downtime, and access to a dermatologist. The major options each have a different center of gravity in the evidence.

**Cryotherapy** with liquid nitrogen — a quick spray that flash-freezes the lesion to around –50°C — remains the workhorse for isolated AKs. A landmark prospective study by Thai and colleagues reported a complete-response rate of about **67%** overall, rising to 83% when the freeze lasted more than 20 seconds.[^17] Cryotherapy is fast, inexpensive, and effective for individual lesions, but it does nothing for the subclinical field of damaged cells surrounding them. Common side effects include pain at the moment of freezing, blistering, and lasting hypopigmentation, particularly in darker skin tones.[^17]

**Topical 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)** is a cream that works as a thymidylate synthase inhibitor, selectively poisoning rapidly dividing atypical keratinocytes while sparing most normal skin. It treats the entire field, not just visible lesions. In the Veterans Affairs Keratinocyte Carcinoma Chemoprevention Trial, a single two- to four-week course of 5% 5-FU produced a striking **75% reduction in squamous cell carcinomas** requiring surgery during the following year.[^18] The trade-off is real: 5-FU produces dramatic erythema, erosion, and crusting during treatment that patients sometimes find alarming. Counseling about the expected inflammatory reaction is essential.

**Imiquimod**, available in 5% and 3.75% strengths, takes a different route — it activates Toll-like receptor 7, recruiting the immune system to attack atypical cells. Pivotal trials show complete clearance in roughly 48–55% of treated patients with the 5% formulation.[^19] Like 5-FU, imiquimod produces local inflammation; flu-like symptoms from interferon induction are not unusual but resolve when treatment ends.

**Ingenol mebutate** deserves a careful, honest mention. Originally derived from the sap of *Euphorbia peplus* and marketed as Picato, it produced rapid lesion clearance with just two or three days of application.[^20] In 2020, however, the European Medicines Agency suspended and then withdrew its marketing authorization after a three-year safety study found a higher rate of skin malignancies — particularly squamous cell carcinoma — in the treatment area among ingenol mebutate users compared with imiquimod users (6.3% versus 2.0%).[^21] The manufacturer voluntarily withdrew the product in the United States the same year. The withdrawal is a meaningful safety signal, not a verdict on any single patient who used the medication, and the regulatory consensus is that the risks outweighed the benefits at population level.[^21]

**Diclofenac 3% gel** uses COX-2 inhibition to reduce the prostaglandin signaling that supports atypical cell growth. It is gentler than 5-FU but slower, requiring twice-daily application for 60 to 90 days, with complete clearance rates around 33–50%.[^22] For patients who cannot tolerate the inflammatory storm of stronger field therapies, diclofenac offers a reasonable, well-tolerated alternative.

**Photodynamic therapy (PDT)** is the field-treatment heavyweight. A photosensitizing prodrug — usually 5-aminolevulinic acid or methyl aminolevulinate — is applied to the skin, where it concentrates inside dysplastic keratinocytes and converts to protoporphyrin IX. Activation by red or blue light then generates reactive oxygen species that selectively destroy abnormal cells. Long-term follow-up of two phase III trials showed sustained clearance rates near 47% at twelve months with excellent cosmetic outcomes.[^23] Daylight PDT, in which natural sunlight serves as the activating source, has been shown non-inferior to conventional clinic-based PDT and is considerably less painful.[^24] Cost and access remain barriers.

For unusually thick, widespread, or treatment-refractory disease, **ablative laser resurfacing, dermabrasion, and curettage** can be useful as second- or third-line options under specialist care.[^25]

> **Key Fact:** Treating a single visible AK without addressing the surrounding sun-damaged skin misses the bigger picture. The concept of **field cancerization** — that mutated cells exist in a wider zone than the eye can see — is why dermatologists increasingly favor field-directed therapies like 5-FU, imiquimod, or photodynamic therapy when multiple AKs cluster in the same region.[^11][^13]

Network meta-analyses comparing these options generally place photodynamic therapy and 5-FU among the most effective field treatments, with imiquimod close behind, while cryotherapy remains the standout for isolated, well-defined lesions.[^26][^27] Choice is always individualized.

## Sunscreen, sun protection, and primary prevention

If treatment is about cleaning up the damage already done, sun protection is about preventing the next chapter. The evidence here is among the most solid in dermatology. The landmark **Nambour Skin Cancer Prevention Trial**, conducted by Green and colleagues in subtropical Queensland, randomized 1,621 adults to either daily SPF 15+ broad-spectrum sunscreen or discretionary use over four and a half years. Daily users developed significantly fewer new AKs and significantly fewer squamous cell carcinomas.[^28] Follow-up eight years after the trial ended showed a sustained **40% reduction in SCC tumors** in the daily-sunscreen group, and a later analysis demonstrated a roughly **50% reduction in melanoma**.[^29][^30]

> **Key Fact:** In the Australian Nambour trial, adults randomized to daily broad-spectrum sunscreen developed about 24% fewer actinic keratoses than those using sunscreen at their discretion, with long-term reductions in squamous cell carcinoma of around 40% and in invasive melanoma of around 50%.[^28][^29][^30]

Modern guidance from the American Academy of Dermatology and most professional societies recommends a broad-spectrum sunscreen of at least SPF 30, applied generously (about an ounce for the whole body) and reapplied every two hours when outdoors, and after swimming or heavy sweating.[^31] Most people apply only 20–50% of the amount needed for the labeled SPF — under-application is the rule, not the exception.

The mineral-versus-chemical sunscreen debate deserves a fair hearing on both sides. **Mineral sunscreens**, based on zinc oxide and titanium dioxide, work largely by absorbing ultraviolet light and to a lesser extent by reflecting it. They are well tolerated, suitable for sensitive skin, and have been classified by the FDA as **Generally Recognized as Safe and Effective (GRASE)**.[^32] **Chemical sunscreens** — including avobenzone, oxybenzone, octocrylene, octinoxate, homosalate, and octisalate — absorb UV through aromatic chemistry and feel cosmetically lighter. Two important FDA studies by Matta and colleagues, published in JAMA in 2019 and 2020, demonstrated that several of these ingredients are absorbed through the skin into the bloodstream at concentrations above the FDA's 0.5 ng/mL threshold for further safety testing, often after a single application, with detectable levels persisting for weeks.[^33][^34] The FDA was careful to emphasize that **systemic absorption is not the same as harm**: under its current GRASE framework, twelve common chemical filters lack sufficient data to confirm safety and effectiveness, but they have not been judged unsafe either.[^32] The agency has asked for more research, and reformulation is ongoing.

The environmental conversation is a separate question. Hawaii banned the sale of sunscreens containing oxybenzone and octinoxate in 2021 because of coral reef concerns, not human health concerns.[^35] Both stories are worth knowing, and neither one should be used to dismiss the other. The most defensible practical position is that **a sunscreen worn consistently is far better than one avoided**, and the FDA's currently confirmed GRASE ingredients — zinc oxide and titanium dioxide — are reasonable defaults for anyone who wants to err on the side of caution.

Sunscreen is one tool among several. UPF-rated clothing, wide-brimmed hats, sunglasses, shade, and time-of-day choices (the sun is strongest from 10 a.m. to 2 p.m.) provide layered protection that no cream can match alone. For people with significant AK burden, behavioral sun avoidance often matters more than the brand of bottle on the bathroom counter.

A word about vitamin D. Sunlight is the body's main natural source of vitamin D, and many people worry that diligent sun protection will leave them deficient. The most rigorous review of this question, by Neale and colleagues in 2019, examined four experimental studies, three field trials, and 69 observational studies — and concluded that in real-world conditions, sunscreen use does not meaningfully reduce serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D.[^36] A 2025 Australian randomized trial reached the same conclusion.[^37] Diet (fatty fish, fortified milk, egg yolks) and modest oral supplementation (typically 600–2,000 IU per day for most adults) reliably maintain healthy levels without requiring unprotected sun exposure.[^38] For people at elevated skin cancer risk, this is the safer path.

## Nutrition, diet, and skin health

Food does not replace sunscreen, but the inside of the body matters too. Some of the most interesting research in dermatology over the last decade has come from nutrition trials, with results ranging from genuinely exciting to sobering.

The standout finding belongs to **nicotinamide**, the amide form of vitamin B3 (also called niacinamide — distinct from niacin, which causes flushing). In the **ONTRAC trial**, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2015, Chen and colleagues randomized 386 patients who had each had at least two non-melanoma skin cancers in the prior five years to either 500 mg of oral nicotinamide twice daily or placebo for twelve months. The results were striking: a **23% reduction in new non-melanoma skin cancers** and a **13% reduction in actinic keratoses** at twelve months, with no meaningful safety concerns.[^39] The benefit faded six months after stopping the supplement, suggesting nicotinamide must be taken continuously to maintain its effect. Mechanistically, nicotinamide replenishes cellular NAD+ and ATP that UV exposure depletes, accelerates DNA repair, and prevents UV-induced immunosuppression.[^40][^41]

> **Key Fact:** Oral nicotinamide at **500 mg twice daily** is one of the most accessible, well-studied, and inexpensive nutritional interventions for adults at high risk of AK and non-melanoma skin cancer. The Chen 2015 ONTRAC trial showed a 23% reduction in new skin cancers and a 13% reduction in AKs over twelve months in immunocompetent high-risk patients.[^39] A follow-up trial in organ transplant recipients (ONTRANS, 2023) did not show the same benefit, suggesting nicotinamide's role may be most clear in immunocompetent patients with significant sun damage.[^42]

Talking to a clinician before starting nicotinamide is sensible — the dose is specific, and the niacinamide form must be distinguished from the flushing-prone niacin form. But few interventions in this field combine such low cost, low risk, and credible randomized-trial support.

**Antioxidant vitamins C and E** offer a more modest story. Small trials by Eberlein-König and others showed that oral combinations of about 2 g vitamin C and 1,000 IU vitamin E daily raised the minimal erythema dose (the UV dose needed to produce sunburn) by roughly 20–40% over several weeks.[^43][^44] Topical vitamin C and E formulations reduce UV-induced DNA damage in human skin.[^45] Neither has been shown in large trials to prevent AK or skin cancer directly, but dietary intake of these vitamins — through citrus, peppers, berries, leafy greens, nuts, and seeds — is unlikely to hurt and contributes to broader health.

**Beta-carotene** is the cautionary tale. Greenberg and colleagues randomized 1,805 patients with prior non-melanoma skin cancer to 50 mg of beta-carotene daily or placebo and found **no benefit** for preventing new skin cancers.[^46] More alarmingly, the **ATBC trial** in nearly 30,000 Finnish male smokers reported an 18% increase in lung cancer incidence among those taking 20 mg of beta-carotene daily.[^47] The **CARET trial** in smokers and asbestos-exposed workers was halted nearly two years early when the active arm showed a 28% increase in lung cancer and 17% higher all-cause mortality.[^48] The takeaway is precise: dietary carotenoids from whole foods (tomatoes, carrots, leafy greens, sweet potatoes) are part of a healthy pattern, but **isolated high-dose beta-carotene supplements should be avoided**, especially by current or former smokers.

**Omega-3 fatty acids** from oily fish and supplementation show interesting but preliminary results. Pilkington and Rhodes have demonstrated in small human trials that oral EPA-rich supplementation reduces UV-induced erythema, lowers p53 expression in irradiated skin, and dampens photoimmunosuppression — surrogate markers, not cancer outcomes.[^49][^50] The large VITAL trial found no overall cancer reduction from marine omega-3 supplementation, though skin cancer was not a primary endpoint.[^51] The evidence supports omega-3 as part of an anti-inflammatory diet; it does not yet support it as a stand-alone AK preventive.

**Polyphenols and green tea catechins** (especially EGCG) have impressive preclinical credentials — they reduce UV-induced DNA damage, oxidative stress, and immunosuppression in mouse and cell models — but human evidence remains limited and mostly focused on short-term endpoints like erythema reduction.[^52][^53] Worth knowing about, not worth overclaiming.

Broader **dietary patterns** matter. In a prospective study of more than 67,000 French women, higher Mediterranean-diet adherence was associated with a **17% lower risk of overall skin cancer**, with significant reductions in melanoma and basal cell carcinoma but not in squamous cell carcinoma.[^54] By contrast, an Australian cohort found that a "meat and fat" dietary pattern roughly doubled the risk of new SCCs, and nearly quadrupled the risk in patients with prior skin cancer.[^55] Plant-forward eating, abundant in colorful vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and olive oil, is supported by these and many other studies as a low-risk way to reduce oxidative stress and overall cancer risk.

**Alcohol** has emerged as an independent risk factor. A 2017 meta-analysis by Yen and colleagues found that each additional 10 g of ethanol per day (roughly one standard drink) increased BCC risk by 7% and SCC risk by 11%.[^56] A large European cohort study confirmed dose-response associations with all three major skin cancers.[^57] Acetaldehyde, the first metabolite of alcohol, is a recognized carcinogen, and alcohol both increases photosensitivity and dampens cutaneous immune surveillance.

A surprising bright spot: **coffee**. Pooled data from the Nurses' Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-up Study (more than 110,000 participants) found that those drinking three or more cups of caffeinated coffee per day had about a **20% lower risk of basal cell carcinoma**.[^58] Decaffeinated coffee showed no effect, suggesting caffeine itself is the active compound. No association with SCC or melanoma was found, so this is a modest, BCC-specific signal — interesting, but not a reason to start drinking coffee for skin protection.

## Lifestyle factors that shape risk

Beyond food and sunscreen, several life circumstances shape AK risk in important ways.

**Immunosuppression** sits at the top of the list. Solid organ transplant recipients carry a **65- to 100-fold increased risk of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma** compared with the general population, with some sources citing rates as high as 250-fold for lung and heart recipients.[^59][^60] AKs in transplant patients appear in greater numbers, behave more aggressively, and progress to SCC at substantially higher rates.[^61] Patients on chronic immunosuppression for autoimmune disease, those with advanced HIV, and recipients of biologic therapies face elevated risk as well, though the magnitude is typically smaller. For anyone in these categories, dermatologic care is not optional — it is a core part of post-transplant or chronic-disease management, with most specialty groups recommending at least annual full-skin examinations and often more frequent visits.[^62]

**Smoking** has been linked in a careful 2012 meta-analysis to a **52% increase in cutaneous SCC risk**, with no clear effect on BCC.[^63] Mechanisms include direct carcinogen exposure, impaired wound healing, reduced cutaneous immune surveillance, and increased oxidative stress. The AK-specific evidence is suggestive rather than definitive, but the SCC story is solid enough that smoking cessation belongs on any skin-health checklist.

**Outdoor occupational exposure** is the single largest preventable driver of cumulative UV dose. Farmers, construction workers, fishermen, lifeguards, postal workers, landscape workers, ski instructors, and outdoor athletes all carry significantly elevated risk.[^64][^65] A systematic review of AK-specific risk factors found that chronic occupational or recreational sun exposure tripled AK risk.[^66] In 2015, Germany formally recognized squamous cell carcinoma and **multiple actinic keratoses caused by natural UV radiation as a compensable occupational disease (BK 5103)** — a regulatory acknowledgment of the harm that decades of outdoor work can cause.[^67] Workplace sun-protection programs, broad-brimmed hats, UPF clothing, and shaded breaks are not luxuries for outdoor workers; they are essential equipment.

**Exercise**, although deeply beneficial for overall health and immune function, has no direct trial evidence as a preventive against AK. The complication is that outdoor exercise increases cumulative UV exposure unless paired with deliberate sun protection. Indoor and shaded outdoor exercise carry no such trade-off.

**Sleep and stress** affect immune function and DNA repair through plausible mechanisms, but no high-quality studies have directly linked either to AK incidence. Framing them honestly: adequate sleep and stress management support general immune health, with limited direct evidence for AK-specific benefit.

**Hydration and skin barrier care** support overall skin health but should not be considered preventive treatments for AK.

## Navigating common controversies

Searches for "natural cures" for AK return a flood of products and protocols. Most lack supporting evidence; one in particular causes documented harm.

**Bloodroot (Sanguinaria canadensis) and black salve products demand explicit caution.** Black salve is a corrosive escharotic that typically contains sanguinarine and zinc chloride. It causes non-selective tissue necrosis — destroying healthy and abnormal tissue alike — and has been associated in the peer-reviewed literature with severe disfigurement, including loss of the nasal ala, full-thickness facial defects requiring multi-stage reconstructive surgery, deep eschars on the face and neck, and cellulitis requiring hospitalization.[^68][^69][^70][^71] A comprehensive 2017 review by Croaker and colleagues documented that among 25 published case reports, roughly 20% of patients suffered significant facial deformity, with at least two reported deaths from cancers that progressed after self-treatment masked their true extent.[^72] The U.S. Food and Drug Administration lists black salve and Cansema among its illegally marketed cancer treatments and has issued multiple warning letters; Australia's Therapeutic Goods Administration has done the same.[^73] **This is not a coaching-appropriate option, and it is not a debate.** Anyone considering it should be redirected — gently but firmly — to a dermatologist.

**Apple cider vinegar** is a different case. There is no peer-reviewed evidence that it treats AK, and chemical burns have been documented when undiluted vinegar is applied under occlusion, particularly in children.[^74][^75] Brief, dilute contact is unlikely to cause major harm, but there is no therapeutic rationale for using it on AK, and any reliance on it could delay proper care.

**Do-it-yourself freezing kits** sold over the counter raise two concerns. First, they cannot match the controlled depth and temperature of clinical cryotherapy. Second, and more importantly, a person cannot biopsy their own skin — meaning a lesion treated as an AK might actually be an invasive squamous or basal cell carcinoma that needs different management. False reassurance is a real risk.

**Essential oils and herbal topicals** have not been adequately studied for AK. Some carry their own risks, including allergic contact dermatitis and, in the case of citrus-derived oils containing furocoumarins, paradoxical phototoxicity.

**Intentional sun exposure or tanning beds as treatment** has no evidence base whatsoever and is contraindicated. Tanning devices are classified as Group 1 carcinogens by the International Agency for Research on Cancer.[^76] The very exposure that creates AKs cannot also treat them.

Finally, the **vitamin D question** deserves a compassionate answer. Adequate vitamin D matters for bone, immune, and overall health, and a desire to maintain it is reasonable. But the evidence is now clear that real-world sunscreen use does not meaningfully impair vitamin D status, and that supplementation and dietary sources can fully meet the body's needs without contributing to skin cancer risk.[^36][^37] For people at elevated risk of AK, supplementation is the safer route. Talking with a clinician about checking serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D and adjusting intake is a simple step that respects both concerns.

## When to see a clinician

Some lesions can be watched. Others should be evaluated promptly. The line between the two is not always obvious, which is why a low threshold for professional evaluation is sensible. Specific triggers warrant a dermatology visit:

- Any rough, scaly, or unusual skin lesion that persists more than a few weeks
- Any lesion that bleeds, crusts, grows rapidly, becomes painful, or develops firmness underneath
- Any known AK that changes in color, size, thickness, or symptoms
- Multiple AKs in the same area, suggesting field cancerization
- Any new or changing lesion in a person who is immunosuppressed, has had an organ transplant, or has a prior history of skin cancer
- Lesions on cosmetically or functionally sensitive sites such as the lips, eyelids, ears, or nose
- Any lesion about which the patient — or the person supporting them — feels uncertain

When in doubt, refer. A short visit to a dermatologist is one of the lowest-risk, highest-information steps in all of medicine, and early evaluation of a suspicious lesion can be quite literally life-changing.

## Putting it all together

The story of AK is, in the end, a story about cumulative choices — most of them small, most of them within reach.

Sun protection becomes a daily rhythm rather than a vacation accessory: broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher, applied in the morning to face, ears, neck, and the backs of hands, with a hat tucked into the car for outdoor errands. UPF-rated long sleeves on bright days, sunglasses, shade-seeking during midday hours. None of this requires perfection. Consistency beats intensity.

Monthly self-examinations take about ten minutes. Stand in front of a mirror with another hand mirror for the back, scalp, and ears, and run your fingertips slowly over sun-exposed skin. Photograph anything that catches your fingernail. Watch what changes over the next month.

Diet does not need to be elaborate. A plant-forward pattern with abundant vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, fish, and olive oil supports skin and general health alike. Moderating alcohol and avoiding tobacco lowers risk further. Coffee, for those who already enjoy it, may offer a small bonus.

For higher-risk individuals — those with significant sun damage, prior skin cancers, or numerous AKs — a conversation with a clinician about **oral nicotinamide 500 mg twice daily** is worth having. It is inexpensive, well-studied, and supported by a randomized trial in the *New England Journal of Medicine* showing meaningful reductions in both AKs and non-melanoma skin cancers in immunocompetent patients.

And for anyone fair-skinned, long sun-exposed, or carrying lesions they have never had examined, a baseline dermatology visit is one of the simplest, most useful health investments available.

> **Key Fact:** Caring for skin is not vanity. The skin is the body's largest organ, its first line of defense, and the most visible record of how we have lived. Tending to it — protecting it from harm, paying attention to its changes, asking for help when needed — is one of the quieter ways a person honors the body they have been given.

A small rough patch on the back of a hand may seem like nothing. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it is the body's earliest, kindest warning. Either way, knowing what to look for, what to do, and when to ask for help turns a frightening unknown into something manageable — and that is the entire point.
