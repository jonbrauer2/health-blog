## TL;DR

- Migraine is a complex neurological disease — not “just a headache” — affecting roughly 1.16 billion people worldwide and about 39 million Americans, and ranks among the top causes of disability on Earth, hitting women two to three times as often as men.[^1][^2][^9]
- Most people do best with a layered plan: identify and reduce personal triggers, build protective daily habits (steady sleep, regular meals, hydration, exercise, stress skills), use the right acute medication early, and add prevention — modern CGRP drugs, older preventives, Botox, supplements, or devices — when attacks are frequent.[^16][^17][^21]
- A few things deserve real caution: medication overuse can worsen headaches, combined estrogen birth control raises stroke risk in people who have migraine with aura, butterbur must be “PA-free,” and certain “red flag” headaches need emergency care.[^15][^25][^20][^22]

## Key Findings

- **Migraine is a brain disease with a well-mapped biology.** A wave of electrical change called cortical spreading depression underlies aura, and activation of the trigeminal nerve system with release of a protein called CGRP drives the pain. This is why the newest and most effective drugs target CGRP directly.[^3][^4][^5]
- **Triggers and root causes are different things.** Triggers (a glass of red wine, a skipped meal, a storm front) set off an attack in a brain that is already vulnerable. Root causes (genetics, low magnesium, poor sleep architecture, hormonal shifts, central sensitization) lower the threshold so triggers work more easily.[^10][^11]
- **The strongest “natural” evidence is for magnesium, riboflavin (B2), and coenzyme Q10.** These are inexpensive, generally safe, and supported by randomized trials, though benefits are modest and build over 2–3 months.[^11][^12][^13]
- **Behavioral therapies rival drugs.** Biofeedback, relaxation training, CBT, and aerobic exercise each have real evidence and no medication side effects.[^18][^19]
- **Newer tools have widened the toolbox.** CGRP monoclonal antibodies and gepants, plus FDA-cleared neuromodulation devices, give options to people who can’t tolerate or shouldn’t take older drugs — though cost and access are real barriers.[^16][^17][^24]

## Details

### 1. More Than “Just a Headache”

If you live with migraine, you already know the phrase “it’s just a headache” lands like a slap. A migraine is not a bad headache; it is a neurological event that can swallow a day or three of your life, complete with throbbing pain, nausea, a brain that can’t tolerate light or sound, and a hangover afterward that leaves you wrung out.

The scale is staggering. The Global Burden of Disease 2021 study estimated that about 1.16 billion people worldwide live with migraine, up from 733 million in 1990.[ ^1] In the United States, migraine affects roughly 39 million people, according to the American Migraine Foundation,  and the best peer-reviewed government-survey data show U.S. prevalence of about 20.7% in women versus 9.7% in men.[ ^2][^9] It is one of the most disabling conditions on the planet — migraine ranks as a leading cause of years lived with disability, and among women aged 15–49 it is one of the single biggest causes of disability of any kind.[ ^1]

Migraine is also underdiagnosed and undertreated. Many people never get a formal diagnosis, are told to “tough it out,” or cycle through over-the-counter pills that slowly make things worse. It disproportionately affects women, and research suggests people of color are less likely to receive a migraine diagnosis or be prescribed migraine-specific medication, even though the disease occurs across all groups.[^9] If you’ve felt unseen by the medical system, you’re not imagining it — and you’re not alone.

The good news: we understand migraine better than ever, and the options for managing it have expanded dramatically in the last decade.

### 2. What Is Actually Happening in a Migraine?

For a long time, migraine was thought to be mainly a “blood vessel” problem — vessels widening and throbbing. We now understand it primarily as a brain disorder in which pain-signaling networks become overexcited. The vascular changes are part of the story, but they’re downstream of what the nervous system is doing.[^3][^4]

**Cortical spreading depression (CSD).** First described by the Brazilian physiologist Aristides Leão in 1944, CSD is a slow wave of intense electrical activity that rolls across the surface of the brain, followed by a wave of quieting.[ ^3] This wave is the physical basis of the visual aura some people see, and brain imaging has confirmed that blood-flow changes during aura match the pattern and timing of CSD.[ ^3] CSD involves a surge of potassium and the neurotransmitter glutamate outside cells, and it appears to trigger the pain system.[ ^4]

**Trigeminal nerve activation and CGRP.** The trigeminal nerve carries sensation from your face and the membranes around your brain (the meninges).  When this system is activated, nerve endings release inflammatory chemicals — most importantly calcitonin gene-related peptide, or CGRP. CGRP is a potent blood-vessel dilator and a key driver of migraine pain and inflammation.[^4] The neurologist Peter Goadsby and colleagues first documented that CGRP is elevated in cranial venous blood during migraine attacks in 1990, and it has since become the central treatment target.[^5]

**Why “neurological,” not “vascular.”** Because the trigger is electrical and chemical activity in brain and nerve tissue — not simply blood vessels misbehaving — migraine is classified as a neurological disorder. The blood-vessel changes happen because the nerves tell them to.[^3][^4]

**The four phases.** Not everyone gets all four, and they can overlap:[^6]

- *Prodrome* (hours to two days before): yawning, food cravings, irritability, fatigue, neck stiffness, trouble concentrating. About 8 in 10 people with migraine notice prodrome symptoms, which can serve as an early warning to act.
- *Aura* (usually 20–60 minutes): visual zigzags, blind spots, shimmering lights; sometimes tingling, numbness, or speech trouble. Only about a quarter to a third of people with migraine experience aura.
- *Headache* (4–72 hours): the classic throbbing pain, often one-sided, with nausea and sensitivity to light and sound.
- *Postdrome* (up to a day or two): the “migraine hangover” — drained, foggy, tender — though a few people feel oddly euphoric.

**Why some people get aura and some don’t** isn’t fully understood, but it likely reflects differences in how susceptible a given brain is to cortical spreading depression.[^3] Aura matters clinically because migraine *with* aura carries some different risk considerations (see the section on birth control).

**Episodic vs. chronic.** Episodic migraine means 14 or fewer headache days per month. Chronic migraine means 15 or more headache days per month for at least three months, with at least eight having migraine features.[ ^6] Episodic migraine can “transform” into chronic migraine — especially when attacks aren’t treated well or acute medications are overused.[^6]

### 3. Who Gets Migraines — and Why?

**Genetics.** Migraine runs in families. If a parent has migraine, your odds go up; most migraine is “polygenic,” meaning many small genetic variations add up to a more excitable, more sensitive nervous system.

**Why women are hit harder.** Before puberty, boys and girls have similar rates. After puberty, women pull far ahead — roughly two to three times the prevalence.[ ^9] The key player is estrogen. Estrogen influences the migraine brain in several ways: it increases cortical excitability and lowers the threshold for cortical spreading depression, it ramps up CGRP production and release in trigeminal nerve cells, and it modulates pain-processing networks.[ ^7] It’s not high or low estrogen per se that’s the problem — it’s the *drop*.[^7][^8]

**The migraine threshold.** A useful mental model: imagine a cup that fills with various stressors — poor sleep, a skipped meal, hormonal shifts, weather, stress. When the cup overflows, you get an attack. This is why a trigger that’s harmless one day causes an attack on another day; it depends on how full the cup already is. Raising your threshold (through sleep, nutrition, stress management, and prevention) means it takes more to tip you over.

**Comorbidities.** Migraine travels with company. Anxiety and depression are the most common psychiatric companions, and the relationship is bidirectional.[^10] Migraine is also linked with fibromyalgia, irritable bowel syndrome, and sleep disorders. These aren’t coincidences — they share underlying mechanisms involving how the nervous system processes pain and regulates itself.

**Across a lifetime.** Migraine often begins in adolescence or young adulthood, peaks in the 30s and 40s, and for many women eases after menopause once hormone levels stabilize.[^8] But the perimenopausal years can be rocky.

### 4. The Trigger Landscape

A crucial reframing: triggers don’t “cause” migraine; they set off attacks in a brain that’s already predisposed. Triggers are also highly individual, and many people overestimate some triggers and miss others. Keeping a trigger diary (logging sleep, meals, stress, hormones, weather, and attacks) can reveal patterns — but be aware of two limitations: some “triggers” are actually early prodrome symptoms (craving chocolate, for instance, may be the migraine starting, not the chocolate causing it), and triggers often need to stack up before they tip you over.[^6]

#### Dietary and Nutritional Triggers

- **Tyramine-containing foods** (aged cheeses, cured meats, some fermented foods): tyramine can affect blood vessels and neurotransmitter release. Evidence is mixed and individual.
- **Caffeine** is double-edged: small amounts can *help* abort an attack (which is why it’s in some combination pain relievers), but regular heavy use and especially *withdrawal* can trigger headaches.
- **Alcohol**, especially red wine and beer. (See the controversies section for the red-wine debate.)
- **Aspartame and MSG**: frequently blamed, but the controlled evidence is weak and inconsistent.
- **Skipping meals / fasting / blood sugar swings**: a well-recognized and common trigger; the migraine brain dislikes energy dips.
- **Dehydration**: a common, easily fixable contributor.

#### Hormonal Triggers

- **Menstrual migraine**: attacks clustering from about two days before to three days after the start of bleeding. The leading explanation is the **estrogen withdrawal hypothesis** — the sharp premenstrual drop in estrogen triggers attacks. First proposed by Brian Somerville in 1972, it’s widely accepted, though researchers note the supporting evidence has methodological limits.[^7][^8] Menstrual attacks tend to be more severe, longer, and harder to treat.[^7]
- **Oral contraceptives and HRT**: genuinely contested. They can help some people (by smoothing hormonal fluctuations) and worsen others. The hormone-free week of traditional pills can trigger estrogen-withdrawal attacks.[^7]
- **Perimenopause and menopause**: the erratic hormone swings of perimenopause often worsen migraine before things calm down post-menopause.[^8]

#### Environmental and Sensory Triggers

- **Weather and barometric pressure**: many people are certain weather affects them; the research is real but messy, with pressure changes the most-studied factor.
- **Bright, flickering, or fluorescent light; screens**: light sensitivity is part of migraine biology, and light is both a symptom and a trigger.
- **Strong odors** (perfume, smoke, chemicals): called osmophobia; common and underappreciated.
- **Altitude changes**: can provoke attacks, partly via oxygen and pressure changes.

#### Sleep-Related Triggers

- **Too little *and* too much sleep** can both trigger attacks; the migraine brain craves regularity.
- **Sleep apnea** is an underrecognized driver — if you snore, wake unrefreshed, or have morning headaches, it’s worth evaluating.
- **Circadian disruption** (shift work, jet lag, irregular schedules) destabilizes the system.

#### Stress and Nervous-System Triggers

- **Acute stress** and the **“letdown” migraine**: attacks often hit *after* stress eases — the Saturday-after-a-hard-week phenomenon.
- **Chronic stress** contributes to central sensitization (see root causes).
- **Trauma and adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)**: a striking and growing body of research links childhood adversity to migraine, with a dose-response pattern — more ACEs, higher odds. In one study of 787 adolescent mothers, those who experienced four or more ACEs had roughly **three times the odds of migraine** (adjusted odds ratio 3.09) compared to those with none, and ACEs are especially associated with chronic migraine.[^10]

#### Musculoskeletal and Structural Factors

Neck tension, poor posture, and jaw clenching (TMJ issues) commonly accompany migraine and can contribute to attacks. True *cervicogenic* headache (arising from the neck) is a distinct diagnosis. Physical therapy and manual approaches can help some people, particularly when neck dysfunction is prominent — see the complementary section for the evidence landscape.[^23]

### 5. Root Causes and Contributing Factors: Going Deeper

#### Nutritional Deficiencies

- **Magnesium** has one of the strongest nutritional associations with migraine. People with migraine tend to have lower magnesium levels, including in the brain, and magnesium is involved in cortical spreading depression, neurotransmitter release, and vascular tone.[^11] Randomized trials support oral magnesium for prevention — in a 1996 trial of 81 patients, 600 mg daily of trimagnesium dicitrate reduced attack frequency by 41.6% versus 15.8% on placebo in weeks 9–12.[ ^11] It’s inexpensive and safe for most people.
- **Riboflavin (vitamin B2)** supports mitochondrial energy production, which is thought to be impaired in the migraine brain. Studies support its use for prevention, typically at 400 mg/day, with few side effects (it turns urine bright yellow).[^13]
- **Coenzyme Q10** also supports mitochondrial function. A 2005 randomized trial (Sándor et al.) found 100 mg three times daily was superior to placebo, with a 50%-responder rate of 47.6% versus 14.4% — a number-needed-to-treat of 3.[^12]
- **Vitamin D**: an emerging association. Meta-analyses find people with migraine tend to have lower vitamin D levels, and some studies suggest supplementation modestly reduces attack frequency — though the direction of cause and effect isn’t settled, and at least one recent study suggests low vitamin D may be a *consequence* of migraine’s impact rather than a cause.[ ^26]
- **Iron/ferritin**: especially relevant for menstruating women. Low iron stores may contribute to migraine, and some end-of-period migraines may relate to menstrual blood loss and low ferritin rather than purely to hormones; population data link higher dietary iron and ferritin with lower migraine odds in women.[ ^27]

#### Gut-Brain Axis

The gut and brain talk constantly, and migraine sits at this intersection. The large majority of the body’s serotonin — a neurotransmitter central to migraine — is made in the gut.[^14] Emerging research links the gut microbiome to migraine severity and frequency,  possibly through inflammation, the vagus nerve, and neurotransmitter production, and a few small trials of probiotics have shown promise.[^14] This is a genuinely exciting frontier, but it’s early — the practical takeaway is that a fiber-rich, diverse, whole-food diet supports gut health, not that any specific probiotic is proven to fix migraine.[^14]

#### Neuroinflammation and Central Sensitization

Here’s why early treatment matters. Repeated attacks can “wind up” the nervous system, lowering the migraine threshold over time — a process called central sensitization. A telltale sign is **allodynia**: when normally non-painful things (brushing hair, glasses on your face, a light touch) hurt during an attack. Allodynia is a marker that sensitization has set in, and it’s associated with attacks becoming more frequent and harder to treat.[^4] Treating attacks early and effectively — and preventing them when they’re frequent — helps keep the system from getting more sensitized.

#### Hormonal and Metabolic Factors

- **Insulin resistance** is more common in people with migraine and may worsen attacks; blood-sugar stability matters.[^28]
- **Thyroid dysfunction** (both low and high) is associated with headaches.
- **Mitochondrial dysfunction** is a leading hypothesis: if the migraine brain is “energy-hungry” and inefficient at producing energy, that helps explain why energy dips (skipped meals, poor sleep) trigger attacks and why mitochondrial-supporting nutrients (riboflavin, CoQ10) help.[^13][^28]

#### Sleep Architecture

It’s not just *how long* you sleep but *how well*. Poor sleep quality — fragmented sleep, untreated apnea, disrupted deep sleep — drives migraine cycles even when total hours look adequate. Sleep and migraine share brain regions (notably the hypothalamus and brainstem), which is why fixing sleep is one of the highest-yield things you can do.

### 6. Conventional Medical Treatments: What Works, What to Know

#### Acute (Abortive) Treatments — to stop an attack in progress

- **OTC options**: NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen), acetaminophen, and combination analgesics (e.g., aspirin-acetaminophen-caffeine) work well for many, especially milder attacks. The catch: using acute medications too often — generally 10–15+ days per month for three months — can cause **medication overuse headache (MOH)**, a cycle where the treatment fuels more headaches.[ ^15]
- **Triptans** (sumatriptan, rizatriptan, and others) are migraine-specific, work on serotonin receptors, and remain highly effective first-line prescription options. They constrict blood vessels, so they’re generally avoided in people with established cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled high blood pressure, or certain stroke risks.[^17]
- **Gepants** (CGRP receptor blockers) are a newer class. Ubrogepant and zavegepant are for acute treatment; rimegepant treats and prevents; atogepant is for prevention.[^17] They don’t constrict blood vessels, so they’re an option for people who can’t take triptans, and they don’t appear to cause medication overuse headache. They’re somewhat less potent than triptans for fast pain relief (number-needed-to-treat around 9–12, similar to NSAIDs), but very well tolerated. Notably, ubrogepant can even work if taken during the prodrome.[^17]
- **Ergotamines** (including dihydroergotamine) are an older class, still useful in specific situations but largely replaced by triptans and gepants due to side effects.

#### Preventive Treatments — to reduce how often attacks happen

- **Beta-blockers** (propranolol), **tricyclic antidepressants** (amitriptyline), and **anticonvulsants** (topiramate, valproate) have long track records. Each has trade-offs: topiramate can cause tingling, word-finding trouble, and weight loss; valproate causes weight gain and is dangerous in pregnancy; amitriptyline causes drowsiness and dry mouth; beta-blockers can cause fatigue. Both topiramate and valproate carry pregnancy risks requiring reliable contraception.[^25]
- **CGRP monoclonal antibodies** (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab) are injectable (or, for eptinezumab, IV) preventives designed specifically for migraine. They reduce monthly migraine days and are generally well tolerated.[^16] In 2024, the American Headache Society stated that CGRP-targeting therapies are a **first-line option** for prevention and should not require trying and failing older drugs first.[ ^21] The honest catch is **cost and access**: they’re expensive, and in practice insurance often still requires step therapy.
- **Botox (onabotulinumtoxinA)** is FDA-approved specifically for *chronic* migraine (15+ headache days/month). In the pivotal PREEMPT trials, injections every 12 weeks reduced headache days and worked even in people who were overusing acute medications.[^22] It’s not effective for episodic migraine.

**A word on medication overuse headache, without shame.** If you’ve fallen into a cycle of taking more and more pain medication and feeling worse, that is not a personal failing — it’s a known biological trap that happens precisely because your migraines weren’t being controlled. The fix is better prevention plus a plan (with your clinician) to reduce the overused medication, and several preventives, including Botox and CGRP drugs, work even in people with overuse.[^22]

### 7. Lifestyle and Nutritional Approaches: Building a Strong Foundation

These are the foundation — the things that raise your threshold so triggers have less power.

- **Hydration**: simple, free, and a genuine factor for many. Aim for steady fluid intake.
- **Sleep regularity**: going to bed and waking at consistent times — even on weekends — is one of the strongest protective habits. Irregularity, not just short sleep, drives attacks.
- **Regular eating**: don’t skip meals; keep blood sugar steady with balanced meals and snacks.
- **Exercise**: aerobic exercise has moderate-quality evidence for reducing the number of migraine days.[ ^19] The nuance, said with empathy: exercise can also *trigger* attacks in some people, especially sudden intense exertion. The answer isn’t to avoid exercise but to start gently, warm up, stay hydrated, and build gradually. Yoga and moderate continuous aerobic activity are reasonable starting points.[ ^19]
- **Stress management** (strong evidence): **Biofeedback** has good evidence and is recommended; **relaxation training**, **cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)**, and **mindfulness-based stress reduction** can each reduce attack frequency,  with effectiveness comparable to some preventive drugs and no medication side effects.[^18] Paced breathing and practices that engage the vagus nerve can help calm an overactive stress response.
- **Dietary approaches**: anti-inflammatory, whole-food eating patterns (like a Mediterranean pattern) are sensible and support overall health. Low-tyramine and elimination diets help *some* individuals but aren’t universally effective and can become restrictive — best done thoughtfully, ideally with guidance. (The ketogenic diet is covered in controversies.)

**Supplements** (always loop in a clinician, especially if pregnant, nursing, or on other medications):

- **Magnesium** (glycinate or citrate are well-tolerated forms): strongest evidence; research doses around 400–600 mg elemental magnesium daily.  Can cause loose stools; avoid high doses in kidney disease.[^11]
- **Riboflavin (B2)**: ~400 mg/day; very safe.[^13]
- **CoQ10**: ~100 mg three times daily (or 300 mg/day); safe, somewhat pricey.[^12]
- **Feverfew**: an herb with mixed evidence; some trials show modest benefit, others don’t. Generally well tolerated; can cause mouth irritation.[^20]
- **Butterbur**: here safety is the headline. Butterbur extracts (notably the Petadolex brand) showed efficacy in trials, but raw butterbur contains **pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs)** that can cause serious liver damage and may be carcinogenic. The American Academy of Neurology stopped recommending it in 2015 over liver-toxicity concerns. Only **certified PA-free** products should ever be considered, and even then it warrants clinician guidance and possibly liver monitoring.[^20]
- **Melatonin**: helpful for sleep-associated migraine and for sleep regularity; typically 3 mg at night.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids**: emerging evidence; anti-inflammatory and generally healthy.[^14]

### 8. Complementary and Integrative Approaches: Navigating the Evidence

- **Acupuncture** has a meaningful evidence base. A 2016 Cochrane review of 22 trials with 4,985 people found acupuncture reduces migraine frequency: 50% of people receiving true acupuncture had their headache days cut in half versus 41% with sham,  with effectiveness at least comparable to preventive drugs and few side effects.[^24] A course of at least six sessions is a reasonable, well-supported option.
- **Chiropractic and spinal manipulation**: helps some people, particularly when neck dysfunction (cervicogenic component) is present, but high-quality evidence specifically for migraine is limited. See the controversies section for the important safety discussion on neck manipulation.[^23]
- **Massage and myofascial release**: can reduce muscle tension and stress; modest evidence, low risk, and many people find it genuinely helpful as part of a broader plan.
- **Neuromodulation devices** (FDA-cleared): **Cefaly** (external trigeminal nerve stimulation, available over-the-counter), **gammaCore** (non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation), **Nerivio** (remote electrical neuromodulation), and **SAVI Dual** (single-pulse transcranial magnetic stimulation). Evidence quality ranges from low to moderate, but safety is excellent and there’s no medication overuse risk.[^24] The main downsides are cost and insurance coverage.
- **Craniosacral therapy**: limited controlled studies; what’s reported is largely anecdotal. Low risk, unproven.
- **Essential oils** (peppermint, lavender): limited research; small studies suggest topical peppermint oil or inhaled lavender may ease symptoms for some. Low risk if used sensibly.
- **Cold and heat therapy**: cold packs on the head/neck and warmth on tense muscles are accessible, cheap, and supported by clinical experience even if formal trials are sparse. Worth trying.

### 9. When Migraines Are a Warning Sign: Red Flags

Most headaches, even severe ones, are not dangerous. But certain features — captured in the medical “SNNOOP10” checklist — mean you should seek prompt or emergency evaluation. **Do not wait** if you have:[^29]

- **Thunderclap headache**: the worst headache of your life, exploding to maximum intensity within seconds to a minute (possible brain bleed — call emergency services).
- A **new or different headache after age 50**.
- Headache with **fever and a stiff neck** (possible meningitis).
- Headache with **neurological symptoms** that don’t fit your usual aura: weakness, numbness, confusion, trouble speaking, vision loss, or loss of consciousness.
- Headache after **head trauma**.
- A headache that is **steadily getting worse** or changes pattern, or is triggered by coughing, exertion, or position changes.
- New headache with a **history of cancer, HIV, or a weakened immune system**, or in **pregnancy**.

**Who to see:** Start with primary care for diagnosis and first-line treatment. Consider a **neurologist or headache specialist** if attacks are frequent (generally 4+ per month), disabling, not responding to treatment, or if the diagnosis is unclear. Preventive treatment should be considered when you have frequent attacks, significant disability, or are using acute medication too often.[^21] Accurate diagnosis matters — both to rule out the rare dangerous causes and to get you the right treatment.[^29]

### 10. Controversial and Commonly Debated Topics

- **“Red wine always causes migraines.”** Not for everyone, and not always. Proposed culprits include histamine, tannins, sulfites, and tyramine, but controlled evidence is surprisingly thin and individual variation is huge. Some people are reliably triggered by red wine; others tolerate it fine but react to beer or champagne. Track your own pattern rather than assuming.
- **Hormonal contraceptives and migraine with aura.** This is a genuine medical concern, not fearmongering. Migraine with aura independently raises ischemic stroke risk, and combined (estrogen-containing) hormonal contraceptives raise it further — one large analysis (Champaloux et al., 2017) found the combination of migraine with aura plus combined hormonal contraceptives was associated with about a **six-fold higher stroke risk** (odds ratio 6.1) versus having neither factor.[^25] Absolute risk in young healthy non-smokers is still low, but guidelines generally advise against combined estrogen contraceptives in people who have migraine with aura. Progestin-only and non-hormonal methods are considered safe. This is a conversation to have explicitly with your clinician.[^25]
- **Ketogenic diet for migraine.** Genuinely promising but contested. The theory — that the migraine brain is energy-starved and ketones offer an alternative fuel — is biologically reasonable,  and several small trials (including in people with overweight) show reduced migraine days.[ ^28] But studies are small, diets are hard to sustain, and large rigorous trials are lacking.  Worth discussing as an option for some, not a proven prescription, and best done with nutritional supervision.[^28]
- **Cervical spine manipulation and stroke risk.** Neck manipulation has been statistically associated with vertebral artery dissection and stroke, especially in younger people.[^23] The crucial nuance: this may largely be *reverse causation* — people with a dissection already in progress have neck pain and headache, and seek manual treatment for it. A large population study (Cassidy et al., 2008) found similar stroke associations whether people saw a chiropractor *or* a primary care doctor, suggesting the dissection often came first.[^30] Causation isn’t established and absolute risk is very low, but the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association concluded a “second-hit” triggering mechanism can’t be fully excluded and recommends informed consent before cervical manipulation.[^23]
- **Magnesium IV therapy.** Intravenous magnesium is used in some emergency settings for acute attacks. Evidence is mixed; it appears most helpful for migraine *with* aura and for certain patients, and it requires medical supervision.[^11] It’s not a substitute for oral prevention.
- **Hormonal/menstrual migraine and progesterone-only approaches.** For menstrually-related migraine, options include timing NSAIDs or triptans around the period (“mini-prophylaxis”), stabilizing estrogen, or using continuous/progestin-only hormonal strategies — particularly relevant when aura makes estrogen risky.[^7][^25] The clinical picture is individual and evolving; this is best navigated with a clinician who treats hormonal migraine.

### 11. A Word on the Emotional and Relational Weight of Migraines

Migraine is an **invisible disability**. You can be in agony while looking completely normal, which feeds one of the cruelest parts of the disease: not being believed. Being told “it’s just a headache,” or sensing that colleagues think you’re flaky for canceling, adds a layer of grief on top of the pain.

There’s also the **psychological weight of unpredictability** — never quite knowing if you’ll be able to show up for the event, the trip, the workday, the bedtime story. That uncertainty wears on relationships and on your sense of yourself.

The link between migraine and **depression and anxiety is bidirectional** — each makes the other more likely, through shared brain chemistry, not weakness.[^10] Naming this matters, because treating mood and anxiety is part of treating migraine, and vice versa.

If there’s a hopeful note here, it’s this: people living with migraine often develop real expertise in their own bodies, fierce self-advocacy, and deep empathy. Connecting with others who get it — through patient communities and advocacy organizations — can ease the isolation. You are not weak, you are not exaggerating, and you deserve good care.

### 12. Key Facts at a Glance

- Migraine affects roughly **1.16 billion people worldwide** and is among the top causes of disability on Earth — more disabling at the population level than many “headline” neurological diseases.[^1]
- About **39 million Americans** live with migraine.[^2]
- Migraine hits **women two to three times more often than men** (about 20.7% vs. 9.7% in U.S. data) — and the trigger is the *drop* in estrogen, not high or low levels.[^7][^9]
- The visual aura you see is an actual electrical wave — **cortical spreading depression** — crawling across your brain’s surface.[^3]
- **CGRP**, the protein at the center of the newest migraine drugs, was documented as elevated during migraine attacks back in **1990**, but CGRP-blocking treatments only reached patients years later.[^5][^16]
- Only about **1 in 4 to 1 in 3** people with migraine experience aura.[^6]
- People with **four or more adverse childhood experiences** had roughly **three times** the odds of migraine in one study.[^10]
- **Coenzyme Q10’s** number-needed-to-treat in one randomized trial was just **3** — remarkably good for a supplement.[^12]
- The infamous **“letdown” migraine** strikes *after* stress lifts — which is why so many attacks land on weekends and vacations.
- **Gepants and CGRP antibodies** don’t constrict blood vessels, making them options for people with heart concerns who can’t take triptans.[^17]
- **Allodynia** — when your hair or glasses hurt during an attack — is a sign your nervous system has become sensitized, and a reason to treat early.[^4]
