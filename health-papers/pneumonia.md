*A resource for health ministry leaders, health coaches, people living with pneumonia, and the families who care for them.*

Pneumonia is one of the most common serious infections in the world, and also one of the most misunderstood. It can be a mild illness that clears with rest and time, or it can become a life-threatening emergency within hours. Because that range is so wide, the most useful thing anyone supporting a sick person can learn is how to tell the difference—and how to combine sensible home care with timely medical help. This guide walks through what pneumonia is, how it is treated, what lifestyle and nutritional factors genuinely matter, and where the honest limits of our knowledge lie. Throughout, it also marks the line between what a coach or lay supporter can helpfully do and what belongs in the hands of a licensed clinician.

## What Pneumonia Is

Pneumonia is an infection that inflames the tiny air sacs (alveoli) in one or both lungs. These sacs can fill with fluid or pus, which is why a person with pneumonia may cough up phlegm, run a fever, and struggle to catch their breath.[^1] The word simply refers to the lungs; the infection behind it can come from bacteria, viruses, or, less often, fungi.[^1] Globally, pneumonia and other lower respiratory infections remain among the leading infectious causes of death, and pneumonia is the single leading infectious killer of children under five.[^2]

Bacterial pneumonia is frequently caused by *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (the pneumococcus), which is the most common bacterial cause worldwide; the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 identified it as the leading cause of fatal lower respiratory infections, linked to 653,000 deaths (95% CI 553,000–777,000) in that year.[^3] Viral pneumonia can follow influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), or the virus that causes COVID-19. A third category, "atypical" or "walking" pneumonia, is often caused by *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*. It tends to come on gradually, with a dry cough, sore throat, headache, low fever, and enough energy that people keep walking around—hence the nickname.[^4] Walking pneumonia is usually milder and rarely requires hospitalization, though the cough and tiredness can linger for weeks.[^4] [^5]

Aspiration pneumonia is different in origin. It develops when food, liquid, saliva, or stomach contents are breathed into the lungs rather than swallowed safely. This is especially common in people with swallowing difficulty, and it is discussed in more detail later.

> **Key Fact:** Pneumonia is not one disease but a family of lung infections with different causes—bacterial, viral, and atypical—which is why treatment and recovery vary so much from person to person.

How pneumonia develops usually follows a pattern: a germ reaches the lower airways, the body's defenses are overwhelmed or weakened, and inflammation sets in. Some people are far more vulnerable than others. Risk rises steeply with age. Even healthy adults 65 and older are much more likely to be hospitalized with pneumococcal pneumonia than younger adults, and an 80-year-old has a higher risk than a 65-year-old.[^6] Very young children are also at high risk. So are people with chronic lung disease (such as COPD or asthma), heart disease, diabetes, and conditions or medicines that weaken the immune system.[^7] Smoking, prior pneumonia, difficulty moving around, and poor oral hygiene each raise risk as well.[^8] Understanding these risk groups helps a coach or ministry leader know who most needs encouragement toward prevention and early care.

## Warning Signs and When to Seek Emergency Care

This section is deliberately direct. Pneumonia can worsen quickly, and delays cost lives. Anyone supporting a sick person should know these red flags and act on them without second-guessing.

Call emergency services or go to an emergency department right away if a person has any of the following: struggling to breathe, gasping, or unable to speak in full sentences; pale, blue, or blotchy skin, lips, or tongue; coughing up blood; sudden confusion or not knowing where they are; or a baby who cannot be woken or feels floppy.[^9] Public health authorities list similar adult emergency signs: difficulty breathing or shortness of breath, persistent pain or pressure in the chest, and persistent dizziness or confusion.[^10] Clinical guidance also advises seeking medical attention for difficulty breathing, chest pain, a persistent high fever, or a cough that brings up pus.[^11]

Two warning signs deserve special attention because they are easy to miss. In older adults, pneumonia may show up as new confusion or a sudden change in alertness rather than an obvious cough or fever, and some older people run a lower-than-normal temperature instead of a high one.[^11] An elevated breathing rate is often one of the earliest clues in an older person. Families sometimes assume confusion is "just aging" and wait too long. It should be treated as a possible medical emergency.

> **Key Fact:** In people over 65, sudden confusion or a drop in alertness can be the first—and sometimes only—obvious sign of pneumonia. Do not wait for a classic cough and fever before seeking care.

For a coach or ministry leader, the scope-of-practice rule here is simple: you are not diagnosing anything. You are recognizing danger and helping the person get professional care fast. When in doubt, err toward calling for help.

## The Conventional Medical Treatment Landscape

When someone is evaluated for pneumonia, the clinician's first job is to judge how sick they are and decide where they should be treated—at home, in a hospital ward, or in intensive care. Tools exist to help. One widely used bedside score, CURB-65, assigns a point for each of five findings: new confusion, a raised blood-urea level, a respiratory rate of 30 breaths per minute or more, low blood pressure, and age 65 or older.[^12] In the study that defined it, 30-day mortality rose steadily with the score—from under 1 percent at a score of 0 to roughly 40–57 percent at scores of 4 and 5—so lower scores generally point toward safe home treatment while higher scores point toward hospital admission and, at the top of the range, intensive care.[^12] In the United States, guidelines favor a different tool (the Pneumonia Severity Index) for the admit-or-home decision and use a separate set of "severe pneumonia" criteria to guide intensive-care admission.[^13] Oxygen level in the blood, measured painlessly with a fingertip pulse oximeter, is another key factor: a reading below about 92 percent suggests the body is not getting enough oxygen and needs medical assessment.[^14]

For bacterial pneumonia, antibiotics are the cornerstone of treatment. The specific choice depends on how sick the person is, their other health conditions, and local patterns of bacterial resistance.[^13] Amoxicillin is a common first choice for otherwise healthy adults with milder disease in many guidelines, while sicker or higher-risk patients may need broader or combination therapy.[^15] There is an active push in medicine to use antibiotics more sparingly and to stop them as soon as it is safe, because overuse drives resistance.[^15] Antibiotics do not help viral pneumonia, though a person hospitalized with influenza pneumonia may still receive them because bacterial and viral infection can overlap and are hard to tell apart at the bedside.[^13]

Supportive care matters as much as the antibiotic. People who cannot maintain a safe blood-oxygen level receive supplemental oxygen, often through a small nasal tube, with target ranges set by their clinical situation.[^14] Those who are dehydrated may receive fluids, and the most severely ill may need a ventilator to help them breathe. Most people with mild pneumonia are treated at home and recover, but the decision about where care happens is a medical judgment, not a home guess.

> **Key Fact:** Antibiotics treat bacterial pneumonia, not viral pneumonia—but because the two can look alike and can occur together, only a clinician can decide whether antibiotics are appropriate.

The parallel risks here are worth naming plainly. The risk of *taking* antibiotics includes side effects and contributing to resistance. But the risk of *not* getting appropriate treatment for bacterial pneumonia—delaying care in favor of home remedies—can be severe illness or death. For a genuinely bacterial pneumonia, standard medical care is not optional, and no supplement or home practice replaces it.

## Lifestyle, Nutritional, and Functional Factors

This is the area where a health coach can contribute the most, because these are the everyday habits that shape resilience. It is also an area where honesty about the strength of the evidence matters. The framing below moves along a spectrum—from well-established to emerging to limited—rather than sorting practices into "proven" and "unproven."

**Smoking cessation** sits at the well-established end. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 27 studies (460,592 participants) found that tobacco smoke exposure significantly raises the risk of developing community-acquired pneumonia: current smokers had a pooled odds ratio of 2.17 (95% CI 1.70–2.76) and ex-smokers 1.49 (1.26–1.75) compared with never-smokers, with a clear dose-response relationship in which heavier smoking meant higher risk.[^16] Encouragingly, the risk of bacterial pneumonia falls back toward that of a never-smoker within roughly a decade of quitting, and smoking cessation reduces hospitalizations for pneumonia.[^16] [^17] Passive (secondhand) smoke also raises risk, particularly in adults over 65, who carried a 64 percent increased risk (OR 1.64, 1.17–2.30).[^16] A hospitalization for pneumonia is often described as a "teachable moment" for quitting. Helping someone build a quit plan, or connecting them to a cessation program, is squarely within a coach's role.

**Indoor air quality** is closely related. Household air pollution—especially from burning wood, dung, charcoal, or other solid fuels indoors—is a major driver of childhood pneumonia worldwide and is responsible for a large share of pneumonia deaths in young children.[^18] [^19] Reducing indoor smoke, improving ventilation, and avoiding indoor tobacco smoke are reasonable, well-supported steps.

**Vitamin D status** is a genuinely nuanced topic. Laboratory work shows vitamin D helps the immune system respond to respiratory viruses, and observational studies link low vitamin D levels to more respiratory infections.[^20] But the trial evidence is mixed and has shifted over time. A large individual-participant meta-analysis (25 randomized trials, 11,321 participants) found that vitamin D supplementation modestly reduced the risk of acute respiratory infections overall (adjusted odds ratio 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.96), with the clearest benefit in people who were very deficient at baseline (aOR 0.30 for those under 25 nmol/L) and those taking daily or weekly rather than large infrequent doses.[^20] Later and larger analyses have narrowed that effect: a 2024 update added six new trials (including one with 15,804 participants), and the pooled estimate's confidence interval now includes 1.00, meaning the overall protective effect no longer reaches statistical significance.[^21] The fair summary: correcting a true deficiency is sensible and may help, but vitamin D is not a reliable shield against pneumonia, and mega-doses are not supported. Testing and dosing decisions belong with a clinician.

**Zinc** shows a similar pattern of context-dependent benefit. In children in low- and middle-income countries, where zinc deficiency is common, supplementation has been associated with a lower incidence of pneumonia.[^22] As an add-on treatment once pneumonia has developed, however, the evidence is weaker; a Cochrane review found insufficient evidence that zinc speeds recovery in young children already being treated with antibiotics.[^23] This is a good example of the difference between "helpful for prevention in a deficient population" and "helpful as a treatment for everyone."

**Hydration, sleep, and whole-food eating patterns** round out the picture. Staying adequately hydrated is standard supportive advice during any febrile illness, though it should be individualized for people with heart or kidney conditions. Adequate sleep supports immune function generally. Dietary patterns rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, and nuts are consistently associated with lower levels of inflammation and, in a large cohort study, with a lower risk of being hospitalized for respiratory infection.[^24] [^25] The benefit appears tied to *healthy* plant foods; diets heavy in refined grains and sugar did not show the same association.[^24] This is emerging, mostly observational evidence—encouraging and biologically plausible, but not proof that a particular diet prevents pneumonia. Encouraging a nourishing, plant-forward eating pattern is well within a coach's role and carries little downside.

> **Key Fact:** For nutrients like vitamin D and zinc, the evidence points to a clear theme: correcting a real deficiency can help, but supplements are not a substitute for vaccination, not-smoking, and prompt medical care—and more is not better.

## Prevention: Vaccination Against Pneumococcus and Influenza

Vaccination is the most effective single tool for preventing several of the most dangerous forms of pneumonia, and it deserves both a clear presentation of the evidence and genuine respect for the questions people raise.

The landmark trial of the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, known as CAPiTA, enrolled 84,496 immunocompetent adults aged 65 and older and showed that the 13-valent vaccine (PCV13) prevented a first episode of vaccine-type pneumococcal community-acquired pneumonia, with a vaccine efficacy of about 45 percent against that specific outcome.[^26] [^27] Newer vaccines (PCV15 and PCV20) cover more strains and were approved based on immune-response data comparable to the older vaccine.[^28] Early real-world data on the 20-valent vaccine in older adults found meaningful reductions in invasive pneumococcal disease and all-cause pneumonia.[^29] Influenza vaccination matters too, because influenza can lead directly to viral pneumonia or set the stage for bacterial pneumonia. Studies show influenza vaccination reduces the risk of hospitalization for influenza-associated pneumonia, though the size of the benefit varies year to year with how well the vaccine matches circulating strains.[^30] [^31]

People raise fair questions about vaccines, and these deserve straight answers rather than dismissal. Common questions include which vaccine is right for a given age or health condition, how the timing works (especially when someone has had an older pneumococcal vaccine before), and what side effects to expect. The trials found that these vaccines were generally well tolerated, with most reactions being mild-to-moderate soreness, redness, or short-lived body aches.[^28] Serious adverse events were infrequent and occurred at similar rates in vaccinated and comparison groups.[^28] Effectiveness is real but not absolute—a vaccinated person can still get pneumonia, which is why vaccination works alongside, not instead of, the other measures in this guide.

For a coach or ministry leader, the appropriate role is to share accurate information, listen to concerns without judgment, and encourage a conversation with a clinician or pharmacist who can tailor recommendations to the individual. Who should and shouldn't receive a given vaccine—and when—is a clinical decision. What a coach can do is make sure hesitancy is met with respect and good information rather than pressure.

> **Key Fact:** Pneumococcal and influenza vaccines meaningfully lower the risk of the most dangerous pneumonias, but they are not perfect. They work best as one layer of protection among several.

## Hydrotherapy and Fever Management

Water-based treatments and careful fever management have a long history in home and traditional care, and they remain a reasonable area for supportive comfort—provided expectations are realistic and warning signs are respected.

Consider first what fever actually is. Fever is not the enemy; it is part of the body's coordinated defense. Laboratory studies show that fever increases the mobility and activity of immune cells, and several retrospective studies of patients hospitalized with serious infections—including community-acquired pneumonia—found that those who mounted a fever had better survival than those who stayed unusually cool.[^32] Some evidence suggests that routinely suppressing fever with medication may, in certain infections, slightly prolong illness.[^33] This does not mean fever should never be treated. Fever is uncomfortable, raises the body's demands, and can be genuinely risky for people with heart or lung disease, and fever-reducing medicine can help those people feel and function better.[^34] The reasonable middle ground, supported by the evidence, is to treat fever for comfort and in vulnerable people rather than reflexively driving every temperature down to normal.[^34]

Practices such as warm baths, cool compresses, and warm-and-cold applications to the chest are traditional approaches used to ease discomfort and help a person rest. What the evidence supports here is comfort, not cure. Chest physiotherapy—a related set of hands-on techniques to help clear mucus—has been studied in adults with pneumonia, and a Cochrane review concluded the current evidence is very uncertain: some techniques may slightly shorten hospital stay or fever duration, but none clearly improved cure rates or survival, and all of this rests on low-certainty evidence.[^35] [^36] In other words, these are areas of limited and low-certainty evidence rather than areas that have been studied thoroughly and found useless. They may offer comfort; they should not replace medical evaluation.

Fever should prompt medical attention rather than home management in specific situations: a very high or persistent fever, fever in an infant, fever accompanied by any of the emergency warning signs listed earlier, or fever in someone with a serious chronic illness or weakened immune system.[^9] [^11] A coach can support comfort measures and rest; a coach cannot judge whether a fever is a minor nuisance or a sign of dangerous infection. That judgment requires a clinician.

## Reduced Intake During Acute Illness

Many people lose their appetite when they are sick, and families often worry about it. The science here is genuinely interesting and still developing. Loss of appetite during infection—called sickness-associated anorexia—is not simply a malfunction; it appears to be an evolved, actively organized part of the body's response to infection.[^37] Research suggests that the same inflammatory signals that trigger immune defenses also reduce appetite, and that this reduced intake may, in some infections, help the body tolerate and fight the illness.[^37] [^38]

The picture is not one-size-fits-all. Animal research has found that whether reduced eating helps or harms can depend on the type of infection—broadly, the body's optimal strategy may differ between bacterial and viral illness.[^38] Importantly, all of this is a description of what naturally happens and what it may mean, not a prescription. Deliberate fasting protocols during acute pneumonia have not been established as safe or beneficial, and in frail, older, or already-undernourished people, going without adequate nutrition and fluids can be genuinely harmful.

The sensible, evidence-respecting message for coaches and families is this: it is normal and usually fine for a sick person's appetite to drop for a short time, and gentle, easy foods plus adequate fluids are reasonable. It is not appropriate to encourage a structured fast, to withhold food, or to set eating or fasting "rules" for someone who is acutely ill—especially an older or frail person. Concerns about a person who is not eating or drinking, becoming dehydrated, or losing strength should go to a clinician.

> **Key Fact:** Losing your appetite during infection is a normal, likely purposeful response—but deliberately fasting through pneumonia is a different matter and is not supported as a treatment, particularly for the frail or elderly.

## Aspiration Pneumonia and Care Near the End of Life

Aspiration pneumonia occupies sensitive territory, particularly for families caring for an elderly or seriously ill loved one. It develops when material—food, drink, saliva, or stomach contents—enters the lungs instead of the stomach. People with swallowing difficulty (dysphagia), often from stroke, Parkinson's disease, or dementia, are at particular risk, and dependence on others for feeding is itself a strong risk factor.[^39] [^40]

Several practical measures can reduce risk and are well within a caregiver's ability to support. Good oral hygiene matters a great deal, because a mouth heavy with bacteria means that even small amounts of aspirated saliva can be dangerous; the number of decayed teeth and the frequency of mouth care are linked to aspiration pneumonia.[^39] Careful positioning during and after meals, unhurried feeding, appropriate food textures, and rest before eating can all help.[^40] [^41] Speech-language therapists, dietitians, and other professionals can assess swallowing and tailor a plan.[^39]

The harder territory arrives in advanced illness. For people with advanced dementia, the evidence does not show that feeding tubes prevent aspiration pneumonia or prolong life; tube feeding cannot stop a person from aspirating their own saliva, and in some cases may increase risk.[^41] [^42] A Cochrane review found insufficient evidence that tube feeding benefits people with advanced dementia, and guidance in some countries reserves it for cases where swallowing difficulty is expected to be temporary.[^42] Because of this, many families and care teams choose "comfort feeding" or "eating and drinking at risk"—continuing to offer food and drink by mouth for pleasure and dignity, accepting some risk, rather than pursuing aggressive interventions.[^41] [^42]

These are among the most personal decisions a family can face, and there are no universal right answers. This guide offers information, not direction. Questions worth bringing to the care team might include: What are this person's own wishes and values? What would each option mean for comfort and quality of life? What is realistic to expect from aggressive treatment versus comfort-focused care? A coach or ministry leader can be a compassionate, listening presence and can help a family organize their questions—but decisions about feeding, tube placement, and the goals of care must be made with clinicians who know the person's full medical situation.

> **Key Fact:** In advanced dementia, feeding tubes have not been shown to prevent aspiration pneumonia or extend life. Continuing gentle, careful feeding by mouth is a legitimate, dignity-centered choice that many families and clinicians make together.

## Probiotics, Elderberry, and Other Complementary Supports

Complementary products are popular, and readers deserve an honest accounting—neither overselling nor dismissing them.

**Probiotics** have been studied most rigorously in hospitalized patients on ventilators, where several meta-analyses found that probiotics may reduce the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia.[^43] [^44] However, the certainty of this evidence is low, and a large well-designed trial (PROSPECT) did not find a benefit, which tempers the enthusiasm from earlier, smaller studies.[^43] This is a setting-specific finding in critically ill patients; it does not translate into evidence that taking a probiotic prevents or treats ordinary community pneumonia. The honest status is "emerging and uncertain."

**Elderberry** (*Sambucus nigra*) has been studied for upper respiratory symptoms such as colds and flu—not for pneumonia. Some randomized trials and a meta-analysis suggest it may modestly reduce the duration and severity of cold and flu symptoms.[^45] [^46] A more cautious systematic review found only a small number of trials and called for better evidence.[^47] Crucially, there are no trials showing elderberry treats or prevents pneumonia, and it should never be used to delay medical care for a suspected lung infection. As a comfort measure for mild upper-respiratory symptoms in an otherwise healthy person, it carries little risk; as a pneumonia treatment, it is unsupported.

The parallel-risk principle applies to all complementary supports. The risk of trying a low-harm product like elderberry for a cold is generally small. The risk of relying on any of these products *instead of* seeking care for warning signs of pneumonia is potentially severe. That distinction—comfort support versus substitute for care—is the one a coach should keep front and center.

> **Key Fact:** Elderberry and probiotics have been studied for colds and for hospital-acquired pneumonia respectively—not as treatments for community pneumonia. They may have modest supportive roles, but they are not a reason to delay medical care.

## Recovery, Fatigue, and When to Expect Improvement

Recovery from pneumonia is often slower than people expect, and setting realistic expectations prevents needless worry and premature return to full activity. In a classic study tracking symptom resolution in 134 patients, the median time to resolution ranged from 3 days for fever to 14 days for cough and fatigue, and 35 percent of patients still had at least one symptom present at the end of the 28-day study period.[^48] A modeling study of hospitalized patients found that symptoms tended to return toward baseline by about ten days, with the pace of recovery driven largely by how severe the initial illness was.[^49]

In broad strokes: many people with mild pneumonia feel substantially better within a week or so, moderate cases may take two to three weeks, and severe cases—especially in older adults or those with chronic conditions—can take four to six weeks or considerably longer for full recovery.[^50] A lingering cough, reduced energy, and mild breathlessness can persist for weeks after the infection itself has cleared, and chest X-ray changes often lag behind how a person feels.[^50] Fatigue, in particular, is common and normal during convalescence.

The practical counsel here is patience and paced return to activity. Rest supports recovery and helps prevent relapse. A coach can be genuinely helpful by normalizing a gradual recovery, encouraging rest without guilt, and watching for signs that recovery is stalling. Symptoms that fail to improve, or that improve and then worsen again, warrant medical review; persistent symptoms several weeks after treatment may prompt a clinician to look further.[^50]

## Contraindications and Red Flags: When Home Care Is Not Enough

Some situations mean home management is inappropriate from the start, and recognizing them is a core safety skill. Home care is not appropriate—and prompt medical evaluation is needed—for anyone with the emergency warning signs described earlier: serious difficulty breathing, bluish lips or skin, coughing up blood, chest pain, or new confusion.[^9] [^10] Beyond those, certain people should be evaluated by a clinician early rather than waiting to see how things unfold at home: infants and young children, adults over 65, pregnant women, people with chronic heart or lung disease or diabetes, and anyone whose immune system is weakened by illness or medication.[^7]

A low blood-oxygen reading, a high or climbing breathing rate, an inability to keep down fluids, and worsening rather than improving symptoms are all signals that home care has reached its limit.[^13] [^14] No one should attempt to self-manage pneumonia by substituting supplements or home remedies for professional evaluation when these red flags are present. The single most dangerous pattern in pneumonia care is delay.

> **Key Fact:** The most dangerous mistake in pneumonia is waiting too long. When red-flag symptoms appear, or when a high-risk person shows any sign of pneumonia, professional evaluation should come first—home measures second.

## Scope of Practice: What a Coach or Ministry Leader Can Do

Clarity about roles protects everyone. A health coach or health ministry leader operates in the space of education, encouragement, and connection—not diagnosis, treatment, or prescribing.

Appropriately within scope: explaining what pneumonia is and who is at higher risk; teaching people to recognize warning signs and to act on them quickly; encouraging well-supported prevention such as not smoking, improving indoor air quality, nourishing eating patterns, adequate rest, and vaccination conversations with a clinician; supporting comfort and rest during recovery; helping families organize their questions for the medical team; and providing a caring, listening presence. Sharing accurate, balanced information about supplements and traditional practices—including their honest limits—also fits here.

Outside scope, and requiring referral to a clinician: diagnosing pneumonia or judging its severity; recommending, starting, stopping, or dosing antibiotics, prescription medicines, or supplements as treatment; deciding whether someone needs hospitalization or oxygen; interpreting oxygen readings or test results; and guiding decisions about feeding tubes or goals of care near the end of life. When someone shows warning signs, the coach's job is not to manage the illness but to help the person reach medical care.

Clear referral triggers a coach should memorize: any emergency warning sign; a high-risk person (very young, elderly, chronically ill, or immunocompromised) with symptoms of pneumonia; a fever that is very high, persistent, or occurs in an infant; symptoms that worsen or fail to improve over several days; and any situation where a family is being encouraged to delay or refuse standard care in favor of home remedies. In that last situation especially, gentle but clear guidance toward medical care is the responsible path.

## A Closing Reflection on Rest and Community

Illness has a way of stripping life back to essentials. Pneumonia, in particular, forces a slower pace—it asks for rest, patience, and often the humility to accept help. There is something quietly restorative in that, if we let it be. Recovery is rarely a solo effort. A meal delivered, a ride to an appointment, a check-in phone call, a willingness to sit with someone's fear as much as their symptoms: these ordinary acts of care carry real weight when a person is unwell. Communities that surround the sick with practical help and steady presence give something medicine alone cannot. Caring well for the whole person—body, mind, and the need for connection—is not a substitute for good medical care, but it is a genuine part of healing, and it is something everyone, professional or not, can offer.
