## Purpose of This Handout

Health coaches, pastors, and ministry leaders are frequently asked whether Applied Kinesiology (AK) — the “muscle-testing” technique used by some chiropractors, naturopaths, dentists, and a handful of physicians — is a legitimate diagnostic tool, a harmless adjunct, or a practice that ought to be avoided. The question is rarely simple. Many sincere Christians have walked into an AK appointment, watched the practitioner declare an “arm strong” or “arm weak” while a supplement bottle was held to their chest, and walked out convinced something real had happened. Many practitioners are kind, articulate, and confident. And yet the peer-reviewed evidence base for AK is, by every published systematic review, exceptionally thin — and the practice, when combined with energy-medicine concepts, raises serious concerns from a biblical worldview.

This handout aims to give coaches and ministry leaders an accurate, evenhanded, decision-ready summary of the science, the spiritual considerations, and the practical questions to ask, so that they can counsel those they serve with both honesty and pastoral care.

## 1. Foundational Understanding: What AK Is and Where It Came From

### 1.1 Definition and scope

Applied Kinesiology (AK) is a diagnostic and therapeutic system that uses **manual muscle testing (MMT)** as its central tool. Practitioners apply pressure to an isolated muscle  (commonly the deltoid or a finger-resistance test) and interpret the muscle’s apparent “strength” or “weakness” as a window into the patient’s overall health — including organ dysfunction, nutritional deficiencies, food sensitivities, allergies, emotional stress, and “energy imbalances.”[^1] AK is to be distinguished from **kinesiology** proper, which is the scientific study of human movement  and an established field of biomechanics and rehabilitation. The two share a name but almost nothing else.[^2]

The conceptual foundation of AK is the **“triad of health”**: structural, chemical, and mental/emotional factors, depicted as three sides of an equilateral triangle, all of which must be balanced for health.[^3] Within this framework, muscle weakness is interpreted as a sign that one or more sides of the triad are disturbed.

### 1.2 Methodology

ICAK-trained practitioners typically combine three procedures:

- **Manual muscle testing (MMT):** the examiner positions a muscle in isolation and applies a graded force; the patient resists.  A perceived “give” or “unlock” is interpreted as weakness.[^1]
- **Therapy localization (TL):** the patient touches a specific point on his own body while a muscle is tested. A change in the muscle response is interpreted as significant for that location.[^4]
- **Challenge testing:** a substance (a vitamin, an allergen vial, a food sample) is placed in the mouth, held in the hand, or set on the body.  A change in muscle response is interpreted as the body’s response to that substance.[^1]

From these readings, practitioners typically recommend chiropractic adjustments, supplements, dietary changes, reflex-point stimulation, cranial techniques, acupressure on meridian points,  or emotional-stress reduction protocols.[^3]

### 1.3 Historical development

AK was developed in 1964 by **Dr. George J. Goodheart, Jr.**  (1918–2008), a chiropractor practicing in Detroit, Michigan. The PubMed-indexed historical account states: “In 1964, Goodheart claimed to have corrected a patient’s chronic winged scapula by pressing on nodules found near the origin and insertion of the involved serratus anterior muscle. This finding led to the origin and insertion treatment, the first method developed in AK.”[^5] Goodheart subsequently incorporated procedures derived from Frank Chapman (neurolymphatic reflexes), Terrence Bennett (neurovascular reflexes), William Sutherland (cranial techniques), and — most consequentially for the present discussion — **Felix Mann’s** writings on acupuncture and meridian therapy.[^5]

The **International College of Applied Kinesiology (ICAK)** was founded in 1975–1976 to formalize training, certification, and research; it now has chapters in the United States, Canada, Europe, Japan, Russia, and Australasia.[^6] The 2003 National Board of Chiropractic Examiners *Job Analysis of Chiropractic* documented that AK was used by 37.6% of  U.S. chiropractors  and applied to 12.9% of patients seen, making it the tenth most common chiropractic technique  reported.[^7]

### 1.4 ICAK-trained AK vs. derivative methods

This distinction matters enormously for evaluation. ICAK insists that “applied kinesiology” properly refers only to the system taught by ICAK-credentialed instructors and used by licensed diagnosing professionals (chiropractors, medical doctors, osteopaths, dentists, naturopaths). ICAK explicitly states that “using a straight ‘arm pull-down’ muscle test for the sole determinant of what therapy to administer is not considered applied kinesiology.”[^8]

In practice, however, the public encounters many **derivative methods** that borrow the muscle-test mechanic and brand it under new names:

- **Touch for Health  (TFH)** — developed in the early 1970s by  chiropractor **John F. Thie** as a “natural health training for the public” using muscle checking, acupressure points, meridian tracing, and goal-setting; it explicitly integrates Traditional Chinese Medicine, naturopathy, osteopathy, and “energy kinesiology.”[^9]
- **Contact Reflex Analysis (CRA)** — claims that over a thousand health conditions can be diagnosed by pressing on 75 “reflex points” while muscle-testing.[^1]
- **Nambudripad’s Allergy Elimination Technique (NAET)** — based on the premise that allergies are caused by “energy blockages”  diagnosable by muscle testing and curable with acupressure.[^10]
- **Neuro Emotional Technique (NET)** — uses muscle testing combined with “general semantics and Traditional Chinese Medicine”  to identify and release “emotional blocks.”[^11]
- **BodyTalk, Total Body Modification (TBM), NeuroModulation Technique, Nutritional Response Testing, Quantum Reflex Analysis,** and Yoshiaki Omura’s **Bi-Digital O-Ring Test (BDORT)** — all employ a muscle/finger-resistance check as the central diagnostic act.[^12]

When a Christian patient is told by a friend that “muscle testing helped me,” she may have experienced any one of these — most of which are further removed from medical assessment and more deeply embedded in qi/meridian/energy frameworks than even ICAK-style AK.

### 1.5 Who uses AK?

Most AK practitioners are chiropractors,  but the system is also used by some naturopaths,  a smaller number of medical doctors (especially in Germany and Italy, where the ICAK chapter is largely populated by MDs and dentists, respectively), and an array of nutritionists, dental hygienists, massage therapists, nurses, and lay practitioners using derivative techniques.[^6][^13]

## 2. The Evidence Landscape

### 2.1 The headline finding

**The peer-reviewed evidence does not support AK’s diagnostic claims.** Every methodologically rigorous systematic review and every blinded, randomized study has either failed to find an effect or has explicitly concluded that AK performs no better than chance.

### 2.2 The most-cited systematic review

The landmark literature review is **Hall, Lewith, Brien, and Little (2008)**, published in *Forschende Komplementärmedizin*. They searched MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, and AMED  and identified 22 original studies. The studies were scored against established quality tools (QUADAS, STARD, JADAD, CONSORT).  The authors concluded:

> “There is insufficient evidence for diagnostic accuracy within kinesiology, the validity of muscle response and the effectiveness of kinesiology for any condition.  The standards of reporting were low.”[^14]

A 2025 systematic review of AK-specific MMT reliability studies (the most recent published) assessed seven studies and reported “agreement levels ranging from nonexistent to very strong”  — that is, results were inconsistent and study quality remained low.[^15]

### 2.3 Cochrane reviews

There is no Cochrane review devoted to AK as a diagnostic system. Cochrane reviews exist for adjacent areas (manipulative therapies for low-back pain, complementary therapies for allergic conditions), but none establishes AK’s validity as a diagnostic technique.

### 2.4 Inter-rater and intra-rater reliability of MMT

Reliability is the bare minimum prerequisite for validity: two examiners testing the same patient should reach the same conclusion. The Pothmann et al. (2001) study of 315 children — the largest investigation of AK for nutritional intolerance — found “a moderate test-retest reliability (intraclass-kappa 0.62) but **no inter-tester reliability (intraclass-kappa −0.01)**.”[^16] In plain language: a single examiner could roughly reproduce his own findings, but two examiners could not agree with each other at any rate better than chance.

### 2.5 Studies on nutritional deficiencies, allergies, and food sensitivities

- **Kenney, Clemens, and Forsythe (1988):** Three experienced AK practitioners independently evaluated 11 subjects for four nutrients (thiamin, zinc, vitamin A, ascorbic acid).  The study found “no significant interjudge reliability, no significant correlation between the testers and standard biochemical tests for nutrient status, and no significant correlation between mechanical and manual determinations of relative muscle strength.”  Patients responded equivalently to supplements, placebos, and substances AK had labeled “adequate.”[^17]
- **Garrow (1988)**, *British Medical Journal*: a controlled trial of kinesiology for food allergy diagnosis found no diagnostic correlation.[^18]
- **Schwartz, Utts, Spottiswoode, Shade, Tully, Morris, and Nachman (2014)**, *Explore*: a double-blind, randomized study of 51 participants asked two kinesiologists to identify which of two visually identical sealed vials contained a toxin (ionic hydroxylamine hydrochloride at 9 mg/mL) and which contained saline. The reported result was decisive: “Of the 151 sets of trials, the toxic vial was identified correctly in 80 of them (53%), resulting in a one-tailed exact binomial P-value of .258”  — statistically indistinguishable from the 50% expected by chance. The authors concluded AK “has not demonstrated that it is a useful or reliable diagnostic tool upon which health decisions can be based.”[^19]

A 2024 peer-reviewed article by Patterson et al., “Food Allergy: Unproven Diagnostics and Therapeutics” (PMC11250541), states plainly: “Cytotoxic testing, provocation-neutralization, electrodermal testing, applied kinesiology, hair analysis, and iridology lack any scientific evidence to support their use as diagnostic methods for food allergy or intolerance.”  The same review warns that therapeutics built on such tests “may be dangerous because they do not differentiate between IgE-mediated food allergy and other adverse reaction to foods.”[^20]

### 2.6 Position statements

- **American Academy of Allergy, Asthma, and Immunology:** “no evidence of diagnostic validity”  of AK for allergy diagnosis.[^21]
- **American Cancer Society (2009):** “available scientific evidence does not support the claim that applied kinesiology can diagnose or treat cancer or other illness,” and the ACS notes that AK “has occasionally resulted in harm, including one death due to incorrect diagnosis and choice of treatment.”[^22]
- **Australian Government Department of Health (2015):** the NHMRC-administered *Review of the Australian Government Rebate on Private Health Insurance for Natural Therapies* assessed 17 natural therapies and removed health-insurance rebate coverage for all 17; AK was among them on the basis that no clear evidence of effectiveness was found.[^21]
- **European Academy of Allergology and Clinical Immunology, the UK’s NICE, the Australasian Society of Clinical Immunology and Allergy, and the Allergy Society of South Africa**  have issued substantially identical statements.[^21]
- **The World Allergy Organization** lists AK among “unproven tests” in its Global Resources In Allergy educational materials and describes it as “useless.”[^21]

### 2.7 The ideomotor effect and placebo

Why do practitioners and patients experience AK as compelling even when blinded studies show no signal? The most parsimonious scientific explanation is the **ideomotor effect**: subtle, unconscious motor responses to expectation. The same mechanism explains the apparent movement of a Ouija board’s planchette  and the rotation of a dowsing rod.[^23] In MMT, both examiner and patient may slightly adjust pressure or resistance in line with their expectations about a substance  or location, producing a “weak” or “strong” reading that feels objectively detected but is, in fact, generated by the participants themselves. A 2018 paper in *Energy Psychology* titled “Emerging from the Mystical: Rethinking Muscle Response Testing as an Ideomotor Effect” — written *by* kinesiology-friendly authors — concedes the ideomotor explanation and argues for protocol changes to control for it.[^23]

Add to this the well-documented **placebo effect on motor performance** (verbal suggestion alone measurably alters muscle recruitment in the small hand muscles)[^24] and the standard cognitive biases of clinical practice (confirmation bias, regression to the mean, the natural fluctuation of chronic symptoms), and the result is a clinical experience that *feels* diagnostic without actually *being* diagnostic.

### 2.8 Why AK persists despite weak evidence

AK has not disappeared, despite forty years of negative trials, for understandable reasons:

1. **Clinical experience:** practitioners observe patients improving and reasonably credit the technique. (Improvement, however, may stem from concurrent adjustments, dietary advice, placebo, attention, or natural history.)
1. **Patient satisfaction:** patients enjoy being touched, listened to, and given concrete steps to take — all hallmarks of any caring clinical encounter, AK or otherwise.
1. **Business factors:** AK frequently anchors elaborate, ongoing supplement protocols and repeat visits, which create both a livelihood and a system that is hard to disengage from.[^1]
1. **Genuine sincerity:** most practitioners are not con artists. Sincerity and evidence are not the same thing.

## 3. Adventist Theological and Biblical Considerations

The Seventh-day Adventist Church teaches a **wholistic** view of the human person and supports **evidence-based** healthcare. Its Commitment to Health and Healing document (voted by the GC Executive Committee, 2009) commits the Church to be “regarded globally as teaching a wholistic model of evidence based healthful living in primary health care.”[^25] From the church’s earliest days, our medical tradition has stood for scientific approaches to health care over pseudoscientific ones. This is the framework within which AK must be evaluated.

### 3.1 The biblical mandate to test

Scripture is not naive about competing claims to truth, including in matters of healing. Four texts are foundational:

- **1 Thessalonians 5:21** — “Test everything; hold fast what is good”  (ESV).
- **1 John 4:1** — “Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see whether they are from God, for many false prophets have gone out into the world.” 
- **Isaiah 8:19–20** — “And when they say to you, ‘Inquire of the mediums and the necromancers who chirp and mutter,’ should not a people inquire of their God? … To the law and to the testimony! If they will not speak according to this word, it is because they have no dawn.”
- **Colossians 2:8** — “See to it that no one takes you captive by philosophy and empty deceit, according to human tradition, according to the elemental spirits of the world, and not according to Christ.”

The Greek verb in 1 Thessalonians 5:21 (*dokimazō*) is a *demanding* test — the assayer’s test for genuine  gold.[^26] Christian discernment is therefore not a vague feeling but a deliberate examination of claims against (a) Scripture and (b) accurate knowledge of reality.

### 3.2 The Deuteronomy 18 warning

The most important Old Testament backdrop is **Deuteronomy 18:10–12**: “Let no one be found among you who … practices divination or  tells fortunes or interprets omens, or a sorcerer or a charmer or a medium or a necromancer or one who inquires of the dead, for whoever does these things is an abomination to the Lord.”

The category of “divination” is broader than crystal balls. It refers to **any technique that claims to extract hidden, non-empirical knowledge — about the body, the future, or another person — through ritualized signs not grounded in observable cause-and-effect.** When an AK practitioner claims to know which of two sealed vials contains the substance the body “needs,” or to diagnose adrenal insufficiency by a downward pull on the patient’s arm, the diagnostic *mechanism* is, by every published blinded test, indistinguishable from chance. The reading is being generated either by ideomotor cueing (a natural, unconscious process) or — in the worldview from which AK partially derives — by an alleged subtle “energy” that has never been measured. Either way, the act of reading hidden information from a non-empirical sign falls within the biblical concern.

This is not the same as conventional clinical assessment. A physician who percusses the abdomen for dullness over the liver, a physical therapist who grades quadriceps strength on the Medical Research Council scale, or a chiropractor performing orthopedic range-of-motion testing are performing **observable, reproducible, validated** clinical measurements. The biblical concern is not with examination per se — it is with techniques that claim to bypass observable cause-and-effect and read into the body what the practitioner has no empirical means of knowing.

### 3.3 Eastern energy concepts: qi, prana, chakras, meridians

When AK is combined with Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) — which is the explicit posture of ICAK after Goodheart’s adoption of Felix Mann’s meridian work, and the explicit foundation of Touch for Health, NAET, NET, and BDORT — the patient is being treated within a framework whose primary metaphysical claim is that a non-physical “life energy” (qi in Chinese, prana in Sanskrit) flows through invisible channels (meridians, nadis) and through energy centers (chakras),  and that the manipulation of this energy is the cause of healing.[^27]

Dr. Allan Handysides, then director of the General Conference Health Ministries Department, addressed this directly in an Inter-American Division publication: “When you start talking about electrical energy, magnetic energy, [immeasurable] energies, you’re getting to the realm of magical thinking. New Age has in it this element of magical thinking. It gets people propounding therapeutic regimes that are not based on science, but are based on ideas, theories, hypotheses, that are not measurable or subject to analysis. If we get into that sphere of thinking, then our health message becomes totally irrational.”[^28] He further noted: “When you examine several Chinese religions, there is a belief in forces, powers, meridians of power, and even herbal medications are often thought to work through these forces.”[^28]

Manuel Vasquez’s Andrews University doctoral dissertation, *New Age Holistic Health: Implications for Seventh-day Adventist Faith and Practice* (1996), concluded: “Alternative New Age holistic health therapies can be physically and spiritually dangerous for Seventh-day Adventist Christians because these therapies are based on nonbiblical worldview philosophies, universal energy forces, astrology, the occult, and Eastern mysticism.  … It is spiritually dangerous, and sometimes physically harmful, for Christians to participate in these therapies or to think that they can separate the practices from their nonbiblical worldview philosophies and still remain loyal to their God and Savior.”[^29]

This is not paranoid sectarianism. It is the historic and consistent Christian position that the body is the temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19) and that healing comes by physical means God has built into His creation, by ordinary providence, and by the sovereign work of Christ — *not* by the manipulation of an alleged universal life-force that is conceptually equivalent to the “elemental spirits” Paul warns against in Colossians 2:8 and Galatians 4:3, 9.

### 3.4 Ellen White on “mind cure,” mesmerism, and one mind controlling another

Ellen White’s most-cited counsel relevant to AK is not directly about AK (she died fifty years before Goodheart’s 1964 discovery) but about the underlying principle: **one human will or mind being placed under the diagnostic/therapeutic control of another, in ways that bypass the patient’s own reasoning and the ordinary channels of evidence.** In *The Ministry of Healing* (1905), Chapter 18, she wrote:

> “There is, however, a form of mind cure that is one of the most effective agencies for evil. Through this so-called science, one mind is brought under the control of another so that the individuality of the weaker is merged in that of the stronger mind. One person acts out the will of another.”   (*MH* 242)[^30]

> “It is not God’s purpose that any human being should yield his mind and will to the control of another, becoming a passive instrument in his hands.   … He is not to look to any human being as the source of healing. His dependence must be in God.”  (*MH* 242)[^30]

> “It opens a door through which Satan will enter to take possession both of the mind that is given up to be controlled by another,  and of the mind that controls.”  (*MH* 242–243)[^30]

In *Medical Ministry*, she warns: “We are not to tamper with mesmerism and hypnotism, — the science of the one who lost his first estate, and was cast out of the heavenly courts.” (*MM* 110–111)[^31] And further: “This science may appear to you to be very valuable; but to you and to others it is a fallacy prepared by Satan. It is the charm of the serpent which stings to spiritual death. It covers much that seems wonderful, but it is foreign to the nature and spirit of Christ. This science does not lead to Him who is life and salvation.”   (*MM* 112)[^31]

These passages do not directly address AK. They do, however, articulate the principle by which Adventists evaluate any practice in which a practitioner’s reading of a patient’s hidden state (through means not grounded in observable cause-and-effect) becomes the basis of treatment.

### 3.5 By their fruits

Jesus’ criterion is that “by their fruits you shall know them” (Matthew 7:20). The fruits of legitimate clinical assessment include reproducible results, inter-examiner agreement, falsifiability, transparency, and humble revision in the face of contrary evidence. The fruits AK has produced, by its own published literature, are weak inter-rater reliability, results indistinguishable from chance under blinding, expensive supplement protocols whose necessity cannot be independently verified, and — in the case of derivative forms tied to chakra/meridian frameworks — the importation of non-Christian metaphysics into Christian homes under the appearance of “natural” healthcare. These fruits should give the Christian pause.

## 4. Safety and Limitations

### 4.1 Risk of delayed proper diagnosis

The most serious physical risk is **diagnostic delay**. Cancer, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, infections, autoimmune disease, and many other conditions present with vague, fluctuating symptoms  that AK readily attributes to a “weak adrenal,” “liver congestion,” or “food sensitivity.” A patient who accepts these labels and pursues a months- or years-long supplement protocol may lose the window in which conventional medicine could have made the actual diagnosis and intervened decisively.[^22]

### 4.2 Risk of unnecessary supplements or treatments

AK protocols frequently recommend extensive supplement regimens — often from a specific brand the practitioner sells. Without a validated diagnostic basis, the patient is exposed to (a) the financial cost of supplements that may run into hundreds of dollars per month, (b) the risk of nutrient excess and supplement–drug interactions, and (c) a false sense that the underlying condition is being addressed.

### 4.3 Red flags

A coach or pastor should be especially alert when a practitioner:

- claims to diagnose a wide range of medical conditions — cancer, thyroid disease, hormonal imbalance, food allergy — through muscle testing alone;
- combines AK with explicit energy-medicine language (qi, chakras, meridians, “energy blockages,” “auras”) or with named systems like NAET, BodyTalk, NET, CRA, BDORT, or “Quantum Reflex Analysis”;
- resists or discourages independent medical evaluation, conventional laboratory tests, or imaging;
- prescribes lengthy, expensive supplement protocols whose only justification is the muscle test;
- markets the technique using language such as “the body knows,” “tapping into innate intelligence,” or claims to detect “vibrational frequencies.”

### 4.4 Spiritual safety

Where the practice imports explicit Eastern religious metaphysics (chakras, qi balancing, “spirit guides,” visualization protocols, meditative mantras during testing), the Christian patient is exposed not only to ineffective medicine but to a worldview at odds with biblical theology. Even where the practitioner is a sincere Christian who has “stripped” the technique of its overt spirituality, the underlying framework — that an unmeasurable life-force is being read and adjusted — remains foreign to Scripture.

### 4.5 Financial risk

Without an evidence base, the cost of AK-driven supplement protocols, repeat visits, and add-on devices (some practitioners sell “frequency” machines, structured-water systems, and proprietary detoxes) represents money that could otherwise fund evidence-based care, healthful food, or charitable giving.

## 5. Practical Guidance for Coaches, Pastors, and Consumers

### 5.1 Questions to ask the practitioner

If a member or client is considering an AK practitioner, encourage them to ask, in advance and in writing if possible:

1. *What is your medical training and licensure, and what is your scope of practice in this state?*
1. *Do you use muscle testing as your primary diagnostic method, or only as an adjunct to conventional examination, laboratory tests, and imaging?*
1. *If muscle testing suggests one finding and conventional testing suggests another, which takes precedence?*
1. *Will you coordinate with my primary-care physician and share your findings in writing?*
1. *Do you sell the supplements you recommend? What is your financial relationship to those products?*
1. *What is your view of “energy,” “meridians,” “chakras,” or “the body’s innate intelligence”? Is this framework essential to your practice?*
1. *Are you willing to be tested under blinded conditions?*

A practitioner who cannot answer these calmly and transparently is signaling a problem.

### 5.2 Evaluating any health practice — the dual criterion

For Christian discernment, two filters must both be passed:

1. **The empirical filter:** Is the practice supported by reproducible, blinded, peer-reviewed evidence? Or is it supported only by testimonials and unblinded clinical experience?
1. **The biblical filter:** Is the practice grounded in observable cause-and-effect that God has built into creation? Or is it grounded in a metaphysical framework (qi, prana, chakras, “universal energy”) that competes with the biblical doctrine of God as the sustainer of life (Acts 17:28, Hebrews 1:3) and the Holy Spirit as the giver of life (Genesis 2:7)?

A practice that fails either filter warrants caution. A practice that fails both warrants avoidance.

### 5.3 Evidence-based alternatives

For the concerns most often brought to AK, evidence-based alternatives exist:

- **Suspected food allergy or sensitivity:** consult a board-certified allergist for skin-prick testing, serum IgE testing, or — the gold standard — physician-supervised oral food challenges. For non-IgE intolerances, supervised elimination-and-reintroduction diets directed by a registered dietitian.
- **Nutritional concerns:** consult a Registered Dietitian Nutritionist (RDN), order standard laboratory testing (CBC, comprehensive metabolic panel, lipids, ferritin, B12, vitamin D, thyroid function), and address findings systematically.
- **Musculoskeletal complaints:** see a board-certified physical therapist, physiatrist, sports-medicine physician, or chiropractor who uses validated assessment.
- **Functional/integrative concerns:** seek a physician with board certification in internal or family medicine who practices evidence-based integrative medicine and is transparent about which interventions are evidence-supported and which are exploratory.
- **Mental and emotional health:** evidence-based psychotherapy (CBT, ACT, EMDR for trauma), pastoral counseling from a trained pastor, and, where indicated, psychiatric care.

### 5.4 Pastoral counseling for those who have used AK

Many sincere Adventists have already used AK in good faith. Pastoral counsel here should be neither alarmist nor dismissive. Recommended approach:

1. **Listen first.** Understand what drew the person to AK — often unmet needs in conventional care (rushed visits, lack of explanation, dismissal of suffering).
1. **Affirm the legitimate desire.** The desire to be heard, examined wholistically, and addressed as a whole person is a *Christian* desire, modeled in Christ’s own healing ministry (*Ministry of Healing*, ch. 1).
1. **Distinguish levels.** Help the person see the difference between (a) an AK practitioner who used the technique alongside legitimate chiropractic care for a back problem, and (b) one who claimed to diagnose disease, sold expensive supplements, or imported energy-medicine concepts.
1. **Address spiritual concerns gently.** If the person experienced fear, oppression, or spiritual disturbance during or after AK-related sessions (especially those involving chakra work, “clearing,” or guided visualization), pray with them, share the assurance of 1 John 1:9 and Colossians 1:13, and — if persistent — involve a trusted pastor and a Christian counselor.
1. **Avoid scrupulosity.** A person who once visited an AK chiropractor is not “demonized.” Christ’s grace is greater than our errors.
1. **Redirect to evidence-based care** and to the Bible’s own resources for healing: prayer, healthful living, the counsel of qualified physicians, and the community of faith.

## 6. Conclusion: Holding Two Things Together

Christians are not called to be credulous, and we are not called to be cynical. We are called to “test everything; hold fast what is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:21). On the testing of AK, the verdict of forty years of peer-reviewed research is clear: as a diagnostic system, it does not reliably do what it claims to do. On the holding fast of what is good, our calling is to point those we serve to the things that *are* good — accurate diagnosis, honest treatment, the body’s God-built capacity to heal when given rest, real food, clean water, sunlight, exercise, fresh air, and trust in God; the community of the faithful; and the great Physician Himself, who alone reads the heart and to whom alone we yield our minds.

The sincerity of an AK practitioner is real. Some patients have experienced real benefit from the surrounding care (the listening, the dietary advice, the chiropractic adjustment, the placebo, the natural history of illness). But the technique itself, when honestly tested, does not pass. And when it is wedded to qi, meridians, and “universal energy,” it imports a framework foreign to Scripture. For both reasons, the Adventist coach and ministry leader can with quiet confidence guide those they serve toward better.

> “Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see whether they are from God.” — 1 John 4:1

> “To the law and to the testimony! If they will not speak according to this word, it is because they have no dawn.” — Isaiah 8:20
