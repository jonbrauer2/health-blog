## TL;DR

- Canker sores (recurrent aphthous stomatitis, or RAS) are the most common ulcer disease of the mouth, affecting about 20% of people; they are an immune-driven inflammation — NOT contagious, and completely different from cold sores.
- The strongest “do something today” levers are checking for and correcting nutrient shortfalls (especially vitamin B12, iron/ferritin, and folate), switching to a toothpaste without sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS), and managing stress and sleep.
- Most canker sores are harmless and heal in 1–2 weeks; see a professional for any sore larger than 1 cm, lasting more than 3 weeks, or paired with fever, weight loss, gut symptoms, or ulcers elsewhere on the body.

## Key Findings

Canker sores are common, poorly understood, and frequently undertreated. Below is the evidence-graded picture of what triggers them and what actually helps.

## Details

### 1. The 2 a.m. Tongue Test

You know the feeling. You’re brushing your teeth, or biting into a crisp apple, and suddenly there’s that small, fiery spot inside your lip or under your tongue. Over the next day it blossoms into a round, white-or-yellow crater ringed in angry red. Eating hurts. Talking hurts. Even smiling can sting. And then, just to be cruel, it shows up again a few weeks later.

If this is your life, you are very much not alone. Canker sores — known to clinicians as recurrent aphthous stomatitis, or RAS — are the most common ulcer-type disease of the mouth’s lining.[^1] As César Rivera’s 2019 review puts it, RAS “is the most common disease of the oral mucosa,”   affecting roughly 20% of the population, with estimates ranging widely from about 5% to 60% depending on the group studied.[^2] Independent reviews agree: approximately 20% of the general population — about 1 in 5 people — is affected, with incidence varying from 5% to 50% depending on the ethnic and socioeconomic groups studied.[ ^4]

Here’s the frustrating part: unlike cavities or gum disease, you usually can’t just brush, floss, or rinse your way out of canker sores.[ ^1] And because they’re “just” mouth sores, they’re often shrugged off — by patients and professionals alike. But the real-world burden is significant. The pain can interfere with eating, drinking, brushing, and speaking, and recurrent outbreaks take a measurable toll on quality of life.[^3] This handout pulls together what the research actually says — sorted honestly by how strong the evidence is — so you can stop guessing and start making informed choices.

### 2. What Are Canker Sores? (And What They’re Not)

Let’s clear up the single most important point first, because confusing it causes real harm: **canker sores are not cold sores.**

- **Canker sores (aphthous ulcers)** form *inside* the mouth — on the soft, movable tissues like the inner cheeks, the underside of the tongue, the inner lips, and the soft palate. They are not caused by a virus and are **not contagious.**[ ^4]
- **Cold sores (fever blisters)** are caused by the herpes simplex virus (usually HSV-1). They form *outside* the mouth — on or around the lips and nearby skin — usually as clusters of fluid-filled blisters, and they **are** contagious.[ ^4]

A simple rule of thumb: if it’s inside the mouth on soft tissue, it’s most likely a canker sore; if it’s on the lip border or face, it’s most likely a cold sore.[ ^5] This distinction matters enormously. People with canker sores are sometimes wrongly told they have herpes, which brings unnecessary stigma, relationship strain, and the wrong treatment. Meanwhile, the antiviral creams that help cold sores do nothing for canker sores.

**So what is actually happening?** A canker sore is a small patch of immune-mediated inflammation. The body’s own T-cells (a type of white blood cell) mount a localized attack on the lining of the mouth, breaking down the surface tissue and creating the ulcer.[^6] The exact trigger that sets off this immune response in any given person remains officially unknown, which is why there’s still no cure — only ways to reduce frequency, pain, and healing time.[^7]

**The three types:**

- **Minor RAS** — by far the most common, making up roughly 80% of cases. These are small (about 3–10 mm across),  heal within 1–2 weeks, and leave no scar.[^2]
- **Major RAS** — larger than 1 cm, deeper, and slower to heal (sometimes up to 6 weeks), and they can leave scars.[^8]
- **Herpetiform RAS** — despite the misleading name (they have nothing to do with herpes), these appear as clusters of many tiny ulcers (2–3 mm) that can merge into larger irregular sores. They’re often misdiagnosed.[^8]

**Patterns over a lifetime:** RAS most often first appears in the second or third decade of life (roughly the teens to thirties) and tends to become less frequent and less severe with age.[^8] There’s a strong genetic thread, too — having one or both parents with a history of canker sores raises your odds, and those with a family history tend to get them earlier and more severely.[ ^9]

### 3. Why Do They Keep Coming Back? — Triggers and Underlying Factors

Canker sores are what researchers call “multifactorial” — there’s rarely one single cause. Think of it like a threshold: several factors stack up until your immune system tips over into making an ulcer. Here’s the honest evidence rundown.

#### Well-Supported Triggers

**Physical trauma.** This is one of the clearest triggers. Biting your cheek, a poke from a sharp chip or crusty bread, an ill-fitting denture, braces, or even aggressive brushing can all start an ulcer in susceptible people.[^10] Trauma alone won’t cause canker sores in someone who never gets them — but in people prone to RAS, it’s a common spark.

**Stress and the stress–immune connection.** The link between psychological stress and canker sores is well documented. Multiple studies have found higher anxiety levels and elevated salivary cortisol (a stress hormone) in people with active RAS.[^11] One 2024 cross-sectional study of 248 people found significantly higher depression, anxiety, and stress scores in RAS patients than in healthy controls.[^12] Researchers consistently suggest that stress acts as a precipitating factor  — and that stress-management approaches may be a useful add-on to conventional care.[^11] Notably, a study by Gallo and colleagues found that experiencing a stressful life event increased the odds of an RAS episode by almost three times.[ ^13]

**Hormonal fluctuations.** Some people notice their sores cluster around their menstrual cycle, and several small studies report ulcers being more frequent during the luteal phase (the second half of the cycle).[^14] However, the highest-quality critical review of this question — by McCartan and Sullivan — concluded that “no associations have been established” between aphthous stomatitis and the premenstrual period, pregnancy, or menopause, and that no properly designed study has shown a therapeutic effect of ovarian hormones  on RAS.[^15] Bottom line: a hormonal link is plausible and commonly reported, but remains unproven.

**Nutritional deficiencies.** This is one of the most practically useful areas. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis by Mohammadi and colleagues found that people with RAS were significantly more likely to be deficient in several nutrients: the odds ratio for vitamin B12 deficiency was 2.93 (95% CI 2.28–3.78), for low ferritin (an iron-storage marker) 2.50 (1.48–4.22), and for decreased hemoglobin 2.14 (1.38–3.32) compared with healthy individuals.[  ^16] An older landmark study by Wray and colleagues found hematinic (blood-nutrient) deficiencies in up to 21% of adult RAS patients, and replacement therapy helped a majority of those patients.[ ^17] We’ll cover what to do about this in Section 4.

**Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) in toothpaste.** SLS is the detergent that makes toothpaste foam. It’s also a known irritant to the mouth’s lining. A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis by Alli and colleagues pooled randomized crossover trials (4 crossover trials, n=124) and found that SLS-free toothpaste significantly reduced the number of ulcers, their duration, the number of episodes, and ulcer pain compared with SLS-containing toothpaste.[ ^18] The pooled estimate for ulcer count (2 studies, 66 participants) was a mean difference of −1.05 ulcers (95% CI −1.87 to −0.23)  — a real but modest effect, which is why reviewers describe the evidence as limited but consistent.[^19] That’s enough to make switching a low-risk, reasonable experiment.

#### Emerging or Partially-Supported Triggers

**Food sensitivities (gluten, dairy, acidic foods).** Some people genuinely flare with specific foods. The gluten connection is the best studied. A study of 247 RAS patients by Shakeri and colleagues found that 2.83% (7 of 247) had gluten-sensitive enteropathy (celiac disease) — about three times the 0.9% background rate in that population  — and 4 patients who adhered to a strict gluten-free diet showed noticeable improvement in their lesions over six months.[ ^20] Beyond diagnosed celiac, some individuals report improvement reducing gluten or dairy, though controlled evidence for non-celiac sensitivity is thin. Acidic and spicy foods (citrus, tomatoes, vinegar, pineapple) are widely reported to aggravate existing sores, more as irritants than root causes.[^10]

**The gut–oral microbiome connection.** This is a genuinely active research frontier. Studies using modern DNA sequencing have found that people with RAS show shifts in their oral bacterial communities — for example, decreased *Streptococcus salivarius* and increased *Acinetobacter johnsonii*  — a state called dysbiosis (an imbalance in the microbial community).[^21] A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed an association between RAS and altered oral microbial diversity.[^22] What’s *proposed* is that an imbalanced microbiome — in the mouth or gut — may disturb immune tolerance and contribute to ulcers. What remains *speculative* is whether this is a cause or a consequence,  and no specific microbe has been proven to cause RAS.[^21]

**Systemic inflammatory conditions.** Recurrent mouth ulcers are sometimes the visible tip of a deeper condition. Behçet’s disease — a rare inflammatory blood-vessel disorder — features recurrent oral ulcers in 70% or more of patients, often as the first sign.[ ^23] Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn’s and ulcerative colitis) and celiac disease can also drive mouth ulcers. This is why a pattern of severe, frequent, or unusual ulcers — especially with other symptoms — deserves medical evaluation (see Section 6).

#### Discussed in Wellness Communities — An Honest Look

**Mercury amalgam (“silver”) fillings.** Here the evidence supports a narrow, specific phenomenon — not the broad claims sometimes made. In a small minority of people, the mercury in amalgam fillings can trigger a localized allergic reaction called an *oral lichenoid lesion* — a sore or white patch in the tissue directly touching the filling.[ ^24] When these are confirmed by patch testing and the filling is replaced, the lesions often heal.[^25] However, mainstream reviews conclude that amalgam does not pose a serious general health risk except for this exceedingly small number of hypersensitivity reactions,  and these lichenoid lesions are not the same thing as typical canker sores.[^24] Removing healthy fillings in hopes of curing ordinary RAS is not supported by evidence.

**Chronic low-grade “acidosis” and alkaline diets.** This is a popular idea in wellness circles — the notion that an “acidic” diet causes systemic acidity that drives inflammation and sores, fixable with an alkaline diet. Here, honesty requires being clear: there is no controlled clinical evidence that an alkaline diet or “correcting acidosis” prevents or treats canker sores, and the idea conflicts with basic physiology.[^26] The body regulates blood pH within an extremely narrow range (about 7.35–7.45) through the lungs and kidneys; diet can change *urine* pH but does not meaningfully change *blood* pH in healthy people.[^26] One case-control study found no significant difference in dietary habits between RAS patients and controls.[ ^27] A whole-food, plant-rich diet has plenty of genuine benefits (below) — but “alkalizing” your blood isn’t one of the mechanisms.

### 4. Nutrition, Lifestyle, and Functional Approaches

#### Nutritional Interventions (graded by evidence)

**Vitamin B12 — reasonably good evidence.** This is one of the most promising single nutrients. A 2009 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial by Volkov and colleagues (n=58; 31 intervention, 27 control) gave RAS patients a daily 1,000 mcg sublingual (under-the-tongue) B12 tablet for six months.   The results were striking: duration of outbreaks, number of ulcers, and pain levels all dropped significantly, and 74.1% of the treated group reached “no aphthous ulcers status” versus 32.0% in the placebo group (P < .01).[ ^28] Importantly, the benefit appeared *regardless of whether the person started out B12-deficient*  — and the treatment is cheap, simple, and very safe.[^28] **A note for plant-based readers:** B12 occurs naturally almost only in animal foods, so anyone eating fully vegan or mostly plant-based should be supplementing B12 anyway — and that’s doubly relevant if you get canker sores.[^29]

**Iron and ferritin.** Low iron stores (measured by ferritin) are repeatedly associated with RAS.[^16] While there are no large randomized trials proving that iron supplements prevent ulcers, the deficiency-correction studies suggest that fixing a documented shortfall reduces outbreaks in those who are deficient.[^17] The practical move is to get tested rather than guess — and if you supplement iron, include fiber, since iron can cause constipation.[ ^30]

**Zinc — mixed evidence.** Zinc supports immune function and wound healing, and the evidence is genuinely split. A 2020 double-blind randomized trial by Mehdipour and colleagues found that a zinc-sulfate mucoadhesive (stick-on) tablet significantly reduced ulcer size and pain.[^31] Yet an older crossover trial by Wray found no benefit from systemic zinc and noted some people had to stop due to side effects.[^32] The fairest reading: zinc may help, especially topically or when you’re deficient, but it’s not a sure thing.

**Folate (folic acid).** Folate deficiency is associated with RAS, and like B12 and iron, it shows up in deficiency studies.[^16] Folate is abundant in leafy greens, legumes, and fortified grains — a strength of plant-based eating when well planned.

**Vitamin D — emerging evidence.** Multiple studies and a meta-analysis have found that people with RAS tend to have significantly lower vitamin D levels than healthy controls.[^33] What’s not yet clear is whether low vitamin D *causes* the ulcers or simply travels alongside them;  supplementation trials are still needed.[^33] Still, correcting a documented vitamin D deficiency is worthwhile for many reasons.

**Vitamin C — traditional use, limited research.** Vitamin C supports collagen and tissue repair, and there’s some small-study interest, but high-quality evidence specific to canker sores is limited.

**An important reality check:** When researchers tested a complete daily multivitamin (at standard recommended intakes) in a rigorous 2012 randomized trial of 160 people, it did *not* reduce the number or duration of canker sore episodes — the multivitamin group averaged 4.19 episodes versus 4.60 on placebo (P = .69), with similar episode durations.[ ^34] The authors concluded clinicians should not routinely recommend multivitamin supplementation as prophylaxis for RAS.  The lesson: blanket multivitamins aren’t the answer — *targeted* correction of a *specific, tested* deficiency is what the evidence supports.

#### Dietary Patterns

**Whole-food, plant-based eating.** A well-planned plant-based diet is rich in folate, anti-inflammatory compounds, and fiber that supports a healthy microbiome.[^21] The two cautions are real, though: poorly planned plant-based diets risk B12 deficiency (B12 isn’t in plant foods) and sometimes low iron — both of which are linked to canker sores.[^29] So the advice is simple: go plant-rich if you like, but supplement B12 and keep an eye on iron.

**Gluten reduction.** This is worth a targeted try only for the right people. If you have frequent canker sores plus gut symptoms, fatigue, or a family history of celiac disease, ask to be *screened for celiac before* cutting gluten (the blood test only works while you’re still eating gluten).[^20] For diagnosed celiac or gluten-sensitive individuals, a gluten-free diet can substantially reduce ulcers; for everyone else, the evidence is weak.

**Dropping SLS toothpaste.** A concrete, low-cost step: read your toothpaste label and choose one without “sodium lauryl sulfate.”[^18] Several mainstream brands offer SLS-free options.

**Acidic and spicy foods.** The evidence here is mostly about *comfort during* an outbreak rather than prevention. Cutting back on citrus, tomatoes, vinegar, and spicy dishes while a sore is healing reduces irritation; there’s little evidence that permanently avoiding them prevents new sores.[^10]

#### Lifestyle Factors

**Sleep and circadian rhythm.** Sleep is when the immune system recalibrates. Lack of sleep is reported among the factors associated with RAS outbreaks, and good sleep supports the stress-recovery and immune regulation that keep ulcers at bay.[^18]

**Stress reduction.** Given the well-documented stress link, this is one of the most evidence-aligned lifestyle levers.[^11] Researchers specifically suggest that stress-management interventions and psychological support may benefit RAS patients alongside conventional treatment.[^11] Practices that lower physiological stress — mindfulness, regular rest, time outdoors, and especially genuine social connection and a sense of purpose — are reasonable to pursue both for sores and for overall wellbeing.

**Oral hygiene.** Good, *gentle* oral hygiene helps; aggressive brushing with a stiff brush can cause the very trauma that triggers sores.[^10] A soft brush, careful technique, and an SLS-free toothpaste are the practical combination.

#### Topical and Natural Remedies (honest evidence framing)

**Aloe vera gel — limited but positive.** A 2022 three-arm randomized placebo-controlled trial found that topical aloe vera gel accelerated ulcer healing and reduced pain, even outperforming the medication amlexanox on day 5.[^35] Reasonable, low-risk, and supported by small trials.

**Honey (including manuka/thyme honey) — some good trial evidence.** Honey has performed surprisingly well. A randomized controlled trial found honey significantly reduced ulcer size, days of pain, and redness compared to a corticosteroid and a standard paste.[^36] A 2025 trial of thyme honey found it reduced ulcer size and pain more than the steroid triamcinolone on several days.[^37] Among natural remedies, honey has some of the stronger supporting data.

**Licorice root / DGL — used widely, modest evidence.** Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL — a form with a blood-pressure-raising compound removed) has a long history for mouth ulcers. A 2021 systematic review of clinical trials concluded licorice significantly reduced RAS pain, ulcer size, and healing time, typically within 4–8 days,  with no reported adverse effects.[ ^38] A randomized trial of licorice gargle found significantly faster pain relief than placebo.[ ^39] The older studies are weaker, but the overall signal is encouraging for a low-risk option.

**Propolis — emerging research.** Propolis (a resin bees make) has small but positive data. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot study found that 500 mg daily  significantly reduced the frequency of outbreaks.[^40] A 2024 systematic review of 10 trials (825 participants)  concluded propolis may decrease healing time, pain, and redness.[^41]

**Salt water rinses — traditional, soothing, low-level evidence.** Recommended by major authorities including the Mayo Clinic as a soothing home measure, salt water (and baking soda) rinses are cheap and safe.[ ^42] Lab research shows saline promotes the migration of gum-tissue healing cells, supporting the long-held belief  — but no controlled trial proves saline rinses heal canker sores faster.[^43] It’s a low-risk comfort measure, not a proven cure.

**Coconut oil / oil pulling — very limited evidence.** Despite popularity, there is no randomized trial showing oil pulling treats or prevents canker sores, and for general oral health the American Dental Association explicitly does not recommend it due to lack of evidence.[ ^44] There’s also a documented (if rare) risk of lung problems from accidentally inhaling oil.[ ^44]

**Myrrh — traditional use, limited evidence.** Myrrh appears in some over-the-counter oral preparations and has a long traditional history for mouth sores, but high-quality clinical evidence specific to RAS is limited.

### 5. Conventional Medical Treatments — What They Offer and Their Limits

It’s worth being clear-eyed: no conventional treatment *cures* RAS either. They aim to reduce pain, speed healing, and cut down frequency.[^7]

- **Topical corticosteroids — first-line.** Steroid gels, ointments, or rinses (such as triamcinolone, clobetasol, or a betamethasone rinse) are the standard first treatment. They calm the immune-inflammatory attack and are most useful applied early.[^45]
- **Antiseptic/anti-inflammatory rinses.** Chlorhexidine mouthwash and dexamethasone rinses are commonly used to reduce secondary infection and inflammation.[^46]
- **Cauterization.** For stubborn individual ulcers, a clinician may chemically cauterize them with silver nitrate or debacterol; one trial found silver nitrate gave the fastest pain relief among several options.[^40]
- **Systemic treatments for severe/major RAS.** When ulcers are severe, frequent, or major-type, doctors may prescribe systemic drugs — colchicine (especially when linked to Behçet’s), pentoxifylline, short courses of oral corticosteroids, or, in serious refractory cases, dapsone or thalidomide.[^47] These carry meaningful risks and require careful physician supervision — thalidomide in particular causes severe birth defects and must never be used in pregnancy.[^47]

**A scope-of-practice note:** Systemic treatments and any concerning or unusual ulcer are squarely a physician’s or dentist’s territory. Most everyday canker sore management — nutrition, toothpaste choice, stress, gentle topical remedies — is within reach of an individual or a coach, but prescribing and diagnosing serious disease is not.

### 6. When to Be Concerned — Referral Triggers

Most canker sores are harmless. But some mouth ulcers are not canker sores at all, and a few signs should always prompt professional evaluation. See a doctor or dentist promptly if you notice any of the following:

- **An ulcer larger than 1 cm, or one that does not heal within 3 weeks.**[^48] A persistent ulcer is the single most important red flag for oral cancer.
- **Fever, fatigue, or other whole-body symptoms** accompanying the sores.[^12]
- **Increasing frequency or severity over time** rather than the usual fading with age.[^8]
- **Unintended weight loss**, or **gut symptoms** (diarrhea, cramping, blood) that might point to celiac disease or inflammatory bowel disease.[^20]
- **Ulcers elsewhere on the body** — especially genital ulcers, eye inflammation, or skin lesions, which can signal Behçet’s disease.[^23]
- **Suspicion of oral cancer.** A cancerous ulcer often looks and behaves differently: it may be **painless**, have **hard, raised, or irregular (rolled) edges**, and **persist or grow** rather than heal.[^49] This is critically important to distinguish — a typical canker sore is painful and heals; an ulcer that is painless, hard, and lasting more than  3 weeks needs a biopsy.[^48]
- **Immunocompromised individuals** (for example, people with HIV or on immune-suppressing drugs), in whom severe ulcers can signal a bigger problem.[^45]
- **Children with a first or unusual presentation**, where proper diagnosis matters most.

### 7. Contested and Controversial Topics

Rather than declaring winners and losers, here’s where the science actually sits on the most-debated questions — along a spectrum from well-supported to insufficient.

**SLS-free toothpaste: well-supported but with limits.** Multiple randomized crossover trials and a meta-analysis show fewer and less painful ulcers on SLS-free toothpaste.[^18] The catch is that the trials are small and some reviewers rate the certainty as limited.[^19] Given that switching costs little and carries no risk, it’s a sensible experiment — just don’t expect a guaranteed cure.

**Gluten and non-celiac gluten sensitivity: real for some, contested broadly.** For the small subset of RAS patients with celiac or gluten-sensitive enteropathy, a gluten-free diet genuinely helps.[^20] For people *without* any gluten-related condition, the evidence that cutting gluten reduces canker sores is weak. The mainstream position is: screen for celiac first, treat it if present, and don’t assume gluten is the culprit for everyone.

**The gut–oral axis and microbiome: promising but early.** This is the most exciting frontier — and the most over-claimed. Real studies show microbial differences between people with and without RAS.[^21] But the field has not established cause versus effect, identified a culprit organism, or proven that probiotics or microbiome “resets” prevent ulcers.[^22] Treat current microbiome marketing claims with healthy skepticism while watching the research.

**Mercury fillings and oral health: a narrow truth inside a big myth.** The defensible fact: a small number of people develop localized lichenoid lesions from mercury allergy, confirmable by patch testing and reversible by replacing the specific filling.[^24] The overreach: that amalgam fillings broadly cause canker sores or systemic disease, justifying wholesale removal. Mainstream dentistry does not support removing healthy amalgams for this reason, and these lesions aren’t classic RAS.[^24] This is also a clear scope-of-practice line: diagnosing and replacing fillings is a dentist’s job.

### 8. Key Facts

> **🦷 CANKER SORE KEY FACTS — Share These**
> 
> 1. **About 1 in 5 people (≈20%)** get recurrent canker sores — making them the most common ulcer disease of the mouth’s lining.[^2]
> 1. **Canker sores are NOT cold sores.** They’re not caused by a virus and are not contagious  — cold sores (herpes) are both.[^4]
> 1. In a randomized trial, a daily **1,000 mcg under-the-tongue B12** tablet helped **74.1% of users reach “no ulcers” status**, versus 32.0% on placebo — and it worked even in people who weren’t B12-deficient.[ ^28]
> 1. People with **low vitamin B12 were 2.9× more likely**, and those with **low iron stores 2.5× more likely**, to have recurrent canker sores.[ ^16]
> 1. Switching to **SLS-free toothpaste** significantly reduced the number, duration, and pain of ulcers  in pooled randomized trials.[^18]
> 1. **Any mouth ulcer lasting more than 3 weeks** should be checked by a professional — it’s the key warning sign for oral cancer.[^48]

### 9. Living Well with Canker Sores — A Practical Summary

**What you can do starting today:**

- **Switch toothpastes.** Pick one without sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) and brush gently with a soft brush.[^18]
- **Soothe smartly.** Rinse with warm salt water or a baking-soda rinse, and try a low-risk topical like aloe vera gel or honey on the sore.[^42]
- **Ease the irritants.** While a sore is healing, go easy on citrus, tomatoes, vinegar, and spicy foods.[^10]
- **Tend to stress and sleep.** Protect your sleep and build in genuine recovery — rest, connection, and a sense of purpose all support the immune balance that keeps ulcers away.[^11]

**What’s worth discussing with your doctor:**

- **Ask for blood work** to check ferritin (iron stores), vitamin B12, folate, and vitamin D — and correct any *documented* shortfall rather than guessing.[^16]
- **Consider a B12 trial.** A daily 1,000 mcg sublingual B12 is cheap, safe, and backed by a positive randomized trial — worth raising with your clinician.[^28]
- **Get screened before cutting gluten** if you have gut symptoms or a family history of celiac disease.[^20]
- **Flag the red flags.** Any sore over 1 cm, lasting beyond 3 weeks, or paired with fever, weight loss, gut symptoms, or sores elsewhere deserves prompt evaluation.[^48]

**A wider window.** There’s something worth sitting with here: the mouth is often an early window into the rest of the body. Recurrent ulcers can reflect nutrient stores, stress load, sleep, gut health, and immune balance — the same foundations that shape overall wellbeing. Caring for canker sores well isn’t just about the sore; it’s an invitation to tend to the whole person: how you eat, how you rest, how you handle stress, and how connected you feel. That’s good medicine for far more than your mouth.

## Recommendations

1. **Start with the no-risk basics now:** switch to SLS-free toothpaste, brush gently, use salt-water rinses and a topical (aloe or honey) for comfort, and reduce acidic/spicy foods during flares.
1. **Get tested, then target:** ask for ferritin, B12, folate, and vitamin D levels; correct documented deficiencies rather than taking a blanket multivitamin (which a rigorous trial showed doesn’t help).
1. **Trial sublingual B12** (1,000 mcg daily) with your clinician — strong safety, positive randomized evidence, especially relevant if you eat plant-based.
1. **Address stress and sleep** as genuine, evidence-aligned levers, not afterthoughts.
1. **Escalate appropriately:** if outbreaks are severe or frequent, ask about first-line topical corticosteroids; if you have gut symptoms, get celiac screening before changing your diet.
1. **Know the thresholds that change everything:** an ulcer over 1 cm, lasting more than 3 weeks, painless/hard-edged, or accompanied by systemic or genital/eye/skin symptoms — see a professional without delay.

## Caveats

- RAS has no cure; all current treatments — conventional and natural — aim to reduce pain, speed healing, or cut frequency, not eliminate the condition.
- Much of the natural-remedy evidence comes from small trials; “limited but positive” means promising, not proven.
- Deficiency correction helps most in people who are actually deficient; supplementing without testing is less likely to help (B12 being a partial exception).
- Individual triggers vary widely — what helps one person may not help another, so a degree of personal experimentation (within safe limits) is reasonable.
- This handout is educational and not a substitute for personalized diagnosis and care.
