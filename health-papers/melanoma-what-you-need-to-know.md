Every year, doctors discover a new melanoma somewhere on someone's body roughly every four minutes in the United States.[^1] It is not the most common skin cancer — that distinction belongs to basal cell and squamous cell carcinomas, which outnumber melanoma cases by a wide margin. But melanoma is the one that kills. It accounts for only about 1% of skin cancers while causing the large majority of skin cancer deaths.[^1] That gap between incidence and mortality tells you something important about this disease: early, it is nearly always curable. Late, it is a different story entirely.

The other thing worth knowing upfront: the story of melanoma has genuinely changed in recent years. Treatments that did not exist fifteen years ago are now helping people with advanced disease live years longer than they would have otherwise. Mortality rates have been declining.[^4] There is real reason for hope here, alongside real reason to take the disease seriously.

This document covers what melanoma is, who is at risk, how to recognize it early, what conventional treatment looks like, and what lifestyle research suggests about supporting the body before, during, and after treatment. It is written for people who want to understand the evidence landscape — not just a summary of what the guidelines say, but an honest look at what we know, what is still emerging, and where gaps remain.

## What Is Melanoma — and What Makes It Different?

Melanoma begins in melanocytes — the cells responsible for producing melanin, the pigment that gives skin, hair, and eyes their color. Melanocytes are primarily found in the outermost layer of skin (the epidermis), though they also appear in the eyes and mucous membranes. When DNA damage triggers uncontrolled growth in these cells, the result is melanoma.[^2]

What makes melanoma distinctly dangerous is not that it is more common, but that it metastasizes aggressively. The more common skin cancers — basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) — arise from different cell types and tend to grow slowly, with a strong tendency to remain localized. BCC rarely spreads to distant organs. SCC can, but typically sends out warning signs that are difficult to ignore.[^2] Melanoma, by contrast, has the capacity to invade lymph nodes and travel to the lungs, liver, brain, and bone while the original lesion on the skin is still relatively small.[^3]

### The Major Subtypes

Melanoma presents in several distinct forms, and the differences matter clinically.[^3]

**Superficial spreading melanoma** is the most common type, accounting for roughly 70% of cases. It tends to grow outward along the skin surface before it grows downward, which often gives it the color variation, irregular borders, and asymmetry that the standard ABCDE detection criteria are designed to catch. It is most common in fair-skinned individuals and typically appears on the trunk (in men) or the legs (in women).[^3]

**Nodular melanoma** is faster-moving and more dangerous. It grows downward from the start — vertically rather than horizontally — which means it can become thick and invasive while still appearing relatively small and symmetrical. It often presents as a raised, firm, blue-black bump and may bleed. This subtype is where the standard ABCDE criteria can mislead: a nodular melanoma can lack the obvious irregularity that most people are told to look for.[^3]

**Lentigo maligna melanoma** develops most often in older adults on chronically sun-exposed skin — the face, neck, and ears. It grows slowly and spends years as a flat, precancerous lesion (lentigo maligna) before progressing to invasive melanoma. Treatment is often challenging because of its location and tendency to have unclear margins.[^3]

**Acral lentiginous melanoma (ALM)** arises on the palms of the hands, soles of the feet, or beneath the nails. It is the least common major subtype among people of European descent — accounting for roughly 1% of melanoma cases in that population — but it represents over 50% of melanomas among people of African, Hispanic, and Asian descent.[^5] It is critical that this be understood clearly: darker skin does not protect against melanoma. It changes where melanoma tends to appear and, consequently, how and when it tends to be found.

### Staging in Plain Terms

Melanoma is staged from I through IV, reflecting how far the disease has spread.[^3]

Stage I and II melanomas are confined to the skin. The key distinction is Breslow thickness — how deep the tumor has grown into the skin layers. A thin Stage I melanoma detected early carries excellent five-year survival rates, often above 95–99%. Stage II tumors are thicker and may have other features that increase recurrence risk, but are still localized.[^4]

Stage III melanoma has reached nearby lymph nodes or skin in the immediate region. Five-year survival varies widely depending on the specifics. Stage IV means the cancer has spread to distant organs — historically the most difficult stage to treat, and the stage where the recent revolution in treatment has had the most dramatic impact.[^4]

## Who Is at Risk — and Some Answers May Surprise You

### The Usual List Is Incomplete

UV radiation — from the sun and from artificial tanning devices — is the primary environmental driver of melanoma development. DNA damage from UV radiation accumulates over time, and certain mutations (particularly in genes controlling cell growth, like BRAF and CDKN2A) play a central role in tumorigenesis.[^2][^3] But the common shorthand — "melanoma is a white-skinned person's disease" — is dangerously incomplete, and worth examining carefully.

**Fair skin, light eyes, red or blond hair:** These features are associated with reduced melanin production, which means less natural photoprotection and greater UV-induced DNA damage per exposure. Lifetime melanoma risk for non-Hispanic white individuals in the U.S. is approximately 1 in 33. For Hispanic individuals, it is approximately 1 in 200. For Black individuals, approximately 1 in 1,000.[^1] These differences are real and significant.

But raw incidence rates do not tell the whole story, and they can create a false sense of security.

> **Key Fact:** In the United States, Black patients with acral lentiginous melanoma face a significantly higher risk of mortality compared to white patients with the same subtype — with a hazard ratio of approximately 2.04 — even when controlling for stage at diagnosis and socioeconomic factors.[^6] The disparity appears to involve more than delayed diagnosis alone.

**Darker skin tones and the hidden burden:** Among people with darker skin, melanoma is disproportionately the acral lentiginous subtype, appearing in areas not typically associated with sun exposure — under fingernails and toenails, on the soles of the feet, on the palms.[^5][^6] These locations are less likely to be examined during routine skin checks, are often overlooked by patients and clinicians alike, and the disease may be misattributed to benign causes for months or years. People of color are more likely to be diagnosed at a later, more advanced stage — a disparity driven by multiple factors, including reduced access to dermatologic care, underrepresentation in awareness campaigns, and under-recognition by both patients and providers.[^5]

**Family history and genetic risk:** Having a first-degree relative with melanoma roughly doubles the lifetime risk. Specific gene mutations — including CDKN2A (which regulates cell cycle control) and MC1R (which influences pigmentation) — confer additional heritable risk. The BRAF V600E mutation, present in roughly 50% of melanomas, is typically acquired rather than inherited, but its presence shapes treatment options significantly.[^3][^7]

**Moles and dysplastic nevi:** The total number of ordinary moles on the body correlates with melanoma risk. People with more than 50 moles have an elevated baseline risk; those with more than 100 moles have roughly a sevenfold increase in risk.[^1] Dysplastic nevi (atypical moles with irregular features) carry higher individual malignant potential and serve as a marker of overall elevated risk. Regular dermatologic surveillance is warranted for people with multiple dysplastic nevi.

**Tanning beds:** This is no longer a matter of debate.

> **Key Fact:** A 2025 study from Northwestern Medicine and the University of California, San Francisco — analyzing over 5,800 patients — found that tanning bed users had nearly three times the risk of developing melanoma compared to non-users (odds ratio 2.85), with a clear dose-response relationship: more sessions, higher risk.[^8] The study also showed, for the first time at the molecular level, that tanning bed radiation causes melanoma-linked DNA mutations across broad areas of skin, including regions not normally exposed to the sun.[^8]

The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies artificial tanning devices as Group 1 carcinogens — the same category as tobacco and asbestos.[^8]

**Blistering sunburns:** A history of severe, blistering sunburns — particularly during childhood and adolescence — is an independent risk factor for melanoma, separate from cumulative sun exposure. The damage done during a childhood sunburn can influence melanoma risk decades later.[^3]

**Immunosuppression:** People who have received organ transplants and require immunosuppressive medications are at significantly elevated risk for skin cancers including melanoma. The same applies to individuals living with HIV/AIDS and those taking certain immunomodulating medications for autoimmune conditions.[^3]

**Geography and altitude:** Populations closer to the equator, at higher altitude, or with year-round UV-intense climates accumulate more UV exposure across a lifetime. Australia and New Zealand consistently show among the highest melanoma incidence rates globally.[^9]

## Catching It Early: The ABCDEs and What They Don't Tell You

### A Good Tool — With Limits

The ABCDE criteria are among the most widely taught and genuinely useful frameworks for melanoma self-examination.[^10] They were developed for good reason: most melanomas, particularly the superficial spreading type, display a recognizable cluster of visual features before they become deeply invasive.

**A — Asymmetry:** One half of the mole does not match the other. Benign moles are generally round or oval and symmetrical.

**B — Border:** Irregular, ragged, notched, or poorly defined edges. Benign moles tend to have smooth, clearly defined borders.

**C — Color:** Multiple colors within the same lesion — different shades of brown or black, red, white, or blue. A single uniform shade is more reassuring.

**D — Diameter:** Larger than 6 mm (roughly the size of a pencil eraser). Note, however, that approximately 30% of melanomas are smaller than this at diagnosis.[^10]

**E — Evolution:** Any change in size, shape, color, or character — or any new symptoms such as bleeding, itching, or crusting. Evolution may be the single most important criterion.

The ABCDE rule works well for superficial spreading melanoma. It is less effective for other subtypes. Nodular melanoma, in particular, can present as a small, symmetrical, uniformly pigmented or even colorless bump — passing all five ABCDE criteria while growing invasively downward.[^10][^11]

### The Ugly Duckling Sign

An important complement to ABCDE is the "ugly duckling" sign: the recognition that any mole which looks distinctly different from all the other moles on a person's body deserves attention, even if it does not technically meet ABCDE criteria.[^11] Most people's moles share a family resemblance. A lesion that stands apart from that pattern — darker, lighter, raised when others are flat, unusually shaped — is worth having evaluated.

### The Role of Clinical Examination

Dermoscopy — the use of a handheld magnifying device with polarized light — substantially improves diagnostic accuracy over visual inspection alone. A 2024 European guideline update noted that expert use of dermoscopy achieves sensitivity of approximately 89% and specificity of 79% for melanoma detection, compared to lower accuracy with naked-eye assessment.[^12] Total body photography (TBP) combined with dermoscopy is the standard of care for high-risk patients and enables comparison of lesions over time.

Self-examination has value, but it has limits. Melanomas found by patients themselves tend to be thicker and more advanced at diagnosis than those found during clinical examination.[^13] The back, scalp, soles of the feet, nail beds, and mucous membranes are areas that are difficult to self-examine. A thorough clinical skin examination by a dermatologist is not a substitute for self-monitoring — it is an addition to it.

> **Key Fact:** Roughly 30% of melanomas are under 6 mm at diagnosis, which means the diameter criterion of the classic ABCDE rule would miss them entirely. Evolution — any change over time — is often the most sensitive single indicator.[^10]

**Guidance on frequency and referral:** Monthly self-skin examinations are reasonable for most adults, particularly those with risk factors. Annual full-body skin examinations by a dermatologist are appropriate for anyone with a personal or family history of melanoma, multiple or dysplastic nevi, or other significant risk factors. Unusual lesions on the soles, palms, or beneath nails deserve prompt clinical evaluation regardless of ABCDE features.

A note on scope: the purpose of learning these criteria is to become an informed advocate — for yourself and for the people you care about. Detection tools are for prompting clinical evaluation, not for self-diagnosis or for assessing other people's lesions.

## Conventional Treatment: What the Evidence Strongly Supports

### Early-Stage Melanoma: Surgery Is the Standard

For melanomas detected at Stage I or II, surgical excision with appropriate margins remains the cornerstone of treatment and is, in most cases, curative.[^3] The goal is complete removal with a margin of normal-appearing skin, with the margin width determined by the Breslow thickness of the tumor.

For eligible patients with thicker tumors or clinically suspicious lymph nodes, sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) is performed to evaluate regional lymph node involvement. SLNB involves injection of a tracer near the tumor site to identify the "sentinel" lymph node — the first lymph node to which the melanoma would be likely to spread — and examining that node for cancer cells. The finding informs staging and guides decisions about further treatment.[^3]

Five-year survival for Stage I melanoma is consistently above 95%. Stage II survival varies by tumor thickness, ranging from approximately 80–90%.[^4]

### The Treatment Revolution: Immunotherapy

Before 2011, advanced melanoma carried a median overall survival of 8–10 months. Chemotherapy produced objective responses in fewer than 15% of patients, and those responses were typically short-lived.[^14]

That year, the FDA approved ipilimumab — a monoclonal antibody targeting CTLA-4, an immune checkpoint protein — becoming the first systemic treatment to improve overall survival in advanced melanoma.[^14][^15] It worked by releasing a "brake" on the immune system, allowing T cells to attack tumor cells more aggressively.

What followed was one of the most significant therapeutic transformations in modern oncology. In 2014, the FDA approved pembrolizumab and nivolumab, both targeting a different immune checkpoint protein, PD-1.[^15] The combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab — blocking two separate immune brakes simultaneously — showed even more powerful results. Data from the CheckMate-067 trial reported a median overall survival of 72.1 months with the combination, compared to 36.9 months with nivolumab alone and 8 months with ipilimumab alone.[^16]

> **Key Fact:** Before immunotherapy, the five-year survival rate for metastatic melanoma was approximately 10%.[^14] Today, immunotherapy-treated patients with advanced melanoma are living for years, and a meaningful percentage achieve durable responses that persist long after treatment ends.[^14][^15] This is genuinely remarkable.

Immune-related adverse events (irAEs) — including fatigue, rash, diarrhea, and inflammation of the lungs, liver, colon, or endocrine glands — affect a significant proportion of patients and require careful monitoring. These are not minor side effects; they can be serious, and they require oncology team management. Any decision about immunotherapy involves a detailed conversation about both potential benefit and risk.

### Targeted Therapy: BRAF and MEK Inhibitors

Approximately 50% of melanomas harbor a mutation in the BRAF gene, most commonly at position V600E.[^7] This mutation causes constitutive activation of a cell growth signaling pathway (MAPK), essentially keeping the accelerator pressed down permanently. BRAF inhibitors — vemurafenib, dabrafenib, and encorafenib — block this pathway directly.[^7]

These drugs can produce dramatic, rapid tumor responses in BRAF-mutated melanoma — sometimes within weeks. However, a critical challenge is acquired resistance: most patients develop resistance within months, even with combination therapy (adding MEK inhibitors such as trametinib or cobimetinib to block the pathway at a second point).[^7] Combination BRAF/MEK inhibitor therapy achieved median overall survival of approximately 25 months in pivotal trials, compared to 18 months with BRAF inhibition alone.[^7]

Genetic testing for BRAF mutation status is now standard of care for advanced melanoma before starting treatment. The testing result directly determines which treatment options are available.

Radiation is used selectively in melanoma — most often for brain metastases, regional palliation, or in specific situations after lymph node dissection — but is not a primary treatment modality.

These treatments require oncology and dermatology specialists. Melanoma staging, treatment selection, and monitoring involve complex clinical judgment. Lifestyle and supportive strategies discussed in the following section are complementary to, not replacements for, this care.

## Lifestyle and the Immune System: What Research Shows About Support During and After Treatment

The immune system is central to melanoma — both in the disease process and in the most effective treatments for it. It makes sense, then, that lifestyle factors influencing immune function have attracted serious research attention. The following sections present the honest evidence landscape for each area.

### Sun Protection: The Foundation

UV radiation drives the large majority of melanoma development.[^9] The evidence for UV protection as melanoma prevention is well-established and not contested.

**Sunscreen:** High-quality research — including a landmark Australian randomized controlled trial — has demonstrated that regular broad-spectrum sunscreen use reduces the risk of both melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers.[^17] The American Academy of Dermatology recommends SPF 30 or higher, broad-spectrum (UVA and UVB coverage), applied generously and reapplied every two hours when outdoors, and after swimming or sweating.[^17]

A nuance worth addressing: the debate between chemical (organic) and mineral (physical) sunscreens is not simply a matter of preference. The FDA has classified only zinc oxide and titanium dioxide as "generally recognized as safe and effective" based on current data.[^18] Several chemical filters remain under review pending additional safety data on systemic absorption.[^18] This does not mean chemical filters are unsafe — the data is incomplete, not alarming — but mineral sunscreens may be a reasonable preference, particularly for daily use or for those with concerns.

**UVA radiation and indoor exposure:** UVA rays make up approximately 90% of solar UV radiation, penetrate clouds and glass, and contribute meaningfully to skin aging and DNA damage even on overcast days or indoors.[^18] Car windows and office windows are not complete UV barriers.

> **Key Fact:** UVA rays penetrate glass. People who spend significant time driving or sitting near windows receive meaningful UVA exposure even without direct sun. Car-window UV film and office window coatings can reduce this meaningfully.[^18]

**Vitamin D and the sun avoidance tension:** This is a real and underappreciated clinical issue. Vitamin D synthesis requires UV exposure to the skin, and people who conscientiously protect themselves from the sun — or who live at northern latitudes, work indoors, or have darker skin (which requires more UV exposure to produce equivalent vitamin D) — frequently develop suboptimal vitamin D levels.[^9] The good news is that oral supplementation can maintain adequate vitamin D levels without UV exposure.[^9] For individuals avoiding sun for skin protection, discussing vitamin D status and supplementation with a healthcare provider is well worth doing.

Tanning beds for vitamin D: the argument does not hold up. The risk:benefit ratio is deeply unfavorable. Oral supplementation is a far safer option.[^8]

### Nutrition and Anti-Inflammatory Dietary Patterns

The research connecting diet to melanoma risk and outcomes is growing, and some of it is more compelling than the general "eat well" advice that often accompanies health education materials.

**Mediterranean diet and immunotherapy response:** A 2023 study published in JAMA Oncology followed 91 patients with advanced melanoma receiving immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in the UK and the Netherlands. Higher adherence to Mediterranean diet principles — rich in whole grains, fish, legumes, vegetables, nuts, and fruit — was associated with a significantly higher probability of treatment response and progression-free survival at 12 months.[^19] The authors proposed that the gut microbiome is a likely mediating mechanism: fiber and polyphenols shape the microbiome in ways that appear to influence immune system function and the effectiveness of immunotherapy.[^19] This is a single cohort study of modest size, and further research is needed — but the findings are consistent with a growing body of microbiome-immunotherapy literature.

**Polyphenols, carotenoids, and antioxidant foods:** Laboratory and epidemiological studies suggest that polyphenols (found in berries, olive oil, and green tea), carotenoids including lycopene (tomatoes, watermelon), and cruciferous vegetables with their sulforaphane content may have cancer-protective mechanisms through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant pathways.[^20] The evidence here is largely observational and preclinical — this is an area where food-based dietary patterns show more promise than supplementation, and where correlation does not guarantee causation. No single food prevents or treats melanoma.

**Alcohol:** Multiple lines of evidence now converge on alcohol consumption as an independent, modifiable risk factor for melanoma — and this is worth stating clearly rather than burying in caveats.[^21][^22]

A large NIH-AARP prospective cohort study involving nearly 470,000 participants found that higher total alcohol consumption was associated with elevated melanoma risk across beer, wine, and spirits.[^22] A Mendelian randomization analysis — a study design that approximates causal inference by using genetic variants as natural experiments — found a significant positive causal effect of alcohol intake on cutaneous melanoma risk (OR: 2.23).[^21] The mechanisms under investigation include alcohol-induced immunosuppression, increased UV sensitization of the skin, and pro-inflammatory effects.[^21]

**Processed foods and systemic inflammation:** Diets high in ultra-processed foods, refined carbohydrates, and sugar are associated with elevated markers of systemic inflammation.[^20] Chronic inflammation is a recognized contributor to cancer initiation and progression. The research connecting specific dietary inflammatory patterns to melanoma outcomes is at the emerging level — the mechanistic rationale is plausible, but direct evidence remains limited.

### Physical Activity

Research on exercise and cancer has matured substantially over the past decade, and melanoma is increasingly included.[^23]

A 2024 review in the *Journal of Cancer* synthesized preclinical and clinical evidence on physical activity and melanoma hallmarks, concluding that accumulating evidence supports a role for exercise in modulating tumor-promoting processes — including immune surveillance, angiogenesis, inflammation, and metabolic reprogramming.[^23] The mechanisms are multiple: exercise appears to increase natural killer (NK) cell activity, reduce systemic inflammation, and improve the capacity of the immune system to detect and destroy abnormal cells.

For people during and after melanoma treatment, the evidence is consistent across many cancer types: regular moderate exercise reduces treatment-related fatigue, improves mood and anxiety, supports healthy body composition, and reduces inflammatory markers.[^23] The SIO-ASCO guideline on cancer-related fatigue identifies exercise as one of the most robustly supported interventions.[^24]

What "regular moderate exercise" looks like in practice during treatment depends heavily on the individual, treatment type, and any side effects underway. A discussion with the treating oncology team is the appropriate starting point.

### Sleep and Circadian Health

Sleep is when the immune system conducts much of its maintenance work — clearing cellular debris, consolidating immunological memory, and producing anti-inflammatory cytokines. Chronic sleep deprivation consistently impairs immune function, increases inflammatory markers, and disrupts the hormonal environment in ways relevant to cancer biology.[^25]

**Melatonin and cancer:** Melatonin, the hormone produced by the pineal gland in response to darkness, is one of the most studied compounds in the intersection of sleep, immune function, and cancer.[^25][^26] Melatonin has documented immunomodulatory properties — influencing both innate and adaptive immune responses, modulating natural killer cell activity, and affecting the tumor microenvironment.[^26] Preclinical studies have shown interesting antitumor properties, and there is emerging evidence about melatonin's potential role as an adjunct in cancer contexts.[^26]

What's the honest summary? Laboratory and animal research on melatonin in cancer is intriguing. Human clinical trial data is more limited and less conclusive.[^25] Melatonin as a sleep-support intervention has strong safety evidence, and optimizing natural melatonin production — through consistent sleep timing, reduced nighttime light exposure, and screen limitation before bed — requires no supplementation and carries no risk. That is a reasonable and well-supported starting point.

Shift work, chronic late-night light exposure, and circadian disruption are independently associated with cancer risk — and immune dysfunction appears to be a central mediating pathway.[^25]

### Stress and Psychological Well-Being

**The psychoneuroimmunology connection:** Chronic psychological stress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathetic nervous system, producing sustained cortisol elevation and catecholamine release.[^27] These hormones, at chronically elevated levels, suppress natural killer cell activity, impair immune surveillance, and promote pro-inflammatory gene expression.[^27] The idea that psychological stress "affects cancer" is no longer speculative; the mechanisms are increasingly well-characterized.

**What interventions show:** A joint 2023 guideline from the Society for Integrative Oncology (SIO) and the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) formally recommends mindfulness-based interventions, yoga, and relaxation therapies for the management of anxiety and depression in adults with cancer.[^28] These recommendations are based on accumulated randomized controlled trial data. The 2024 SIO-ASCO guideline update on cancer-related fatigue similarly identifies mindfulness-based programs and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) as among the most supported approaches.[^24]

Depression and anxiety are extremely common in cancer patients — estimates of clinically significant distress during melanoma treatment are high — and both are often undertreated. Addressing psychological health is not supplementary to cancer care; it is part of it.[^27] Mental health support, including therapy, may improve not only quality of life but also treatment engagement and adherence.

Community, meaning, and connection — whether through family, faith, friendship, or purpose — appear in research literature as factors associated with psychological resilience and improved outcomes across multiple cancer types.[^27] These dimensions of care may arise naturally in any supportive context.

## Navigating the Gray Zone: Controversial and Contested Approaches

### High-Dose Antioxidants During Treatment

Many people undergoing cancer treatment take antioxidant supplements — vitamins C and E, beta-carotene, selenium, and others — with the reasonable hope of supporting their bodies through a difficult process. The research picture here is genuinely complicated, not something to dismiss or to minimize.

The core tension: certain cancer treatments — particularly radiation and some chemotherapy agents — work partly by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage cancer cell DNA.[^29] Antioxidants, by definition, scavenge these same reactive species. This raises a legitimate question: could high-dose antioxidant supplementation interfere with treatment efficacy?

The evidence is mixed. A secondary analysis of a large SWOG cooperative group breast cancer trial found that women who used antioxidant supplements — including vitamins A, C, and E, carotenoids, and coenzyme Q10 — both before and during chemotherapy had an increased risk of cancer recurrence and, to a lesser degree, increased mortality.[^29] Other studies have suggested antioxidants may reduce treatment toxicity and even enhance efficacy under some conditions.[^30] The field has not resolved this cleanly.

> **Key Fact:** Some antioxidant supplements — including high-dose vitamins C and E — may interfere with how certain cancer treatments work by protecting cancer cells from oxidative damage that treatment is designed to cause. The research is genuinely uncertain, not simply cautionary. This is a conversation to have with the oncology team before starting any supplementation during active treatment.[^29][^30]

The important distinction between food-sourced antioxidants and high-dose supplemental antioxidants is supported by the evidence: consuming antioxidant-rich foods during cancer treatment does not raise the same concerns as high-dose supplement use.[^30]

The correct guidance: this is a conversation worth having with the treating oncology team before starting supplementation. Not because the answer is necessarily "no," but because the decision should be made with full information and with the team's knowledge.

### Medicinal Mushrooms and Immune Support

Turkey tail (*Trametes versicolor*), reishi (*Ganoderma lucidum*), and shiitake (*Lentinula edodes*) have attracted scientific interest for their immunomodulatory properties. The research base is not uniform across species.

Turkey tail has the strongest evidence in cancer contexts. Its active constituents — polysaccharide K (PSK) and polysaccharide peptide (PSP) — have been used as adjunct treatments in Japan and China for over thirty years.[^31] Multiple randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses support improved survival with PSK used alongside conventional chemotherapy in gastric and colorectal cancers.[^31] An FDA-approved phase 1 clinical trial evaluated turkey tail extract in breast cancer patients, finding increased cancer-fighting immune cell activity following use.[^31]

The evidence in melanoma specifically is limited — most of the trials have been in gastrointestinal cancers. The immunomodulatory mechanisms are plausible, and the general safety profile is favorable. Drug interaction considerations and product quality (purity, contamination, standardization) are important practical concerns. This is an area where the evidence is more encouraging than "unproven" but falls short of "established" for melanoma specifically.[^31]

### Cannabis and Cannabinoids

The evidence on cannabis and cannabinoids in cancer care divides clearly into two categories: symptom management (where the evidence is more robust) and anti-tumor effects (where the evidence is preliminary at best).[^32]

For symptom management — pain, nausea, sleep disruption, anxiety — there is meaningful clinical trial data supporting the efficacy of cannabinoids in cancer patients generally. The 2024 ASCO guideline on cannabis and cannabinoids in adults with cancer acknowledges the evidence for symptom management while noting significant variability in individual response and important drug interaction considerations.[^32]

Claims about anti-tumor effects of cannabinoids, based primarily on laboratory and preclinical data, remain insufficiently tested in humans to support confidence. The potential interaction between cannabinoids and immunotherapy — both affecting immune cell function — is an area of active investigation with no clear guidance yet available.[^32]

Legal status, product variability, and the importance of disclosure to the treating oncology team are practical realities that affect how this subject can be navigated in most clinical and coaching contexts.

### Black Salve (Escharotics): A Clear Safety Concern

This is not a gray zone. It deserves to be said directly.

Black salve — also called compound X, bloodroot salve, or escharotic paste — is a corrosive topical preparation, typically containing bloodroot (*Sanguinaria canadensis*) and zinc chloride, that causes tissue necrosis and eschar formation.[^33] It is marketed online as an alternative treatment for skin cancer, including melanoma. It is banned in Australia and has received FDA warnings in the United States.[^33]

The medical literature documents cases of people with confirmed melanoma — including melanoma in situ — who applied black salve to their lesions and sustained extensive tissue destruction, severe scarring, secondary infections, and complications requiring hospitalization, while allowing the underlying cancer to persist or metastasize.[^33][^34] In documented melanoma cases in which patients used black salve as a primary treatment, outcomes included cancer metastasis.[^34]

Black salve is not an alternative with uncertain evidence. It is a treatment with credible evidence of direct harm and documented cases of indirect harm through delay of surgery. Anyone aware of a friend, family member, or community member considering this approach deserves straightforward information: this product can destroy normal tissue indiscriminately, cannot confirm that any cancer has been removed, and can dangerously delay the definitive evaluation and treatment that can actually cure early melanoma.[^33][^34]

## Monitoring, Follow-Up, and Life After Treatment

Finishing treatment is not the end of melanoma care; it is the beginning of a different kind of attention.

For most people treated for melanoma, follow-up surveillance involves periodic dermatologic skin examinations and, for higher-stage disease, imaging and laboratory evaluation at regular intervals. The frequency and scope depend on the original stage and treatment. The specific schedule should be established and maintained with the treating dermatologist and oncologist.[^3]

People who have had one melanoma have a meaningfully elevated risk of developing a second primary melanoma during their lifetime — separate from recurrence of the original tumor.[^3] This is not a reason for fear; it is a reason for consistent, lifelong skin surveillance and sun protection.

**Sun protection for life:** This cannot be overstated. Ongoing UV protection is among the most important behaviors for both prevention of second primaries and long-term skin health.

**The emotional reality of survivorship:** "Scanxiety" — the anxiety that builds before and during surveillance imaging — is a documented, real, and common experience for cancer survivors. So are fear of recurrence, difficulty re-engaging with normal life, and lingering psychological effects of treatment.[^24] These experiences deserve acknowledgment and, when significant, professional support. Mindfulness-based interventions and CBT have a growing evidence base for survivorship anxiety and depression.[^28]

## When to See a Doctor: Referral Triggers

Prompt medical evaluation — within days, not weeks — is appropriate when:

Any mole or spot has changed in size, shape, or color, even subtly, over weeks to months.

A new growth has appeared that bleeds without injury, does not heal, or looks unusual.

A lesion on the skin meets any of the ABCDE criteria.

A suspicious lesion appears on the soles, palms, under a nail, or in any area not normally associated with sun exposure — especially in anyone with darker skin.

A lesion on any person with a personal or family history of melanoma warrants prompt evaluation; the threshold for concern is appropriately lower.

A lesion that simply "feels wrong" to the person living with it deserves evaluation. Patient intuition is a legitimate clinical data point.

The most important principle here is this: early melanoma, caught before it invades deeply into the skin, is nearly always curable with surgery alone. That window closes as disease advances. Early evaluation costs almost nothing compared to what late detection can cost.

## Closing

The knowledge itself is part of the protection. Knowing what to look for on your own skin, knowing to check places that are easy to miss — the soles of feet, beneath nails, the scalp — knowing to take a suspicious spot seriously rather than watching it for months; these are not trivial acts. They are how thin, early, curable melanomas get found.

The lifestyle research reviewed in this document adds further dimension. It suggests — with varying degrees of confidence — that what we eat, how we move, how well we sleep, and how we manage stress all have real bearing on immune function and potentially on how the body responds to treatment. None of it substitutes for surgical care or oncology; all of it complements it. The immune system that checkpoint inhibitors are working with — or that the body is relying on to keep a tumor from coming back — is the same immune system that sleep and movement and nourishment are supporting.

And for those who have been through a melanoma diagnosis: the treatment landscape looks dramatically different than it did even fifteen years ago. The decline in mortality rates over the past decade reflects real advances in early detection, real advances in immunotherapy, and a better collective understanding of this disease.[^4][^9] There is real reason for hope — including for people who once had very little to draw on.
