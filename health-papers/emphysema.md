## When the Stairs Start to Feel Like a Mountain

Maybe you've noticed it slowly. The walk from the parking lot takes a little longer. You pause halfway up the stairs and pretend to check your phone. You stopped gardening, or dancing at family weddings, or carrying the grandkids, not because you decided to, but because it just got too hard to breathe. If that sounds familiar, you are not alone, and you are not "just getting old." Many of these changes are signs of emphysema, one of the most common long-term lung conditions in the world.[^1][^2]

Here is the honest truth, and the hopeful truth, side by side. The lung damage in emphysema cannot be undone with any treatment available today. But a great deal can be done. Quitting smoking slows further decline. Pulmonary rehabilitation, daily movement, breathing skills, good nutrition, cleaner air, vaccines, emotional support and the right medical treatment can all reduce breathlessness, prevent flare-ups, and help people live fuller lives.[^1][^6] Many people with emphysema learn to breathe easier and do more than they thought possible. This handout is meant to help you, and the people who care about you, understand what helps, how strong the evidence is, and when to get medical help. It is not a replacement for your own clinician's advice.

## What Emphysema Is

### A closer look inside your lungs

Emphysema is one form of COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, a group of lung conditions that make it hard to move air out of the lungs). The current international guidance describes COPD as a lung condition caused by problems in the airways and/or the alveoli (the tiny air sacs where oxygen enters your blood) that cause persistent, often worsening airflow blockage.[^1]

Picture healthy alveoli as bunches of tiny grapes at the end of each airway branch. Their thin, stretchy walls give your lungs an enormous surface for oxygen to pass into your blood. In emphysema, those walls break down. The grapes merge into fewer, larger, floppy balloons. That means less surface for oxygen to cross, and less of the natural "spring" that helps push air back out.[^1]

### Why breathing out is the hard part

Healthy lungs work like a stretched rubber band: when you relax, they snap back and push air out. Emphysema weakens that spring, and the small airways tend to collapse as you exhale. Air gets trapped inside. Doctors call this air trapping and hyperinflation (over-filling of the lungs). Imagine trying to blow up a balloon that is already half full — there's little room left for a fresh breath. That is why people with emphysema often feel they can't get a satisfying breath in, when the real problem is getting air out.[^1]

### Emphysema, chronic bronchitis and asthma

Emphysema often overlaps with chronic bronchitis (long-term inflammation of the airways with ongoing cough and mucus). Many people have features of both. Some people also have asthma, or features of asthma and COPD together, which can affect which medicines work best.[^1]

### How common is it?

COPD affects more than 380 million people worldwide.[^3] The World Health Organization (WHO) lists COPD as the fourth leading cause of death worldwide, causing about 3.5 million deaths in 2021 — roughly 5% of all deaths.[^2] (The 2026 GOLD report describes COPD as "one of the top three causes of death worldwide"; the difference reflects different data sources and years.)[^1] Nearly 90% of COPD deaths in people under 70 occur in low- and middle-income countries.[^2] In the United States, more than 15 million adults (6.4%) reported having been diagnosed with COPD in 2021, according to the CDC, and many more do not know they have it.[^40]

The damage is permanent. But the *course* of the disease — how fast it progresses and how much it limits your life — can be influenced in real ways.[^1]

## Causes and Risk Factors

### Tobacco smoke

Tobacco smoking is the dominant cause. WHO estimates smoking accounts for over 70% of COPD cases in high-income countries.[^2] Secondhand smoke also harms the lungs, which is one reason a smoke-free home matters for everyone living there.

### Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD)

Alpha-1 antitrypsin is a protein made in the liver that protects the lungs from being damaged by the body's own enzymes. Some people inherit genes that produce too little of it. This condition, AATD, can cause emphysema at a younger age, sometimes even in people who never smoked, and it is widely underdiagnosed — fewer than 10% of the estimated 100,000 Americans with AATD have been diagnosed, often after long delays.[^28][^42]

The 2026 GOLD report recommends testing all patients diagnosed with COPD for AATD.[^1] A European Respiratory Society statement and a U.S. Alpha-1 Foundation guideline also support testing everyone with COPD, regardless of age or ethnicity.[^28][^29] (A 2025 Canadian guideline prefers more targeted testing, so recommendations are not fully unanimous.) If you test positive, your blood relatives may want testing too, and you may be a candidate for augmentation therapy (infusions of the missing protein), which is aimed at slowing lung damage in selected people.[^28]

> **Key Fact:** Current GOLD guidance recommends that everyone diagnosed with COPD be tested for alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency — a simple blood test that can change care for you and your family.

### Causes beyond cigarettes

In low- and middle-income countries, tobacco explains only 30–40% of COPD cases, and household air pollution — smoke from burning wood, dung, charcoal or crop waste for cooking and heating — is a major risk factor.[^2] Other contributors include workplace dusts and fumes, outdoor air pollution and wildfire smoke, frequent childhood lung infections, poor lung growth before birth or in early childhood, and aging.[^1] GOLD now describes COPD as the result of gene–environment interactions over a whole lifetime.[^1]

### Still being studied

- **Vaping (e-cigarettes):** Vaping exposes the lungs to heated aerosol. Whether it causes emphysema over decades is not yet known, because the products are relatively new. The evidence is **insufficient** to judge long-term lung effects.[^25]
- **Cannabis smoking:** Studies on whether smoking cannabis causes emphysema or large air blisters (bullae) have produced **conflicting** results. Smoke of any kind irritates airways.
- **COPD in never-smokers:** This is common worldwide, driven largely by air pollution, work exposures and early-life factors.[^1][^2]

### What is happening inside

Three processes work together. Oxidative stress is damage from unstable molecules in smoke and pollution — a bit like rust. Protease–antiprotease imbalance means the body's "demolition enzymes" outpace its protective proteins such as alpha-1 antitrypsin. And chronic inflammation keeps the damage going. Understanding these helps explain why removing smoke and pollution matters so much.[^1]

## Recognizing Symptoms and Getting Diagnosed

### The signs

Common symptoms include shortness of breath with activity, a chronic cough (with or without mucus), wheezing, chest tightness and tiredness.[^2] In advanced disease, some people lose weight without trying and develop a "barrel chest" (a rounded chest shape from over-filled lungs).[^1]

### The slow-creep problem

Emphysema usually develops over years. As breathing gets harder, people naturally do less. Doing less weakens muscles, and weaker muscles need more oxygen for the same task — so breathing feels even harder. This downward spiral of deconditioning can make the disease seem worse than the lungs alone would explain. The good news is that this part is often reversible with training.[^6]

### How it's diagnosed

Spirometry is a simple breathing test: you take a deep breath and blow out as hard and fast as you can into a tube. It measures how much air you can blow out in the first second compared with your total breath. GOLD requires spirometry after a bronchodilator (airway-opening medicine) showing a ratio below 0.7 to confirm COPD.[^1] A CT scan (a detailed X-ray picture) can show emphysema directly, and oxygen tests — a fingertip pulse oximeter or a blood test — show how well oxygen is getting into your blood.[^1] COPD is widely underdiagnosed, and GOLD 2026 now emphasizes active case-finding in people with risk factors or unexplained breathlessness, cough or mucus.[^1]

### Flare-ups (exacerbations)

A flare-up, or exacerbation, is a period when symptoms get noticeably worse than usual — more breathlessness, more mucus, or a change in mucus color — often triggered by an infection or air pollution. Each flare-up can speed lung decline and reduce quality of life, and GOLD 2026 notes that even a single moderate flare-up raises the risk of more.[^1][^14] That is why preventing flare-ups is a central goal of care.

## Conventional Medical Care

This section is informational. Your clinician will tailor any treatment to you.

### Help to quit smoking

Counseling plus medication works better than willpower alone. Options include nicotine replacement (patches, gum, lozenges), varenicline and bupropion; cytisine is an emerging, lower-cost option in some countries.[^1] Many people worry about mental-health side effects. The large EAGLES trial of more than 8,000 smokers found that varenicline and bupropion did not significantly increase serious neuropsychiatric side effects compared with nicotine patch or placebo, and varenicline helped the most people quit.[^26] Evidence level: **well-established**.

### Inhaled bronchodilators

Bronchodilators relax the muscles around the airways. Long-acting versions — LAMAs (long-acting muscarinic antagonists) and LABAs (long-acting beta-agonists), often combined in one inhaler — are the foundation of daily treatment for most people.[^1] Evidence level: **well-established**.

But medicine only helps if it reaches your lungs. A review of 144 studies involving more than 54,000 people found that only 31% used their inhaler correctly, and there had been no improvement over 40 years.[^30] Ask for a technique check at every visit.

> **Key Fact:** In a large review of 144 studies, only about 3 in 10 people used their inhaler correctly — a quick technique check can make your medicine work better.

### Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)

Inhaled steroids calm airway inflammation. They help most in people with frequent flare-ups and higher levels of eosinophils (a type of white blood cell linked to allergic-type inflammation) in the blood. GOLD suggests considering them when blood eosinophils are 300 cells/µL or higher in people with flare-ups.[^1] The trade-off: inhaled steroids raise pneumonia risk in COPD, so they are not for everyone.[^1] Evidence level: **well-established for selected people**.

### Newer therapies

- **Ensifentrine (Ohtuvayre):** A nebulized medicine that both opens airways and calms inflammation, FDA-approved June 26, 2024.[^13] In the two ENHANCE trials it modestly improved lung function (about 90 mL) and reduced flare-ups over 24 weeks, though symptom and quality-of-life improvements were significant in only one of the two trials.[^13] It launched at a list price of $2,950 per month.[^32]
- **Dupilumab (Dupixent):** An injected biologic (a targeted antibody medicine) approved September 27, 2024 for adults with uncontrolled COPD and an eosinophilic phenotype.[^31] In the BOREAS and NOTUS trials, people with blood eosinophils of at least 300 who were already on maximum inhaled therapy had about 30–34% fewer moderate or severe flare-ups.[^10][^11]
- **Mepolizumab (Nucala):** Approved May 22, 2025 as add-on treatment for eosinophilic COPD.[^33] In the MATINEE trial, it reduced moderate or severe flare-ups by about 21% compared with placebo.[^12]

A fair note: these trials were funded by the manufacturers, enrolled carefully selected patients, and showed real but modest benefits at high cost. That doesn't make them less valuable; it means they fit a specific group of people. Evidence level: **well-established for selected patients**, with long-term data still accumulating.

### Other options

Roflumilast (an anti-inflammatory pill) and long-term azithromycin (an antibiotic taken regularly) can reduce flare-ups in selected people, with trade-offs such as stomach upset, weight loss, hearing effects or antibiotic resistance.[^1]

### Oxygen therapy

For people whose oxygen is severely low at rest, long-term oxygen helps people live longer — one of the few COPD treatments shown to improve survival.[^1] But more is not always better. The Long-Term Oxygen Treatment Trial followed 738 people with only moderately low oxygen for one to six years and found no benefit for survival, hospital stays, quality of life or walking distance.[^9] Oxygen should be prescribed based on testing.

### Procedures

For carefully selected people with severe emphysema, reducing the size of the most damaged, over-inflated parts of the lung can let healthier parts work better. The National Emphysema Treatment Trial (1,218 patients) found lung volume reduction surgery did not improve survival overall but improved exercise capacity, with the greatest benefit in specific subgroups.[^7] Endobronchial valves — tiny one-way valves placed through a scope — were studied in the LIBERATE trial, which showed meaningful gains in lung function, exercise, breathlessness and quality of life at 12 months in patients without "collateral ventilation" (air leaking between lung lobes).[^8] Collapsed lung was a known risk. Lung transplant is an option for a small number of people.[^1]

### Vaccines

Infections are a leading trigger of flare-ups. GOLD recommends influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19, RSV and Tdap (tetanus, diphtheria, whooping cough) vaccination for people with COPD.[^1] The CDC recommends a single dose of RSV vaccine for all adults 75 and older and for adults 50–74 at increased risk — including those with COPD or emphysema; it is not a yearly shot.[^34] If you have doubts about vaccines, that's understandable. Bring your questions to a clinician or pharmacist you trust; they can explain what is known about benefits and side effects for your situation.

### Pulmonary rehabilitation

If there is one treatment to ask about, this may be it. Pulmonary rehabilitation (a supervised program of exercise training, education and support, usually 6–12 weeks) is one of the best-proven treatments in all of COPD care. A Cochrane review of 65 trials with 3,822 participants found it improves breathlessness, fatigue, sense of control, and exercise ability, and the authors concluded that more trials comparing it with usual care are not warranted — the question has been answered.[^6] The American Thoracic Society's 2023 guideline recommends it for adults with COPD.[^27]

Yet pulmonary rehab is badly underused: in a study of 223,832 Medicare beneficiaries hospitalized for COPD, only 1.9% started rehab within six months and just 2.7% within a year, and those living more than 10 miles from a program were less likely to take part.[^41] Barriers include lack of referral, travel distance, cost, and fear of exertion. Ask your clinician directly: "Am I a candidate for pulmonary rehab? Is there a home-based or virtual option?" Evidence level: **well-established**.

> **Key Fact:** Pulmonary rehabilitation improves breathlessness, fatigue and quality of life in people with COPD, and its benefits are so consistently proven that researchers declared more comparison trials unnecessary.

### Palliative and supportive care

Palliative care is extra support focused on comfort and quality of life. It is not only for the end of life and can work alongside other treatments.[^1] For breathlessness that persists despite everything else, low-dose opioids have been used, but the evidence is **mixed**: the BEAMS trial of 156 people found that low-dose extended-release morphine did not significantly improve worst breathlessness after one week.[^16] Advance care planning — talking with loved ones and clinicians about your values and wishes — helps everyone later. Hospice is available when the focus shifts fully to comfort.

## Lifestyle and Natural Approaches

This is where you have the most daily influence. Each approach is described with its evidence level.

### 1. Quitting smoking and avoiding smoke

The landmark Lung Health Study enrolled 5,887 smokers with early airflow blockage. Those in the quit-smoking program — especially those who stayed quit — had a slower decline in lung function.[^4] After 14.5 years, the intervention group had lower death rates, even though only a minority quit for good.[^5] Evidence level: **well-established**.

Most people who quit successfully try several times. A slip is not failure; it is information for the next attempt. Every smoke-free day counts.

> **Key Fact:** Quitting smoking is the single most powerful way to slow lung-function decline in COPD — and the Lung Health Study showed a quit-smoking program lowered death rates over 14.5 years.

### 2. Movement and exercise

Walking, light strength training (for example, sit-to-stand from a chair or lifting light weights) and interval training (short bursts of effort with rests) can all build endurance. Feeling somewhat breathless during exercise is expected and, within limits your care team sets, safe; the muscles get stronger and need less oxygen over time.[^6] If fear of breathlessness is holding you back, a supervised program is the best place to learn your safe limits. Evidence level: **well-established**.

### 3. Breathing techniques

**Pursed-lip breathing** helps slow your breath and let trapped air out:

1. Relax your neck and shoulders.
2. Breathe in gently through your nose for about two counts.
3. Pucker your lips as if blowing out a candle.
4. Breathe out slowly through pursed lips for about four counts — twice as long as the in-breath.
5. Repeat until you feel calmer.

Pursed-lip breathing is widely taught in pulmonary rehab.[^1] **Diaphragmatic (belly) breathing** has **mixed** evidence; some people with severe hyperinflation actually feel more breathless doing it, so stop if it makes you worse. **Tripod positioning** — leaning forward with hands on your knees or a table — helps the breathing muscles work better. A **handheld fan** blowing cool air on your face can ease the sensation of breathlessness; GOLD recognizes it as a simple symptom-relief measure, with **emerging** evidence.[^1]

### 4. Mind-body and gentle movement

**Tai chi** is a slow, flowing martial art. In a trial of 120 people with COPD, 12 weeks of group tai chi was found equivalent to standard pulmonary rehab for quality of life.[^23] Other experts argued it should not replace full rehab, so the evidence is best described as **promising**, especially where rehab isn't available. **Yoga and pranayama** (yogic breathing) are **promising but limited**. **Singing:** a Cochrane review of three small studies (112 people) found low to very low quality evidence that singing may improve the physical part of quality of life, with no harm.[^24] Evidence level: **limited or preliminary**, though many people report enjoyment and connection.

### 5. Inspiratory muscle training

Inspiratory muscle training uses a handheld device that makes breathing in harder, to strengthen breathing muscles. Evidence is **mixed**: it may help selected people with weak breathing muscles, especially alongside rehab, but it is not routinely recommended for everyone.[^1]

### 6. Nutrition

**Weight and muscle matter.** Being underweight and losing muscle predicts worse outcomes in COPD. Breathing hard burns extra energy, so some people need more calories and protein, not fewer.[^1] Small, frequent meals can help if a full stomach makes breathing harder. Ask about a nutrition assessment.

**Food patterns.** In a Swedish study of 44,335 men followed for 13 years, current smokers who ate the most fruit and vegetables (5.3 or more servings a day) had a 40% lower risk of developing COPD than those eating fewer than 2 servings.[^17] In 34,739 Swedish women, those eating the most fruit over the long term had a 37% lower risk.[^18] Higher fiber intake, especially from cereal grains, was also linked to lower COPD risk in men.[^19] These are **observational** studies — they show associations, not proof of cause — and they studied preventing COPD, not treating it. Still, a plant-forward, Mediterranean-style pattern rich in fruit, vegetables, whole grains and fiber is a reasonable choice. Evidence level: **promising (observational)**.

**Plant-based and vegetarian eating** can fit well, but if you're underweight, make sure you're getting enough calories and protein (beans, lentils, tofu, nuts, dairy or eggs if you eat them).

**High-fat, low-carbohydrate diets** were studied because burning carbohydrate produces more carbon dioxide. Short-term studies showed small effects of limited practical importance. Evidence level: **limited**.

**Processed and cured meats** have been linked in observational studies to higher COPD risk, possibly through nitrites. Evidence level: **limited (observational)**.

**Hydration:** Drinking enough fluids may help keep mucus easier to clear, though formal studies are few. If you have heart or kidney conditions, ask about fluid limits.

### 7. Supplements and nutrients

Supplements are not regulated like medicines, and quality varies. Always tell your clinician and pharmacist what you take.

- **Vitamin D:** A pooled analysis of individual patient data from four trials (560 people) found vitamin D reduced moderate or severe flare-ups in people who were vitamin D deficient, but not in those with normal levels.[^14] Evidence level: **promising, for deficient people only**. Ask about a blood test before supplementing.
- **N-acetylcysteine (NAC):** In the PANTHEON trial of 1,006 people in China, high-dose NAC reduced flare-ups by about 22% over a year.[^15] But the earlier European BRONCUS trial, using a lower dose, found no slowing of lung decline and no reduction in flare-ups overall.[^20] Evidence level: **conflicting and population-dependent**. The positive trial used doses well above typical supplement amounts; discuss with your clinician.
- **Omega-3s, other antioxidants, magnesium and CoQ10:** Evidence is **limited or preliminary**.

> **Key Fact:** Vitamin D reduced COPD flare-ups mainly in people who were deficient to begin with — a blood test can tell you whether you are likely to benefit.

**A critical caution about beta-carotene.** Two large trials found that high-dose beta-carotene *increased* lung cancer in people at risk. In the ATBC study of 29,133 male smokers, lung cancer rose by 18%.[^21] In the CARET trial of 18,314 smokers, former smokers and asbestos-exposed workers, beta-carotene plus vitamin A raised lung cancer risk by 28% and increased deaths.[^22] If you smoke now or smoked in the past, **do not take high-dose beta-carotene or vitamin A supplements.** Evidence level: **not recommended**.

> **Key Fact:** High-dose beta-carotene raised lung cancer risk by 18% to 28% in smokers in two major trials — current and former smokers should avoid these supplements.

### 8. Air quality at home and outdoors

In the CLEAN AIR trial, former smokers with moderate-to-severe COPD who received portable HEPA air cleaners (high-efficiency particulate air filters) had fewer moderate flare-ups and, among those who used them consistently, improvements in symptoms.[^39] A 2026 follow-up trial also suggested benefits in people with COPD who still smoke.[^35] Evidence level: **promising**. Other practical steps: vent cooking fumes, avoid wood stoves and candles where possible, check the daily air-quality index, stay indoors with filtered air on bad air days, have a wildfire smoke plan, and reduce mold and dust.

### 9. Sleep

Some people with COPD also have sleep apnea (pauses in breathing during sleep), sometimes called "overlap syndrome," and low oxygen at night.[^1] Loud snoring, gasping, morning headaches or daytime sleepiness are worth mentioning. Sleeping pills and sedatives can slow breathing and should only be used with clinician oversight.

### 10. Emotional health

Living with breathlessness is frightening. A review of controlled studies found depression in about 27% of people with COPD, compared with 10% of people without it.[^36] Anxiety is also common, and both are often unrecognized and undertreated, which worsens daily functioning.[^37] Breathlessness can trigger panic, and panic worsens breathlessness — a cycle that breathing skills, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT, a practical talk therapy that helps change unhelpful thought patterns) and support groups can help interrupt.[^1] Caregivers carry real strain too, and deserve support and rest.

> **Key Fact:** About 1 in 4 people with COPD live with depression — nearly three times the rate in people without it — and treating anxiety and depression is part of good COPD care.

### 11. Traditional and naturopathic comfort practices

Some traditions use hydrotherapy (water-based treatments) such as warm fomentations (hot moist compresses) on the chest, contrast showers, and steam inhalation, along with sunlight, fresh air, rest, and peace of mind. These may plausibly offer comfort, relaxation and possibly help loosen mucus, but they have been **little studied** in COPD — evidence is **insufficient**, which is different from having been proven ineffective. Cautions matter: steam and hot compresses can burn, very hot or humid air can worsen breathlessness in severe disease, and heat stress is a real risk. Keep temperatures moderate and have someone nearby.

### 12. Spiritual support (optional and for everyone)

For many people, hope, purpose, gratitude, prayer, meditation and belonging to a caring community are deep sources of resilience. You do not need any particular belief to benefit from connection and meaning. Research on spiritual coping specifically in lung disease is still limited, but emotional and social support are recognized parts of comprehensive COPD care.[^1] Whatever sustains you — faith, nature, music, family — is worth making room for.

## Contested and Controversial Topics

### "Reversing emphysema"

Some products and programs promise to reverse emphysema. With current methods, destroyed alveolar walls do not regenerate.[^1] What *can* genuinely improve is how you feel and function: stronger muscles, better breathing technique, fewer flare-ups and less air trapping can make a real difference. Be wary of anyone who promises regrowth of lung tissue.

### Stem cell clinics

Legitimate research on stem cells for lung disease is at an early stage and happens in regulated clinical trials. Separately, commercial clinics sell unapproved stem cell treatments, often for thousands of dollars. The FDA warns that such products can be illegal and potentially harmful, and it has documented serious injuries, including blindness and tumors.[^38] **Do not pay for stem cell treatment for emphysema outside a registered clinical trial.** Evidence level: **not recommended**.

### Nebulized hydrogen peroxide, nebulized glutathione, ozone therapy and colloidal silver

Inhaling hydrogen peroxide or ozone can irritate and injure lung tissue; nebulized glutathione has been reported to trigger bronchospasm (sudden airway tightening) in some people; and colloidal silver has no proven benefit and can cause permanent skin discoloration. **These are not recommended** and carry real risk of harm.

### Essential oils and diffusers

Scented oils released into the air can irritate sensitive airways and trigger coughing or wheezing. If you use them, avoid diffusing them in shared rooms and stop if symptoms worsen.

### Salt rooms (halotherapy) and Buteyko breathing

Halotherapy means sitting in a room with fine salt particles in the air; Buteyko is a breathing method focused on slower, shallower breathing. People report feeling relaxed, but evidence in COPD is **limited**. Salt aerosol can irritate some airways. Neither should replace prescribed treatment.

### E-cigarettes as a quitting tool

This is a genuine debate. The latest Cochrane living review (evidence to January 2026) found high-certainty evidence that nicotine e-cigarettes help more people quit smoking than nicotine replacement therapy.[^25] On the other hand, long-term lung effects are unknown, and health organizations disagree about how to advise smokers. A fair summary: for someone who has been unable to quit with other methods, switching completely to a regulated e-cigarette is likely less harmful than continuing to smoke, but the goal is ultimately to be free of both.[^25] Using both together does not bring the same benefit.

### Fear of oxygen

Some people worry oxygen is "addictive" or will weaken their lungs. It is neither. Oxygen is a medicine prescribed at a set flow for a specific need.[^1] A small number of people with advanced COPD retain carbon dioxide, so too much oxygen can be harmful for them — which is exactly why flow settings should come from testing, not guesswork.

### Lung "detox" and cleansing programs

Many cleansing programs for the lungs combine teas, herbal blends, fasting or special breathing routines. Specific herbal products have not been tested in COPD trials, so evidence is **insufficient**. Parts of these programs — drinking fluids, breathing exercises, avoiding smoke — overlap with helpful habits. The cautions: fasting or very restrictive programs can cause harmful weight and muscle loss in people with emphysema, and some herbs interact with medicines. The most powerful way to reduce the lungs' exposure to harmful substances is to stop inhaling them — smoke, fumes and polluted air.[^1][^39]

### Cannabis and CBD

Some people use cannabis or CBD for anxiety or breathlessness. Evidence is **limited**. Smoking or vaping cannabis irritates the airways, and cannabis products can interact with other medicines. Talk with your clinician before trying them.

### The inhaled steroid debate

As noted earlier, inhaled steroids help people with frequent flare-ups and higher eosinophil counts but raise pneumonia risk.[^1] The debate is really about who benefits most, which a blood test and flare-up history help answer.

## Cautions and Safety

- **Oxygen and fire:** Oxygen makes fires burn hotter and faster. Never smoke, use candles, gas stoves or open flames near oxygen, and keep equipment away from heat sources. A National Fire Protection Association report found home medical oxygen was involved in an average of 1,190 burns treated in U.S. emergency rooms each year, and about 46 people per year died in home fires involving medical oxygen — with smoking by far the leading factor.[^43]
- **Sedatives:** Benzodiazepines, opioids, sleeping pills and alcohol can slow breathing; use them only with clinician oversight.[^16]
- **Supplement interactions:** Theophylline (an older COPD medicine) can be affected by St. John's wort and by large amounts of caffeine. Ephedra (ma huang) is dangerous for the heart. Licorice can worsen potassium loss and fluid retention with steroids. Some supplements increase bleeding risk with blood thinners.
- **Beta-carotene and vitamin A:** Avoid high doses if you are a current or former smoker.[^21][^22]
- **Restrictive diets:** Fasting, aggressive weight loss, or restrictive cleanses can be harmful if you are underweight.[^1]
- **Exercise safety:** Use oxygen during exercise if prescribed, avoid extreme heat and humidity, and stop and rest if you have chest pain, dizziness, or breathlessness far beyond your usual.
- **Travel and altitude:** Air travel and high altitude lower oxygen levels. Ask about an oxygen assessment before flying. People with large bullae (air blisters in the lung) may face a higher risk of collapsed lung and should get specific advice.[^1]

## When to Seek Medical Help

**EMERGENCY — call 911:**

- Severe breathlessness at rest
- Unable to speak in full sentences
- Blue or gray lips or fingertips
- Confusion or unusual drowsiness (possible carbon dioxide buildup)
- Chest pain
- Sudden sharp chest pain with breathlessness (possible collapsed lung)
- Oxygen level below the target your clinician set, and not improving

**CONTACT A CLINICIAN PROMPTLY (same or next day):**

- Flare-up signs: more breathlessness than usual, more mucus, or a change in mucus color
- Fever
- Coughing up blood
- New swelling in legs or ankles
- Unintended weight loss
- Worse sleep or morning headaches
- Depression, hopelessness, or thoughts of self-harm — call or text **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) anytime

**ROUTINE FOLLOW-UP — raise at your next visit:**

- You have never had spirometry
- You have COPD but have never been tested for alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency[^1]
- You have not been offered pulmonary rehabilitation[^6]

## Supporting Someone with Emphysema

Family members, friends and wellness supporters can make a real difference. Walk alongside the person — literally, on short daily walks, at their pace. Support quitting without shaming; encouragement works better than lectures, and relapse is part of the process for most people.[^5] Help keep a list of questions for appointments, and notice warning signs such as confusion, blue lips, or rising breathlessness. Make the home smoke-free and reduce irritants such as strong scents and wood smoke. Offer practical help — rides, meals, errands — and simply listen. Caring for someone with a chronic illness is demanding, so supporters need rest and support too.[^37]

### Questions to Ask Your Doctor

- Have I been tested for alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency?
- Am I a candidate for pulmonary rehabilitation, and is there a home or virtual option?
- Can you check my inhaler technique today?
- Do I need an oxygen assessment, at rest, with exercise, or for travel?
- Which vaccines do I need, including RSV?
- Can we write a flare-up action plan together?
- Should my blood eosinophils or vitamin D level be checked?

## Small Steps You Can Take This Week

- Take a 10-minute walk, at a comfortable pace, on most days.
- Practice pursed-lip breathing for five minutes, twice a day.
- Check the local air-quality index each morning.
- If you smoke, set a quit date and call a quitline (1-800-QUIT-NOW in the U.S.).
- Ask your clinician about pulmonary rehab and an inhaler technique check.
- Add one extra serving of fruit or vegetables to your day.
- Call a friend, join a support group, or spend time in a place that brings you peace.

Emphysema changes your lungs, but it doesn't have to define your life. The damage is real, and so is the progress people make every day — walking farther, having fewer flare-ups, sleeping better, and returning to activities they thought were gone. Start with one small step. Then take another. You don't have to do this alone, and it's never too late for your next breath to be a little easier.
