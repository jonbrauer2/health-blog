## Introduction

Here is a statistic that tends to surprise people: roughly **one in four adults worldwide** reports having solar lentigines — what most of us call age spots, liver spots, or sun spots.[^1] That is not a rare condition. It is one of the most common skin changes on the planet, and yet most people know remarkably little about what is actually happening beneath those small, flat, brownish patches.

The names themselves are a source of confusion. Age spots, liver spots, solar lentigines, sun spots, senile freckles — these are all different labels for the same thing, and nearly all of them tell only part of the story. "Age spots" implies that time is the main culprit, which is not quite right. "Liver spots" suggests a connection to liver disease, which is not true at all — the name likely arose from their color, not their cause. "Sun spots" and "solar lentigines" get closest to the truth: these marks are primarily the visible record of a lifetime's worth of ultraviolet (UV) exposure, written on the skin in concentrated pigment.[^2]

They most often appear after age 40, though anyone with significant cumulative sun exposure — regardless of age — can develop them. They tend to show up on the face, hands, forearms, and shoulders: exactly the places that have spent the most time in the light.[^3]

For most people, age spots are cosmetically bothersome but medically harmless. But that is not always true, and that distinction matters enormously. There are conditions that look very much like an age spot — including a slow-growing form of melanoma called lentigo maligna — that require prompt medical attention. Knowing the difference is not optional; it is a genuine health skill.

This guide walks through what age spots are, who gets them, what the research shows about treating them, what can be done nutritionally and naturally, and — most importantly — when to call a dermatologist.

> **Key Fact:** In a 2024 international survey of 48,000 adults across 34 countries, 27% reported having solar lentigines, with a mean age of presentation around 48 years. Women comprised 58% of those affected.[^1]

## What Are Age Spots?

An age spot is not a bruise, a mole, or a growth. It is a flat, well-defined patch of concentrated pigment — typically brown, dark tan, or occasionally near-black — caused by an accumulation of melanin in the outer layers of the skin.[^4]

Melanin is the pigment that gives skin, hair, and eyes their color. It is produced by specialized cells called melanocytes, which sit at the boundary between the outer skin (epidermis) and the deeper layer (dermis). Under normal circumstances, each melanocyte connects with around 40 nearby skin cells, supplying them with melanin in a carefully regulated process.[^5] When UV radiation hits the skin repeatedly over time, keratinocytes — the cells surrounding melanocytes — become damaged and begin sending out inflammatory chemical signals. Those signals stimulate melanocytes to ramp up pigment production continuously, even after the sun exposure has ended.[^6]

This is what makes solar lentigines different from a regular tan. A tan fades because the UV stimulus goes away. Solar lentigines, by contrast, develop because the keratinocytes themselves have been altered by accumulated UV damage, and they now send those "produce more melanin" signals indefinitely.[^7] The result is a permanent patch of excess pigment — one that tends to slowly expand or darken if sun exposure continues.

Age spots differ from other common skin marks in important ways:

- **Freckles** (ephelides) are flat and light-colored but fade in winter and are primarily genetic. Solar lentigines darken with age and don't fade seasonally.[^8]
- **Moles** (nevi) are usually raised, involve different cell types, and can appear anywhere on the body regardless of sun exposure.
- **Seborrheic keratoses** are raised, waxy-looking, and often have a "stuck on" appearance. They are completely benign but can look alarming.
- **Lentigo maligna**, discussed in detail below, is a slow-growing form of melanoma that can be indistinguishable from an age spot to the naked eye.[^9]

> **Key Fact:** Solar lentigines develop on sun-exposed skin because repeated UV radiation triggers keratinocytes to continuously produce chemical signals that activate nearby melanocytes — even years after the original sun exposure occurred. Unlike a tan, which fades when UV stimulus stops, the cellular programming behind age spots tends to be permanent.[^7]

## Who Gets Them and Why

### Age

Age spots become more common after 40, though they can appear earlier in people with significant sun histories. The relationship is not just chronological — it reflects the cumulative burden of UV exposure over a lifetime, combined with a gradual reduction in the skin's ability to repair and regulate pigment distribution as we age.[^3]

### Skin Tone

Fair-skinned individuals (Fitzpatrick skin types I–III) are more susceptible to solar lentigines because lighter skin contains less melanin overall, offering less intrinsic UV protection — a natural SPF of roughly 3.3 compared to 13.4 in darker skin types.[^10] That said, people of all skin tones can and do develop solar lentigines. And for people with darker skin tones, certain treatments for hyperpigmentation carry unique risks, which is explored later in this guide.

### Sun Exposure History

This is the single strongest risk factor. Age spots are, in a real sense, a cumulative record of UV exposure. Research confirms that solar lentigines on the back are strongly and independently associated with both cumulative sun exposure and the number of sunburns experienced in childhood — even after controlling for age, sex, and skin type.[^8] The damage that creates spots at 55 often begins in earnest at 15.

### Tanning Beds

Artificial UV carries the same risks as natural sun exposure — and in some respects, greater ones. Tanning beds emit predominantly UVA radiation, which is primarily linked to premature skin aging and pigmentation changes. Using tanning beds before age 35 has been associated with a 75% increase in the risk of developing malignant melanoma.[^11]

### Genetics

Some individuals are simply more prone to hyperpigmentation regardless of sun history, suggesting a genetic component to melanocyte behavior. Research into this area is ongoing.

### Hormonal Factors

Hormonal shifts — pregnancy, oral contraceptive use, hormone replacement therapy, and menopause — can trigger a related but distinct condition called melasma, which presents as larger, irregular patches of pigmentation, often symmetrically placed on the face. While melasma and solar lentigines are different conditions, they share the underlying mechanism of melanocyte activation and can overlap in appearance.[^12] Understanding which type of pigmentation is present matters, because they respond differently to treatment.

### Oxidative Stress

Functional and integrative medicine practitioners often emphasize the role of reactive oxygen species (ROS) — unstable molecules produced by UV exposure, inflammation, and other stressors — in accelerating pigment changes in the skin. There is solid mechanistic grounding for this perspective: research confirms that excessive ROS exacerbates skin pigmentation and aging, contributing to tone unevenness and pigmentary disorders.[^13] UV-induced ROS drives oxidative damage to cellular DNA and signals melanocytes to increase melanin output. A high antioxidant status — achievable through diet and targeted supplementation — represents a biologically rational strategy for supporting skin health, even if the direct research linking antioxidant status specifically to age spot formation is still maturing.[^14]

## When to Be Concerned: Distinguishing Age Spots from Skin Cancer

This section is direct because it needs to be.

Typical age spots are benign and do not become cancerous. But there is a type of melanoma — lentigo maligna — that develops on chronically sun-damaged skin, grows slowly, and can look almost identical to a harmless age spot for years.[^9] It is the second most common subtype of melanoma in the United States, with an annual incidence of approximately 1.37 cases per 100,000 people, and it most frequently appears on the head and neck — the same areas where solar lentigines cluster.[^15] The challenge is not rare: studies on periorbital pigmented lesions have documented how frequently lentigo maligna and its benign look-alikes are confused with one another, even among trained clinicians.[^16]

The standard tool for distinguishing concerning spots from benign ones is the **ABCDE criteria**:

- **A — Asymmetry:** A true age spot is symmetrical. Draw an imaginary line through it and both halves should match. A melanoma lesion often does not.
- **B — Border:** Age spots have smooth, regular, well-defined edges. Melanoma borders tend to be irregular, scalloped, or blurred.
- **C — Color:** Age spots are uniformly one shade of tan or brown. Multiple colors within a single lesion — shades of black, blue, pink, gray, or red — are a warning sign.
- **D — Diameter:** Most age spots are smaller than 6 millimeters (about the size of a pencil eraser). Larger lesions warrant attention.
- **E — Evolving:** This is often the most important factor. A true age spot is stable. It may slowly darken over years, but it does not change shape, develop new colors, or suddenly grow. Any spot that is changing — in size, shape, or color — deserves prompt evaluation.[^17]

The "ugly duckling" sign is a complementary concept: any lesion that simply looks different from all your other spots is worth showing to a dermatologist.[^18]

Specific warning signs that require a dermatologist visit without delay:
- Irregular edges or multiple colors within one spot
- Any spot that bleeds, itches, crusts, or becomes raised
- Rapid growth or any spot appearing in an unusual location
- A spot that is new on skin with significant sun damage history — especially on the face or scalp

When evaluation is needed, dermoscopy (a handheld lens that illuminates skin structures not visible to the naked eye) and sometimes biopsy are the tools dermatologists use to distinguish benign lentigines from lentigo maligna and other concerning lesions. These are the appropriate instruments for uncertain cases — not watchful waiting at home.

> **Key Fact:** Lentigo maligna, a slow-growing form of melanoma in situ, develops predominantly on chronically sun-damaged facial skin and can closely mimic a benign age spot for years. The ABCDE rule — Asymmetry, Border, Color, Diameter, Evolving — is the key framework for identifying spots that warrant professional evaluation. Any spot that is changing should be seen by a dermatologist.[^9][^15]

## Conventional Treatments: What the Research Shows

A 2025 systematic review of 41 clinical trials involving 3,234 patients provides the most comprehensive current picture of what works for solar lentigines.[^19] The findings are worth understanding because they help set realistic expectations — most treatments lighten rather than eliminate spots, and results depend heavily on consistency, sun protection, and individual skin response.

### Well-Established Approaches

**Topical hydroquinone** is the most extensively studied skin-lightening agent in dermatology, used for decades to treat solar lentigines, melasma, and other hyperpigmentation.[^20] It works by inhibiting tyrosinase, the enzyme that catalyzes melanin production. In the United States, over-the-counter formulations currently require a prescription (the regulatory landscape around OTC hydroquinone has shifted in recent years); prescription-strength formulations typically contain 4%. Long-term or excessive use can cause a paradoxical darkening reaction called ochronosis, which is why use should be supervised and time-limited. Hydroquinone is most effective when combined with sun protection and sometimes with retinoids or other agents.

**Retinoids (tretinoin, retinol)** speed up skin cell turnover, gradually pushing pigmented cells toward the surface where they are shed. Tretinoin, the prescription-strength form, has solid research support for hyperpigmentation and photoaging broadly. The combination of mequinol 2% and tretinoin 0.01% achieved efficacy rates between 52.6% and over 80% in clinical trials for facial solar lentigines.[^19] Retinoids increase sun sensitivity, require a sunscreen-first approach, and produce visible results over months, not weeks.

**Laser therapy and intense pulsed light (IPL)** represent the most effective procedural options. Picosecond lasers have demonstrated 67.9%–93.02% success rates, and IPL shows 74.6%–90% success in published trials.[^19] These treatments work by targeting pigmented cells with focused light energy. They require professional administration, carry costs, and — importantly — carry a meaningful risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) in individuals with darker skin tones (Fitzpatrick types IV–VI). This is not a reason to avoid these treatments categorically, but it is a critical reason to work with a dermatologist experienced in skin of color.

**Cryotherapy** (controlled freezing of lesions) is effective in 37%–71.4% of cases in studies but is associated with a higher rate of significant side effects than laser options, including blistering and PIH.[^19]

**Chemical peels** using glycolic acid or trichloroacetic acid (TCA) remove outer layers of skin and can lighten pigmented lesions, with TCA peels achieving 12%–46% success in trials. Professional-grade peels carry higher risk and require appropriate expertise; home-use peels at lower concentrations offer more modest effects.

### Emerging and Mixed Evidence

**Niacinamide (vitamin B3)** has a growing evidence base and works differently from most brightening agents — it interrupts the transfer of melanosomes (melanin packets) from melanocytes to keratinocytes rather than blocking melanin production directly.[^21] Its safety profile is excellent, making it an appealing option for long-term use and for sensitive or darker skin tones. Clinical research supports measurable improvement in hyperpigmentation with regular use, and it combines well with other brightening ingredients.

**Tranexamic acid** has attracted significant research attention over the past decade, initially for melasma and increasingly for other hyperpigmentation types. It works by inhibiting plasmin-mediated pathways that activate melanocyte stimulation from UV exposure. Clinical studies show meaningful reduction in pigmentation indices, and its safety profile is favorable.[^22]

**Azelaic acid** has dual utility — it addresses both hyperpigmentation and inflammatory skin concerns. Two randomized controlled trials support its use for melanin-related conditions, and it is generally well-tolerated by a wide range of skin types.[^23]

**Kojic acid**, derived from fungi, inhibits tyrosinase similarly to hydroquinone but with a different safety profile. Evidence supports meaningful efficacy, though kojic acid is notoriously unstable in formulations — products that have changed color significantly may have lost potency.[^23]

**Alpha-arbutin** works via a mechanism related to hydroquinone but with a gentler profile; it is derived from bearberry. Emerging evidence supports its brightening effects, and it is commonly found in over-the-counter serums.

> **Key Fact:** Across a 2025 systematic review of 41 clinical trials, intense pulsed light (IPL) demonstrated 74.6%–90% success rates and picosecond laser achieved 67.9%–93.02% success for solar lentigines. Topical agents provide meaningful improvement for many patients, with the mequinol/tretinoin combination showing the strongest topical evidence. Realistic expectations matter: most treatments lighten significantly rather than eliminate spots completely, and all require consistent sun protection to prevent recurrence.[^19]

## Nutritional and Lifestyle Approaches

### Sun Protection — The Non-Negotiable Foundation

Before any cream, peel, or procedure is discussed, broad-spectrum sunscreen has to come first. The evidence for sun protection as the most impactful available tool is straightforward: it is the single most effective strategy for preventing new age spots and for keeping existing ones from worsening.[^24] Without it, nearly every topical treatment is a losing battle — UV exposure can and does reverse treatment progress.

Daily broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher, applied consistently (and reapplied every two hours during prolonged sun exposure), is the standard recommendation. For those actively treating hyperpigmentation, SPF 50 or higher is often recommended by dermatologists. Protective clothing, wide-brimmed hats, and shade-seeking during peak UV hours (10 AM to 4 PM) meaningfully complement sunscreen use.

A note worth making: the goal of sun protection is smart moderation, not hermetic avoidance. Some sun exposure supports vitamin D synthesis and has well-documented benefits for mood and circadian rhythm. The evidence suggests approximately 15 minutes of morning or evening sun exposure is sufficient for vitamin D — prolonged, unprotected midday exposure is where risk accumulates.

### Vitamin C — Topical and Dietary

Vitamin C (L-ascorbic acid) plays a role in both preventing and treating UV-induced pigmentation. Topically, it inhibits tyrosinase — the same enzyme targeted by hydroquinone — and reduces existing melanin intermediates through direct chemical interaction with pigment already formed.[^25] A 2019 Bayesian meta-analysis across 31 randomized controlled trials concluded that topical vitamin C prevents UV-induced pigmentation, with objective skin lightening measurements supporting its use for solar lentigines specifically.[^26] Notably, the molecule is inherently unstable in formulations: oxidation degrades it quickly. Effective topical vitamin C products require low pH formulations, opaque packaging, and careful storage.[^27]

Dietary vitamin C is important for collagen synthesis and supports the skin's antioxidant defenses, though it does not deliver the same targeted depigmenting effects as topical application.

> **Key Fact:** Topical L-ascorbic acid inhibits tyrosinase activity, reduces existing melanin intermediates, and provides antioxidant protection against UV-generated free radicals. A meta-analysis of 31 randomized controlled trials confirmed measurable brightening effects on solar lentigines. Formulation stability is a real challenge — look for products in opaque, airless packaging stored away from heat and light.[^25][^26]

### Carotenoids, Polyphenols, and the Diet Connection

This is an area where the mechanistic evidence is more mature than the direct clinical evidence for age spots specifically — but the picture is coherent and worth understanding.

Carotenoids (beta-carotene, lycopene, lutein, zeaxanthin) accumulate in the skin and provide photoprotective activity by absorbing UV energy and functioning as antioxidants.[^28] A 2021 clinical review published in *Photodermatology, Photoimmunology & Photomedicine* confirmed that oral carotenoid supplementation reduces UV-induced skin damage, particularly from UVA radiation.[^29] A meta-analysis of 61 dietary intervention studies found that dietary carotenoids reduce skin redness through antioxidant and photoprotective effects.[^30] Whether these effects translate specifically to fewer age spots has not been adequately studied, but the upstream biology is rational and well-supported.

Polyphenols — found in green tea, berries, dark chocolate, pomegranates, and numerous other foods — have antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and emerging evidence for inhibiting melanin synthesis pathways (melanogenesis inhibition).[^31] Flavanols and polyphenol supplements have been found to alleviate skin photoaging in meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.[^32]

A broadly anti-inflammatory diet — rich in colorful plant foods, low in refined sugars and heavily processed oils — provides the substrate for antioxidant defenses and supports the skin's intrinsic repair mechanisms. Framing this honestly: the direct evidence linking specific dietary patterns to age spot reduction is limited. The mechanistic evidence for antioxidant and anti-inflammatory diets supporting skin health is substantially stronger. This is a case where dietary change is low-risk, biologically rational, and broadly supported — even if the age spot-specific clinical trials haven't yet been done.

### Gut Health, Oxidative Stress, and Skin Pigmentation

Integrative and functional medicine practitioners often emphasize the connection between chronic oxidative stress, impaired antioxidant capacity, and accelerated skin aging including pigmentation changes. Research confirms that oxidative stress is directly implicated in the pathogenesis of hyperpigmentation disorders — it activates cellular signaling pathways that promote excessive melanin production.[^33] There is also emerging research connecting gut microbiome dysbiosis to oxidative stress in skin conditions, though most of this work is in inflammatory diseases like psoriasis rather than solar lentigines specifically.[^34]

On the frequently asked question of liver health: despite the name "liver spots," solar lentigines have no direct causal relationship to liver disease or liver function. The name most likely originated from their color, not their biology. However, it is fair to note that metabolic health broadly — including the liver's role in managing oxidative stress and inflammation — is relevant to overall skin aging. This is different from saying impaired liver function causes age spots; the research does not support that direct claim.

### Sleep and Stress

Chronic stress and poor sleep elevate cortisol, promote systemic inflammation, and accelerate the oxidative burden on the skin.[^13] The direct connection between stress and solar lentigines specifically has not been well-studied, but the upstream effects on skin aging and cellular health are established. This is not a footnote — it is a genuine lever in overall skin health.

### Hydration

Adequate hydration supports skin barrier function and overall skin health. Direct evidence connecting hydration to hyperpigmentation is limited. This honestly belongs in the "good practice with limited pigmentation-specific evidence" category — worth doing, not worth overstating.

## Natural and Integrative Approaches: What People Use and What We Know

Many people reach for natural options first — whether because of cost, preference for gentler approaches, or simply because a familiar ingredient is already in the kitchen. What follows is an honest look at what the evidence suggests for the most commonly discussed options.

**Apple cider vinegar** is frequently recommended online for age spots, with acetic acid cited as a potential mild exfoliant. The evidence is largely anecdotal. There are also documented reports of chemical burns from applying undiluted apple cider vinegar to skin.[^35] At appropriate dilutions and appropriate pH, mild acid exfoliation has rational properties — but the lack of standardization in home preparations is a genuine safety concern.

**Aloe vera** contains aloesin, a compound with demonstrated tyrosinase-inhibiting and DOPA-oxidase-inhibiting activity.[^23] A randomized controlled trial found that aloesin was effective at treating UV-induced pigmentation both independently and synergistically with arbutin. Topical aloe vera is generally well-tolerated and safe for most people. The clinical effect is likely modest compared to well-studied topical agents, but aloe belongs in the "plausible mechanism, emerging evidence, low risk" category.

**Lemon juice** deserves specific safety attention. The vitamin C content is often cited as a rationale, but lemon juice contains furocoumarins — phototoxic compounds that react with UV radiation to cause severe inflammatory reactions, blistering, and ironically, deeper and more stubborn hyperpigmentation (a condition called phytophotodermatitis).[^36] Applying lemon juice to skin before sun exposure — even unintentionally, such as handling citrus before going outdoors — can result in burns and dark patches that last for months. This is a genuine safety risk, not a theoretical one. Separately, the low and variable pH of undiluted lemon juice can disrupt the skin barrier and cause chemical irritation. If you want topical vitamin C, a properly formulated L-ascorbic acid product at a controlled concentration and pH is a substantially safer and more effective choice.

**Turmeric / curcumin** has anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, and some research suggests it may inhibit melanogenesis pathways. The evidence in humans for hyperpigmentation is emerging and limited. Dietary curcumin consumption is generally safe and broadly health-supportive. Topical use carries a practical challenge: turmeric stains skin, clothing, and surfaces a vivid yellow.

**Licorice root extract (glabridin)** is among the more scientifically grounded natural options for hyperpigmentation. Glabridin, the active compound extracted from Glycyrrhiza glabra, inhibits tyrosinase and UV-induced pigmentation. In vitro studies have reported skin-lightening effects 16 times greater than hydroquinone in cell models.[^37] A double-blind clinical study comparing a glabridin-containing brightener to 4% hydroquinone found significant pigmentation reductions, with the glabridin formulation producing greater increases in measured skin brightness.[^37] The evidence base is still smaller than that for established pharmaceutical agents, but licorice root extract has one of the more credible research profiles among natural lightening agents.

**Castor oil, rosehip oil, and sea buckthorn** are frequently recommended in natural health circles. These oils contain antioxidants and fatty acids relevant to skin barrier health. The evidence for pigmentation specifically is minimal. They are generally safe for topical use and may offer complementary skin nourishment, but should not be expected to significantly lighten established age spots.

Framing this section honestly: many natural approaches are safe to use and may provide incremental benefit. They are unlikely to match the efficacy of well-studied topical or procedural treatments for significant lightening. Their stronger value may be as complementary, preventive, or maintenance tools. The most important safety point: do not allow focus on natural remedies to delay evaluation of any spot that is changing, irregular, or new.

> **Key Fact:** Licorice root extract (glabridin) has one of the strongest research profiles among natural agents for hyperpigmentation. It inhibits tyrosinase through a mechanism similar to other depigmenting agents, with in vitro studies showing significant melanogenesis inhibition and a double-blind human study demonstrating measurable skin brightening. Aloe vera's active compound aloesin has similarly shown tyrosinase-inhibiting activity in a controlled trial. Both are plausible, generally safe options — most effective as complementary tools rather than standalone treatments.[^23][^37]

## A Note on Skin Tone and Equity

Most age spot treatment research has historically been conducted on lighter-skinned populations, a limitation that matters for anyone with a medium-to-deep complexion seeking to use these findings.

People with all skin tones develop solar lentigines, and the conditions deserve equal attention across demographics. What differs is the risk profile for certain treatments. Laser therapy, chemical peels, and high-concentration acid treatments carry a significantly elevated risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) in Fitzpatrick skin types IV–VI — a cruel irony, in that aggressive treatments intended to lighten spots can create new, deeper ones if not applied with expertise.[^38]

Sunscreen, notably, remains the prevention strategy with the strongest evidence across all skin types — and there is a persistent educational gap in which people with darker skin tones are less likely to use it regularly, often based on the belief that darker skin does not need protection. This is not accurate: all skin types are susceptible to UV-induced damage, and for people already managing hyperpigmentation, sun protection is arguably even more essential.[^39]

For individuals with deeper complexions seeking treatment for hyperpigmentation, seeking a dermatologist with specific expertise in skin of color is not a detail — it is the difference between treatment that helps and treatment that harms.

## Prevention: The Most Powerful Tool You Have

Prevention is meaningfully more effective than reversal. Most treatments for established age spots require months of consistent application, come with risks, and deliver partial rather than complete results. Prevention, by contrast, has strong evidence and straightforward application.

Daily broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher remains the single most impactful intervention, and the research supporting it is unambiguous.[^24] Start today if you haven't. Reapply if you're outdoors for extended periods.

Begin sun protection habits early. The UV damage that creates spots at 50 often begins in earnest in childhood and young adulthood. Sunburns in childhood are independently associated with higher rates of solar lentigines decades later.[^8] Teaching children and teenagers sun protection habits is genuine long-term health investment.

A diet broadly rich in antioxidants — colorful vegetables and fruits, especially those high in carotenoids, polyphenols, and vitamin C — supports the skin's internal defenses against UV damage. The direct evidence for age spot prevention through diet specifically is limited; the biological rationale is strong.[^28][^31]

Avoid tanning beds. The artificial UV they produce carries the same risks as natural sun exposure — and in many devices, a higher concentration of UVA radiation, which is more deeply damaging than UVB to connective tissue and pigment regulation.[^11]

Annual skin checks with a dermatologist are an important preventive health behavior for adults over 40, particularly those with significant lifetime sun exposure or a personal or family history of skin cancer. These are not alarming appointments — they are low-effort, high-value.

## When to See a Doctor

The following situations warrant a dermatologist visit. None of these are reasons for alarm; they are simply reasons to act:

- Any spot that has **changed** in size, shape, or color
- Any spot that **bleeds, itches, or crusts**
- Any spot that is **new** on skin with a history of significant sun exposure, particularly on the face
- Any spot that **looks different** from your other spots — the "ugly duckling"
- Any desire to **confirm a diagnosis** before beginning any treatment regimen
- Cosmetically significant spots where you would like to discuss options beyond what is available over the counter

A dermatologist can examine spots with dermoscopy, recommend biopsy when appropriate, and offer prescription or procedural treatments unavailable without professional guidance. Scheduling an appointment is not dramatic — it is good stewardship of your health, and it takes very little time.

## Practical Summary: A Starting Point for Anyone Ready to Act

Age spots are common, usually benign, and genuinely responsive to the right strategies. Here is where to begin:

**Today:** Apply broad-spectrum sunscreen — not just at the beach, but every day. UV comes through car windows. It bounces off pavement and water. Daily SPF is your most cost-effective skin investment.

**This week:** Look at your diet with an antioxidant lens. Add more colorful foods — berries, tomatoes, dark leafy greens, orange vegetables. Less refined sugar and processed oils. This supports more than just your skin, but your skin will notice.

**When you're ready to try a topical:** A vitamin C serum (L-ascorbic acid, in a stabilized formulation) is a reasonable, accessible first step for pigmentation support and broader photoprotection. Add it to a morning routine, layered under sunscreen. If you want something more targeted, niacinamide is gentle, well-researched, and plays well with other ingredients.

**If you haven't had a skin check recently:** Schedule one. If anything on your skin is changing, schedule one now.

**If you want to pursue brightening treatments:** Talk to a dermatologist first, especially if you have a medium or deeper skin tone. The range of options is real, and the right one depends on your skin, your goals, and your history.

Our skin carries the visible record of our years — the summers, the laughter, the days outdoors. Caring for it is not vanity. It is attention to the body we inhabit, and to the long-term wellbeing that begins with knowing what we're looking at.
