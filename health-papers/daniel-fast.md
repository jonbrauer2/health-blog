*A reference for AAHWC health coaches, church health ministry leaders, and individuals/families*

-----

## TL;DR

- The Daniel Fast is a 21-day partial fast modeled on Daniel 1 and Daniel 10 that excludes animal products, refined foods, added sweeteners, leavened breads, caffeine, and alcohol. Published peer-reviewed studies — primarily by Bloomer and colleagues at the University of Memphis — show meaningful short-term improvements in blood pressure, total and LDL cholesterol, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and inflammation in healthy adults.[^1][^2][^3]
- For most healthy adults the fast is safe and well-tolerated (98.7% compliance in the Bloomer 2010 trial), but it is not appropriate without medical supervision for people with insulin- or sulfonylurea-treated diabetes, eating-disorder history, advanced kidney or liver disease, pregnancy or lactation without modification, young children, frail elders, or athletes in heavy training.[^1]
- The fast’s greatest value is as a structured *nutrition education and spiritual reset*, not as a quick-fix diet — the 21-day rationale is biblical rather than physiologic, and the science of habit formation makes the post-fast transition the decisive determinant of long-term benefit.[^4]

## Opening: What the Daniel Fast Is — and Isn’t

The **Daniel Fast** is a partial, food-based fast inspired by two passages from the Old Testament book of Daniel. In Daniel 1:8–16, the young prophet refuses the royal Babylonian rations and asks instead for “vegetables to eat and water to drink” for a 10-day test, at the end of which he and his companions appear “healthier and better nourished” than those who ate the king’s food. In Daniel 10:2–3, Daniel describes a three-week mourning fast: “I ate no choice food; no meat or wine touched my lips, and I used no lotions at all until the three weeks were over.” From those two passages, modern Christian communities have developed a 21-day partial fast that emphasizes whole-plant foods and water while excluding meat, dairy, eggs, leavened breads, added sweeteners, processed foods, caffeine, and alcohol.[^1][^5]

The phrase “Daniel Fast” does not appear in Scripture; it is a modern application of biblical principle. That distinction matters for both pastoral honesty and for clinical conversation: participants should understand that they are joining a *tradition of inference from Scripture*, not following an explicitly prescribed biblical diet. The fast’s purpose, as taught in most ministries, is primarily **spiritual** — humility, prayer, dependence on God, and the disciplined re-ordering of appetite — with physical benefits as a secondary, though often substantial, outcome.[^5]

From a clinical standpoint, the Daniel Fast is essentially an **ad libitum, strict, whole-food vegan dietary pattern with additional exclusions** (no caffeine, alcohol, added sugar, or leavening). It is not a calorie-restricted diet; participants eat to satiety. Yet most studies show spontaneous calorie reduction — Bloomer et al. (2010) dietary records documented an average drop of approximately 460 kcal/day (from 2,185 ± 94 to 1,722 ± 85 kcal) because the allowed foods are bulky, fiber-rich, and minimally processed.[^1]

**What this handout will and will not do.** This document summarizes what the peer-reviewed evidence currently shows (mostly favorable for short-term cardiometabolic markers), where the evidence is thin or extrapolated from broader plant-based diet research, who should be cautious, and how to plan, execute, and transition safely. It is not a substitute for individualized medical care.

## 1. Understanding the Daniel Fast Protocol

### Biblical foundation

Two passages anchor the fast:

> **Daniel 1:12** — “Please test your servants for ten days: Give us nothing but vegetables to eat and water to drink.”
> 
> **Daniel 10:2–3** — “At that time I, Daniel, mourned for three weeks. I ate no choice food; no meat or wine touched my lips, and I used no lotions at all until the three weeks were over.”

The 10-day test in Daniel 1 and the 21-day mourning fast in Daniel 10 are the source for the modern fast’s duration and food rules. Most contemporary ministries combine the two — the **21-day duration** from Daniel 10, the **plant-based food list** from Daniel 1.[^1][^5]

### The modern protocol — foods included

Across the most widely cited contemporary protocols (Gregory’s *The Ultimate Daniel Fast*; Warren’s *Daniel Plan* derivatives; Bloomer’s published study protocols), the following foods are permitted:[^5][^6]

- **All fruits** (fresh, frozen, dried without added sugar, canned in own juice, 100% juice in moderation)
- **All vegetables** (fresh, frozen, dried, canned without preservatives)
- **Whole grains** — amaranth, barley, brown rice, buckwheat, bulgur, freekeh, millet, oats, quinoa, rye, sorghum, spelt, teff, whole wheat, wild rice
- **Legumes** — all beans, lentils, split peas, black-eyed peas, peanuts
- **Nuts and seeds** — almonds, walnuts, cashews, pecans, sunflower, sesame, flax, chia, pumpkin (unsalted, unsweetened); nut and seed butters with no added sugar or oil
- **Plant oils** in modest amounts (olive, coconut, sesame, avocado) — *note: stricter interpretations exclude all extracted oils*
- **Herbs, spices, salt and pepper**
- **Water** as the primary beverage; unsweetened plant milks acceptable in most versions

### Foods excluded

- All **meat, poultry, fish, eggs, and dairy** (animal products)
- **Added sweeteners** — sugar, honey, agave, syrups, artificial sweeteners, stevia (in stricter versions)
- **Leavened breads** and most baked goods (yeast, baking soda, baking powder)
- **Refined grains** — white flour, white rice
- **Processed foods** containing additives, preservatives, artificial flavors/colors
- **Fried foods**
- **Caffeine** — coffee, black/green tea, energy drinks (most herbal teas allowed)
- **Alcohol**

### Variations across communities

There is no single authoritative food list. Common variations include:

- **Modified Daniel Fast (Bloomer & Alleman):** allows approximately 3 oz lean meat or fish and 8 oz skim milk daily; studied because the traditional plan reduces HDL-C and lean body mass.[^7]
- **Lenten Daniel Fast:** extended to 40 days for Lent, as in the Vaughn et al. (2018) church-based study.[^8]
- **Pregnancy/lactation modifications:** many ministries advise adding eggs, fish, or yogurt for protein and DHA.[^9]
- **Diabetic modifications:** lower-glycemic emphasis, smaller grain portions, sometimes added lean protein.
- **“Strict” versions:** exclude all oils, all soy, and all leavening.

Health coaches should ask each participant *which version* they are following before giving counsel; assuming uniformity is a common error.

## 2. Health Benefits: What the Research Shows

The most direct evidence comes from a small but coherent cluster of trials conducted at the University of Memphis Cardiorespiratory/Metabolic Laboratory under Richard J. Bloomer, PhD, plus one church-community study from Drexel/AmeriHealth Caritas. Where direct Daniel Fast research is thin, we extrapolate cautiously from the much larger body of whole-food plant-based and vegan diet research.

### 2.1 Blood pressure

In the foundational Bloomer et al. (2010) trial of 43 adults, systolic blood pressure fell from 114.65 ± 2.34 to 105.93 ± 2.12 mmHg (*p* = 0.007) and diastolic from 72.23 ± 1.59 to 67.00 ± 1.43 mmHg (*p* = 0.03) after 21 days.[^1] The Trepanowski et al. (2012) krill-oil trial replicated the SBP drop (110.7 to 105.5 mmHg, *p* < 0.05).[^3] Vaughn et al.’s (2018) 40-Day Journey church program — 135 enrolled, 69 completed; mean age 49.2 years; predominantly African American women — found SBP fell from 142 to 133 mmHg in the Church 1 subgroup (*t*(22) = 2.46, *p* = 0.02), a 9 mmHg drop.[^8]

These findings are consistent with the broader vegan/vegetarian literature. Gibbs et al. (2021, *Journal of Hypertension*), pooling 41 controlled trials (8,416 participants), concluded that plant-based diets lower both SBP and DBP across sex and BMI, with the DASH diet producing a pooled SBP reduction of −5.53 mmHg (95% CI −7.95, −3.12) and lacto-ovo vegetarian patterns −5.47 mmHg (95% CI −7.60, −3.34).[^10]

### 2.2 Lipids — total cholesterol, LDL, and the HDL caveat

Bloomer et al. (2010) reported total cholesterol falling 19% (171.07 ± 4.57 → 138.69 ± 4.39 mg/dL) and LDL-C falling 23% (98.38 ± 3.89 → 76.07 ± 3.53 mg/dL) — both highly statistically significant (*p* < 0.0001).[^1] The Vaughn et al. (2018) church-based 40-day program found mean total cholesterol fell from 171.7 ± 12.0 to 158.8 ± 30.6 mg/dL (*p* = 0.0001).[^8]

**The HDL caveat:** HDL-C also fell 14% (55.65 ± 2.50 → 47.58 ± 2.19 mg/dL, *p* = 0.02) in the Bloomer 2010 trial.[^1] Bloomer’s group has explicitly flagged this as a downside of the strict vegan protocol and has shown that a *modified* version including a daily serving of lean meat and skim milk preserves HDL while delivering similar LDL reduction.[^7] For most participants the favorable LDL change outweighs the HDL dip; for participants with low baseline HDL or known low-HDL atherogenic dyslipidemia, the modified protocol or post-fast HDL-supportive habits deserve emphasis.

The 2023 European Heart Journal meta-analysis of 30 randomized trials confirms that vegetarian and vegan diets reduce total cholesterol by −0.34 mmol/L (~13 mg/dL), LDL-C by −0.30 mmol/L (~12 mg/dL), and apolipoprotein B by 14% versus omnivorous controls.[^11]

### 2.3 Insulin sensitivity and blood sugar

Bloomer et al. (2010) found insulin fell 24% (4.42 → 3.37 μU/mL), HOMA-IR fell 26% (0.97 → 0.72), and C-reactive protein fell 49% (3.15 → 1.60 mg/L) over 21 days, though these did not reach statistical significance in this small sample.[^1] The Trepanowski et al. (2012) study in a metabolically heterogeneous sample (including individuals with impaired fasting glucose and type 2 diabetes) found these effects reached significance: fasting glucose dropped from 101.4 to 91.7 mg/dL, fasting insulin from 7.92 to 5.76 μU/mL, and HOMA-IR from 2.06 to 1.40 (all *p* < 0.05).[^3] This suggests effects are larger in those with metabolic impairment at baseline — the population most likely to benefit clinically.

### 2.4 Inflammation and oxidative stress

A companion paper (Bloomer et al., 2011, *Nutrition & Metabolism*) showed that 21 days of the Daniel Fast significantly increased Trolox Equivalent Antioxidant Capacity (TEAC) and nitrate/nitrite levels and decreased malondialdehyde (a lipid-peroxidation marker) and hydrogen peroxide — biomarkers consistent with reduced oxidative stress.[^2] CRP fell 49% in the 2010 trial, a clinically meaningful anti-inflammatory signal.[^1]

### 2.5 Weight management

Bloomer et al. (2010) did *not* find significant changes in body weight, BMI, or body composition in 21 days — the duration was too short for substantial weight loss.[^1] The 40-day church program (Vaughn et al., 2018) found an average loss of 3.9 lbs over six weeks (*t*(60) = 2.93, *p* = 0.005), with waist circumference dropping 0.7 inches and 71% of completers reporting weight loss at six-month follow-up (mean 9 lbs lost).[^8] Weight loss is real but modest; the Daniel Fast should not be marketed as a weight-loss diet.

### 2.6 Digestive health and gut microbiome

Direct Daniel Fast microbiome data are not published, but David et al.’s landmark 2014 *Nature* study demonstrated that short-term consumption of diets composed entirely of animal or plant products alters microbial community structure and overwhelms inter-individual differences in microbial gene expression. Microbiome changes were detectable within 1–2 days, with the plant-based arm increasing fiber-fermenting bacteria (*Roseburia*, *Eubacterium rectale*, *Ruminococcus bromii*) associated with butyrate production and lower colonic inflammation.[^12] The increased fiber intake documented in Daniel Fast participants (Bloomer et al., 2010 recorded a doubling of fiber consumption) supports stool regularity and short-chain fatty acid production.[^1] Counsel participants that *transient* increases in flatulence and bloating during the first week are expected and resolve as the microbiome adapts.

### 2.7 Nutrient status improvements

Bloomer’s dietary records documented significant increases in dietary fiber, vitamin C, and carbohydrate percentage, with significant reductions in saturated fat, trans fat, and dietary cholesterol — exactly the macronutrient shifts most strongly associated with cardiovascular and cancer risk reduction.[^1] A 2023 umbrella review of plant-based diets pooling seven systematic reviews (51 primary studies) found significant reductions in weight (−2.09 kg), BMI (−0.95 kg/m²), waist circumference (−2.20 cm), fasting blood glucose (−0.11 mmol/L), and LDL-C (−0.31 mmol/L).[^13]

## 3. Potential Risks and Who Should Be Cautious

The Daniel Fast is broadly safe for healthy adults, but it is a substantial dietary shift and several populations require either medical clearance, modification, or alternative spiritual disciplines.

### 3.1 People with diabetes on insulin or sulfonylureas

The most consequential safety concern. A diet that lowers fasting glucose 10 mg/dL and HOMA-IR 30% within three weeks[^3] is *therapeutically active* — and that is exactly the problem if medications are not adjusted. Insulin, sulfonylureas (glyburide, glipizide, glimepiride), and meglitinides (repaglinide, nateglinide) all carry meaningful hypoglycemia risk that *rises* during dietary restriction. **Anyone on these agents must have their regimen reviewed by their prescriber before and during the fast, with more frequent glucose monitoring (at least 4×/day in the first week).** Metformin, DPP-4 inhibitors, and GLP-1 agonists carry lower hypoglycemia risk but still warrant clinical conversation. SGLT-2 inhibitors raise euglycemic DKA and dehydration concerns and should also be reviewed.

### 3.2 People with a history of eating disorders

Highly restrictive dietary protocols — even framed spiritually — can precipitate or worsen restrictive, binge-purge, or orthorexic patterns. A 2024 meta-analysis (Rijk et al.) of 28 studies reported an average relapse rate of 37% over a mean 31-month follow-up, with restrictive eating as a key precipitant.[^14] The Daniel Fast’s “good food / bad food” framing, public church accountability, and explicit food rules can become disordered cognitive scaffolding in vulnerable individuals. **People with active or past eating disorders should consult their treatment team before participating and may instead benefit from a non-food fast** — a media fast, social media fast, sugar-only modification, or a habit-based fast rather than food restriction.

### 3.3 Pregnant and breastfeeding women

The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics (2016) holds that appropriately planned vegetarian and vegan diets are nutritionally adequate at all stages of life, including pregnancy and lactation.[^9] However, pregnancy and lactation increase requirements for protein, iron, calcium, choline, iodine, B12, and DHA — and the Daniel Fast’s exclusion of all animal products makes meeting these demands harder over 21 days, particularly for women not already eating plant-based. Most Daniel Fast ministries recommend that pregnant or nursing women skip the fast, do a partial/modified version with added eggs, fish, or dairy, or focus the fast on non-food elements while consulting a prenatal provider.

### 3.4 Children and adolescents

Vegan diets can be safe at all ages with planning, but a sudden 21-day shift for a growing child carries higher risk of inadequate energy, protein, B12, iron, calcium, and DHA. Adolescents are particularly vulnerable to dietary restriction triggering eating disorders. Families fasting together should either include modest amounts of dairy, eggs, or fish for minors or limit children to a partial fast (e.g., adding vegetables, removing sugar) rather than the full protocol.

### 3.5 Older adults / frail elders

Older adults often have reduced appetite, lower protein synthesis efficiency (“anabolic resistance”), and higher baseline risk of sarcopenia. The strict vegan version reduces lean body mass even in young adults (Alleman et al., 2013).[^7] In frail elders this could be clinically meaningful. The modified Daniel Fast or close attention to legume + grain protein combinations is preferable.

### 3.6 Chronic kidney disease (CKD) and liver disease

Stage 3–5 CKD often requires individualized protein and potassium management — high-potassium fruits and vegetables can be problematic. Advanced cirrhosis affects protein and ammonia handling. **Both groups should fast only under nephrology or hepatology supervision.**

### 3.7 Competitive and high-volume endurance athletes

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements states that iron requirements are 1.8 times higher for people who follow vegetarian diets than for those who include animal products, because plant (non-heme) iron is less bioavailable; athletes lose additional iron through sweat, foot-strike hemolysis, and GI microbleeding.[^15] Protein needs are also elevated. Consider the modified version, increase legume intake, time vitamin C with iron-rich plant foods to enhance absorption, and avoid initiating the fast during peak training or competition phases.

### 3.8 Gallbladder disease and recent abdominal surgery

A sudden, large increase in legume and fiber intake can trigger gallbladder symptoms in those with stones or biliary dyskinesia, and may cause unwelcome GI distress after surgery. Pre-fast gradual fiber escalation (see Section 5) mitigates this for most people; those with active gallbladder disease should consult their physician.

### 3.9 The nutritional-adequacy debate

A well-planned 21-day Daniel Fast is unlikely to cause clinical deficiency in a previously well-nourished adult because body stores of B12 (3–5 year reserves in healthy people), iron, vitamin D, and most fat-soluble vitamins are ample. **The risk profile changes if the fast is extended beyond 21 days, if the participant starts already deficient, or if the participant’s “Daniel Fast diet” is heavily skewed toward fruit-and-grain rather than including legumes, nuts, seeds, leafy greens, and fortified plant milks.** Coaches should counsel variety and balance, not just compliance.

## 4. Contested Areas: Navigating Mixed Evidence

### 4.1 Why 21 days?

The 21-day duration comes from Daniel 10, not from physiologic optimization. It happens to be long enough to produce measurable cardiometabolic changes[^1][^2] and to begin remodeling the gut microbiome,[^12] but it is *not* long enough to form durable habits in most people. The Lally et al. (2010) habit-formation study at University College London — the most-cited modern research on this question — found that automaticity took on average **66 days** to develop, with individual variation from 18 to 254 days, and that eating habits took a median of 65 days.[^4] The popular “21 days makes a habit” claim is not supported by research. **This is important for coaching: do not promise habit change in 21 days; promise a *starting point*.**

### 4.2 Spiritual benefit vs. physical benefit

The Daniel Fast was undertaken biblically for spiritual reasons. Health coaches should resist two opposite errors:

- **Reductionism:** treating the fast purely as a metabolic intervention drains its meaning for participants and weakens adherence.
- **Spiritualization of physical outcomes:** attributing weight loss to divine favor (rather than fiber and calorie reduction) can foster shame in those whose bodies don’t respond identically.

Honor both dimensions explicitly.

### 4.3 Individual variation

Bloomer’s data show wide variability: in the 2010 trial, BMI ranged from 18.0 to 40.6 kg/m².[^1] Normal-weight subjects responded similarly to overweight/obese subjects in *direction* of change but typically smaller in *magnitude* (less room to improve). Genetic variants in lipoprotein metabolism, baseline insulin sensitivity, gut microbiome composition, and habitual diet all influence response. Set expectations as *averages*, not guarantees.

### 4.4 Sustainability and post-fast outcomes

Most participants regress toward their prior diet after the fast unless additional structure is added. Bloomer & Toline (2014) found approximately 80% compliance at six months in a study that provided ongoing support.[^16] The 40-day church-based intervention (Vaughn et al., 2018) succeeded precisely because it added health education, recipes, text messaging, and group accountability — the fast *plus* structure outperformed the fast alone.[^8]

### 4.5 Comparison to other dietary approaches

The Daniel Fast pattern overlaps substantially with the whole-food plant-based diet (Ornish, Esselstyn, Greger) but is more restrictive (no caffeine, no leavened bread). It differs from the Mediterranean diet primarily in excluding fish, dairy, and wine. It differs from intermittent fasting entirely — IF restricts *timing* of calories; the Daniel Fast restricts *types* of food without restricting timing or quantity. For sustained cardiometabolic benefit, the Mediterranean diet and the DASH diet have stronger long-term outcome data; the Daniel Fast is best understood as a *catalyst* into a longer-term plant-forward eating pattern.

## 5. Practical Guidelines for Safe Implementation

### 5.1 Pre-fast preparation (1–2 weeks before)

- **Get a baseline.** Weight, blood pressure, and (ideally) recent lipid panel, fasting glucose, and CBC. Repeat after the fast — the data motivate and inform.
- **Gradually reduce caffeine** over 5–10 days to avoid withdrawal headaches. Cut by approximately 25% every 2–3 days.
- **Gradually reduce added sugar and refined grains.**
- **Gradually increase fiber.** Sudden doubling of fiber intake triggers gas and bloating; add a serving of beans, lentils, or whole grains every few days in the lead-up.
- **Increase water** to approximately half your body weight in ounces per day (e.g., 75 oz for a 150-lb adult); more if active or in hot climates.
- **Review medications with your prescriber**, especially diabetes, antihypertensive, anticoagulant, and lithium therapies.
- **Plan meals and shop.** Bloomer specifically warns that participants who rely on restaurants and packaged foods struggle — the fast is “forced nutrition education.”[^1]

### 5.2 Meal planning

A balanced day might include:

- **Breakfast:** steel-cut oats with berries, walnuts, ground flax, and unsweetened almond milk
- **Lunch:** large green salad with chickpeas, quinoa, vegetables, sunflower seeds, olive oil + lemon dressing
- **Snack:** apple with almond butter, or hummus with vegetables
- **Dinner:** lentil-vegetable soup with whole-grain (unleavened) bread or brown rice and roasted vegetables
- **Hydration:** water throughout; herbal tea acceptable

Aim for ≥3 cups vegetables and ≥2 cups fruit daily, ≥1 cup legumes, ≥2 servings whole grains, and a handful of nuts/seeds. Variety of color across the day approximates the polyphenol/phytochemical diversity that drives the antioxidant findings documented in Bloomer et al. (2011).[^2]

### 5.3 Supplement considerations

For a 21-day fast in a previously well-nourished adult, the following defaults are reasonable:

- **Vitamin B12:** A short fast is unlikely to deplete stores, but if participants plan to continue plant-based eating beyond 21 days, a B12 supplement is essential. A daily 50–100 μg supplement (or 1,000–2,000 μg twice weekly) reliably prevents deficiency in plant-based eaters.[^17]
- **Iron:** Pair iron-rich plant foods (lentils, tofu, pumpkin seeds, spinach) with vitamin C sources (citrus, peppers, strawberries) and avoid taking with coffee/tea/calcium. Routine supplementation isn’t needed for 21 days unless baseline ferritin is low. Vegetarian iron requirements are 1.8× those of omnivores.[^15]
- **Omega-3s (EPA/DHA):** The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements notes that the body converts ALA (from flaxseed, walnuts, chia) to EPA and DHA, but not efficiently — published estimates put ALA→EPA conversion at approximately 5–10% and ALA→DHA at below 1%.[^18] For a 21-day fast this rarely matters; for those continuing plant-based eating, an **algae-based EPA/DHA supplement** (~250–500 mg/day combined EPA+DHA) is recommended.
- **Vitamin D:** Northern-latitude residents and those with limited sun exposure should check serum 25(OH)D and supplement to the 30+ ng/mL range; this is independent of the fast but often surfaces during baseline labs.
- **Calcium:** Use fortified plant milks, leafy greens, tofu set with calcium sulfate, almonds, and tahini.

Do not start a multivitamin “just in case” if it contains animal-derived gelatin — most ministries consider this a violation of the fast’s spirit. Choose a vegan-formulated supplement if needed.

## 6. Lifestyle Factors Beyond Diet

The Daniel Fast tradition has always been about more than food. The full reward — physical and spiritual — comes from concurrent attention to:

### Sleep

Aim for 7–9 hours. Reduced caffeine usually improves sleep latency within a week; some experience initial sleep disruption from caffeine withdrawal.

### Stress management

The fast itself can be a stressor, especially in the first 3–7 days. Pair the fast with intentional stress reduction: brief daily silence/meditation, journaling, time in nature. Cortisol and inflammation are deeply intertwined; chronic stress can blunt the metabolic benefits of dietary change.

### Exercise

Moderate exercise (walking, light strength training, gentle yoga) supports the fast. Avoid initiating high-intensity training during the first week; the body is adjusting to lower glycemic load and dietary fat. Athletes already in training should consider the modified protocol.

### Community and social support

Fasting in a community context — small group, congregational fast, family fast — substantially improves adherence and emotional outcomes. In Vaughn et al. (2018), 90% of completers reported the program made them feel happier.[^8] Health coaches working with churches should design **group structures** (kickoff event, weekly check-ins, shared recipes, prayer partners) rather than individual programs alone.

### Prayer, Scripture, and spiritual discipline

For most participants, this is the *point*. Daily Scripture reading, intentional prayer, journaling, and worship are typically paired with the food restrictions. The food fast frees up time, attention, and money normally devoted to eating; redirect those toward spiritual practice.

## 7. After the Fast: Transition and Long-Term Considerations

### 7.1 Avoiding the rebound

The single most common failure mode is rebound eating on day 22 — pizza, dessert, alcohol — which not only erases metabolic gains but produces marked GI distress because the gut is no longer adapted to the standard Western diet. **Counsel a 7-day taper** that reintroduces foods slowly: dairy and eggs on days 22–24, fish on days 25–26, modest amounts of meat on day 27, and continued emphasis on plant foods as the dietary base.

### 7.2 Habit formation

Recall Lally et al. (2010): 66 days on average to automatize a new behavior, with eating habits at a median 65 days.[^4] The 21-day fast is approximately one-third of the way to habit formation. To convert short-term gains into sustained change:

- **Pick 1–3 specific behaviors** to carry forward, not “eat plant-based forever.” Examples: one fully plant-based dinner per day, no soda, beans at lunch four days a week.
- **Anchor each new behavior to an existing cue** (after breakfast → take supplement; after Sunday service → meal prep).
- **Plan for lapses.** Missing one day does not reset the clock (Lally et al., 2010); missing two consecutive days starts a pattern of non-performance.[^4]

### 7.3 Sustainable dietary patterns

The Mediterranean diet and the DASH diet have the strongest long-term outcome data and overlap substantially with the Daniel Fast pattern. A reasonable post-fast target is a *plant-forward* diet: 75–90% whole plant foods, with modest amounts of fish, eggs, yogurt, and lean meat; minimal added sugar, refined grain, and ultra-processed food; ample water; limited alcohol. Re-measure weight, blood pressure, and labs at 3 and 6 months to anchor motivation.

## Practical Action Steps

### Pre-fast preparation checklist

- [ ] Medical clearance if you have diabetes (especially on insulin or sulfonylureas), CKD, liver disease, eating-disorder history, are pregnant or nursing, are under 18, or take multiple chronic medications
- [ ] Baseline weight, blood pressure, and (ideally) lipid panel, fasting glucose/HbA1c, CBC, ferritin
- [ ] Taper caffeine over 5–10 days
- [ ] Gradually increase fiber and water over 7–14 days
- [ ] Plan 21 days of meals; shop at least 3 days of food at a time
- [ ] Clear the pantry of non-fast foods that will tempt you
- [ ] Identify your accountability person or group
- [ ] Plan your daily Scripture/prayer time — *protect this on the calendar*
- [ ] Identify a vegan-friendly B12 source if extending beyond 21 days
- [ ] Set 1–3 specific habits to carry forward after day 21

### Daily/weekly monitoring suggestions

- **Daily:** weight (optional, same time of day), water intake, any GI symptoms, energy level (1–10), spiritual journal entry
- **For diabetics:** blood glucose 4×/day in week 1, 2×/day in weeks 2–3
- **For hypertensives:** blood pressure 1×/day, with prescriber’s plan for dose adjustment as readings fall
- **Weekly:** group check-in, recipe planning, progress against the 1–3 carry-forward habits

### Warning signs that warrant stopping or seeking help

**Stop the fast and call your provider** if you experience:

- Persistent hypoglycemia (blood glucose < 70 mg/dL with symptoms)
- Symptomatic hypotension (lightheadedness, fainting on standing)
- Inability to maintain hydration (vomiting, severe diarrhea)
- Chest pain, palpitations, or new shortness of breath
- Severe fatigue beyond expected first-week adjustment
- Resurgence of disordered eating thoughts or behaviors
- Rapid unintentional weight loss (> 3 lbs/week sustained)

**Modify the fast** if you experience:

- Persistent constipation despite adequate fiber and water (add prunes, kiwi, magnesium)
- Persistent gas and bloating beyond week 1 (reduce raw cruciferous vegetables temporarily, soak/sprout legumes)
- Difficulty concentrating at work after week 1 (add more whole-grain carbohydrate, ensure adequate calories)

### When to seek professional consultation

- **Before fasting:** if you have any chronic condition, take prescription medications, are pregnant/lactating, are under 18, have an eating-disorder history, or are over 70
- **During the fast:** for any warning sign above, or for symptom patterns you can’t interpret
- **After the fast:** to interpret repeat labs and design a sustainable maintenance plan

## Key Facts and Takeaways

1. **What it is:** A 21-day biblically inspired, ad libitum, strict-vegan-plus-extras dietary pattern; the foundational practice combines Daniel 1 (food list) and Daniel 10 (duration).
1. **What the evidence shows:** In peer-reviewed studies, 21 days produces an 8–9 mmHg drop in SBP, approximately 19% reduction in total cholesterol, approximately 23% reduction in LDL-C, meaningful drops in insulin resistance and CRP, and improvements in antioxidant biomarkers. HDL-C also falls approximately 14%.[^1][^2][^3]
1. **Who should be cautious:** People with insulin- or sulfonylurea-treated diabetes; eating-disorder history; pregnancy/lactation (without modification); young children; frail elders; advanced CKD or liver disease; high-volume athletes; active gallbladder disease.
1. **The 21-day habit myth:** Habits take an average of 66 days, not 21.[^4] Plan for what happens on day 22.
1. **The compliance reality:** In Bloomer’s 2010 study, compliance was 98.7 ± 0.2% — the food list is restrictive but participants find it tolerable because they can eat to satiety.[^1] Plan-ahead is the largest predictor of success.
1. **Spiritual + scientific:** The fast was conceived for spiritual purposes; its physical benefits are real but secondary. Honor both dimensions.
1. **The catalyst, not the cure:** The Daniel Fast is best understood as a structured introduction to a plant-forward eating pattern, not as an endpoint. Long-term outcomes depend on what happens after day 21.

## Caveats and Honest Limitations of the Evidence

- **Small sample sizes:** Most direct Daniel Fast studies have 30–50 participants without control groups. Findings are consistent but not yet replicated in large RCTs.
- **Largely from one research group:** The University of Memphis lab (Bloomer and colleagues) has produced the majority of direct Daniel Fast research. Independent replication would strengthen confidence; the Vaughn et al. (2018) church-based study is a welcome independent line.[^8]
- **Self-reported dietary intake:** All studies rely on participant food records, which over- or under-estimate intake.
- **Short duration:** Most studies are 21 days; only Bloomer & Toline’s (2014) 6-month follow-up extends beyond.[^16] Long-term cardiovascular event data are not available.
- **Healthy volunteer bias:** Participants are typically self-selected and motivated.
- **HDL reduction:** A documented adverse signal not yet fully resolved; the modified version mitigates it.[^7]
- **Pediatric data:** Essentially absent. Children should not follow the strict version without dietitian input.

-----

> *“Test your servants for ten days … then compare our appearance with that of the young men who eat the royal food, and treat your servants in accordance with what you see.”* — Daniel 1:12–13

The text invites a test. The science, so far, suggests Daniel’s instinct was sound.
