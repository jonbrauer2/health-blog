*A health information handout for patients, families, health coaches, and wellness ministry leaders. This material is educational and does not replace personalized medical advice. Decisions about medications, supplements, and treatments belong to you and your clinician.*

## A condition hiding in plain sight

Picture a woman in her late forties who keeps a water bottle within arm's reach at every moment of the day. Her eyes burn by mid-afternoon. She has unexplained cavities even though her dental hygiene is excellent. She is exhausted in a way that sleep does not fix, and her joints ache. She has been told it might be perimenopause, allergies, anxiety, or fibromyalgia. After three different specialists, a rheumatologist orders a blood test and a small biopsy of her lower lip. She finally has a name for what is happening: **Sjögren's syndrome**.

Sjögren's (pronounced "SHOW-grins") is a chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the body's moisture-producing glands — primarily the tear glands and salivary glands — while also causing widespread effects throughout the body.[^1][^2] It is one of the most common autoimmune diseases worldwide, yet it remains one of the most under-recognized. The Sjögren's Foundation estimates that as many as **four million Americans** are affected, though rigorous population-based studies suggest the true number of formally diagnosed cases is lower, with many people living for years without a diagnosis.[^3][^4][^5] Globally, primary Sjögren's affects roughly **0.01 to 0.05 percent** of the population, with around six to seven new cases per 100,000 person-years.[^4][^6]

> **Key Fact:** Sjögren's affects women about **nine times more often than men**, with average onset in the late forties to mid-fifties.[^4][^6]

Clinicians often distinguish two forms. **Primary Sjögren's** occurs on its own. **Secondary Sjögren's** develops alongside another autoimmune disease such as rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, or systemic sclerosis, though many experts now argue this distinction undersells the severity of "secondary" disease and prefer to simply call it Sjögren's disease.[^2][^7] Whatever the label, the experience is the same: a systemic condition that touches almost every organ system and that, on average, takes years to diagnose correctly.

> **Key Fact:** The average time from first symptoms to a confirmed diagnosis is approximately **2.8 years** and can stretch beyond a decade for those whose disease is initially mistaken for something else.[^8][^9]

This handout walks through what Sjögren's is, how it is diagnosed and treated, what daily life looks like, and where lifestyle, nutrition, and integrative approaches may help — always within the limits of what we currently know.

## What is actually going wrong inside the body

Sjögren's is an autoimmune disease, meaning the body's defense system has lost its ability to distinguish self from non-self. In Sjögren's, the immune system focuses much of its attention on the **exocrine glands** — the glands that secrete fluid onto surfaces such as the eyes, mouth, airways, skin, and vagina. White blood cells called **lymphocytes** infiltrate these glands and gradually damage them, reducing their ability to produce tears, saliva, and other secretions.[^1][^2]

Two lymphocyte types are central to the story. **T cells** drive much of the early inflammation by releasing signaling molecules that recruit more immune cells. **B cells** then become overactive, organizing themselves into clusters that resemble miniature lymph nodes inside the glands and producing autoantibodies — antibodies that target the body's own proteins.[^10][^11] This persistent B-cell hyperactivity is one reason Sjögren's carries a higher long-term risk of lymphoma than other autoimmune diseases.

The two best-known autoantibodies are **anti-Ro/SSA** and **anti-La/SSB**. Anti-Ro/SSA antibodies appear in roughly 50 to 70 percent of patients; anti-La/SSB appears in 30 to 60 percent.[^1][^2] These antibodies do more than mark the disease — they shape clinical decisions. Anti-Ro/SSA positivity is heavily weighted in modern diagnostic criteria, and during pregnancy these antibodies can cross the placenta and affect the developing baby's heart.

> **Key Fact:** Roughly **20 to 30 percent of people with Sjögren's are "seronegative"** — they lack the standard autoantibodies but have the disease on biopsy and clinical evaluation. Seronegative Sjögren's is real, frequently missed, and a leading reason for diagnostic delay.[^12][^13]

Researchers have also identified a striking biological fingerprint in many patients: an elevated **type I interferon signature**. Interferons are immune signaling proteins normally deployed against viruses. In Sjögren's, this antiviral machinery becomes chronically activated, fueling B-cell expansion and autoantibody production.[^14][^15][^16] Genetic susceptibility (such as HLA gene variants), hormonal factors, and possible viral triggers (Epstein-Barr virus among them) all appear to contribute.[^6]

Although Sjögren's begins in the moisture-producing glands, calling it a "dryness disease" misses the bigger picture. Inflammation, autoantibodies, and immune signaling spread well beyond the glands, which is why fatigue, joint pain, nerve problems, lung disease, kidney issues, and other systemic features are part of the same illness.[^2][^17]

## Symptoms reach far beyond dryness

The classic trio of Sjögren's symptoms — dry eyes, dry mouth, and parotid (cheek) gland swelling — is only the surface. **Keratoconjunctivitis sicca**, the medical term for dry eye disease in Sjögren's, affects up to 95 percent of patients and reflects both reduced tear production and inflammatory damage to the ocular surface.[^18] It can cause burning, grittiness, light sensitivity, blurred vision, and a paradoxical reflex tearing. **Xerostomia**, or dry mouth, affects more than 95 percent of patients and brings difficulty swallowing dry foods, altered taste, increased thirst, oral yeast infections, and, perhaps most importantly, dramatically accelerated tooth decay.[^1] Swelling of the parotid glands in front of the ears occurs in roughly 20 to 60 percent of patients and may come and go in flares.[^19]

What surprises many newly diagnosed patients is how much of the disease has nothing to do with dryness. **Fatigue** is reported by 70 to 80 percent of patients and is consistently rated as their most disabling symptom — more limiting than dryness, pain, or joint problems.[^20][^21][^22]

> **Key Fact:** In multiple studies, patients have ranked **fatigue and pain as more disabling than the dryness itself.** This is real, biologically grounded fatigue, not deconditioning or low motivation.[^20][^22]

Joint pain affects up to 60 percent of patients, usually as a non-erosive, symmetric arthritis that can resemble rheumatoid arthritis.[^2] **Peripheral neuropathy** — nerve damage in the hands, feet, or face — appears in up to 55 percent of patients when carefully assessed, and **small fiber neuropathy** is increasingly recognized as a common but easily missed cause of burning pain, tingling, and altered sensation.[^23][^24] Small fiber neuropathy is particularly important because routine nerve conduction studies often look normal; the diagnosis requires a special **skin punch biopsy** to count intraepidermal nerve fibers, a test not yet widely available.[^25]

The central nervous system can be involved as well. Cognitive complaints — often called "brain fog" — include trouble concentrating, word-finding difficulty, and slowed processing. Neuropsychological testing confirms that these problems are objectively measurable, not imagined.[^26][^27] Some patients experience white matter changes on MRI, optic nerve inflammation, or, rarely, conditions that resemble multiple sclerosis.

Other systemic features include **Raynaud's phenomenon** (cold-triggered color changes in fingers, affecting roughly 13 percent of patients);[^28] **interstitial lung disease**, which appears in around 20 percent of patients on careful evaluation and is a leading cause of premature mortality;[^29][^30] **renal tubular acidosis**, an electrolyte disturbance from kidney involvement that occurs in 10 to 40 percent;[^31][^32] **gastrointestinal symptoms** such as reflux, dysmotility, and autoimmune gastritis; and **vaginal dryness with sexual dysfunction**, which affects more than half of women with Sjögren's and is too often left out of clinical conversations.[^33][^34] Skin findings range from generalized dryness to **cutaneous vasculitis**, which is both painful and a marker of more aggressive disease.[^35]

A particularly important overlap involves **fibromyalgia**, which co-occurs in roughly 22 to 47 percent of Sjögren's patients depending on the cohort studied.[^36][^37] This overlap raises real diagnostic and treatment questions discussed later in this handout.

## Getting to a diagnosis

The current gold standard for classifying Sjögren's is the **2016 American College of Rheumatology / European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) criteria**, which uses a weighted scoring system. A score of four or more out of nine, in someone with symptoms suggestive of the disease, allows formal classification.[^38][^39] The five components are:

- A positive **anti-SSA/Ro antibody** test (3 points).
- A **minor salivary gland biopsy** showing focal lymphocytic sialadenitis with a focus score of 1 or higher (3 points).
- An abnormal **ocular staining score** (1 point).
- An abnormal **Schirmer's test** measuring tear production (1 point).
- A reduced **unstimulated whole salivary flow rate** (1 point).

The **minor lip salivary gland biopsy** deserves special attention. A pathologist examines tiny gland tissue from inside the lower lip and counts clusters of lymphocytes called "foci." This biopsy is especially important for seronegative patients, where it has roughly 93 percent sensitivity and 100 percent specificity.[^13][^40] Its limitations include needing an experienced pathologist, possible false negatives in early disease, and brief lip numbness afterward.

Additional blood work typically includes a complete blood count, antinuclear antibodies (ANA), rheumatoid factor (RF), inflammation markers (ESR and CRP), immunoglobulin levels, and complement (C3 and C4). Low C4 and the presence of cryoglobulins are particularly important because they predict more severe disease and higher lymphoma risk.[^41]

Diagnosis is often delayed because Sjögren's overlaps with conditions that are far more familiar to most clinicians: **menopause** (which also causes dryness), **fibromyalgia and chronic fatigue syndrome** (which share the exhaustion and pain), **anxiety and depression** (which can be both consequences and misattributed causes), and **other autoimmune diseases** such as lupus and rheumatoid arthritis.[^9][^42] Patients previously misdiagnosed with another autoimmune disease wait an average of six to seven years before Sjögren's is recognized.[^9]

> **Key Fact:** Some patients wait **more than a decade** for a Sjögren's diagnosis, particularly if they are seronegative or were initially labeled with fibromyalgia, menopause-related symptoms, or anxiety.[^8][^9]

Because the disease touches so many organ systems, care typically involves a team: a **rheumatologist** as the central coordinator, an **ophthalmologist** with dry eye expertise, a **dentist** familiar with high-caries-risk patients, and, depending on symptoms, a neurologist, pulmonologist, nephrologist, or oral medicine specialist.[^38][^43]

## What conventional medicine offers

There is currently no cure for Sjögren's and no medication formally approved by regulators to modify the underlying disease, though several promising therapies are in late-stage trials.[^17][^43] Treatment focuses on relieving symptoms, protecting organs, and managing systemic flares.

**For dry eyes**, the foundation is preservative-free artificial tears used as often as needed, supplemented by prescription anti-inflammatory drops such as **cyclosporine** (Restasis) and **lifitegrast** (Xiidra).[^44][^45][^46] When tears drain away too quickly, **punctal plugs** — tiny silicone or collagen stoppers placed in the tear ducts — can help. For severe cases, **autologous serum eye drops** made from a patient's own blood provide growth factors and nutrients that promote ocular surface healing.[^47][^48] **Moisture chamber glasses**, which look like wraparound sunglasses, reduce tear evaporation and are particularly helpful in dry climates or near forced-air heating.

**For dry mouth**, saliva substitutes (sprays, gels, and lozenges) provide short-term relief. Two prescription medications — **pilocarpine** and **cevimeline** — stimulate remaining salivary tissue to produce more saliva, with the most common side effect being sweating.[^49][^50] Dental care is foundational: most clinicians recommend cleanings every three to four months, daily prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste, and periodic in-office fluoride varnish.[^51]

> **Key Fact:** People with Sjögren's are universally classified as **high caries risk**. Without aggressive prevention, severe tooth decay can develop rapidly, even with excellent brushing.[^51]

For systemic disease, **hydroxychloroquine** (Plaquenil) is the most widely prescribed medication. It dampens immune signaling and has decades of use in lupus and rheumatoid arthritis. The picture in Sjögren's, however, is more complicated. The **JOQUER trial**, a well-designed 2014 randomized study, did not find significant improvement in dryness, pain, or fatigue at 24 weeks compared with placebo.[^52] Despite this, hydroxychloroquine remains a first-line option for joint pain, fatigue, and rashes because later analyses show it lowers inflammatory markers and turns down the interferon signature, and many patients report meaningful benefit over longer time frames.[^53] If your clinician has prescribed it, the JOQUER findings are not a reason to stop without discussion — they reflect a nuanced evidence picture, not a verdict against the medication.

Other systemic options include **methotrexate** and low-dose corticosteroids for inflammatory arthritis, and stronger immunosuppressants such as **mycophenolate** or **azathioprine** for severe organ involvement.[^43] **Rituximab**, a B-cell-depleting biologic, showed promise in early studies but did not meet primary endpoints in the larger **TEARS** and **TRACTISS** trials.[^54][^55] It is still used off-label for severe systemic complications such as vasculitis, cryoglobulinemia, and serious neuropathy.

The most exciting recent developments involve newer biologics. **Ianalumab** (targeting BAFF, a key B-cell survival factor) met its endpoints in a phase 2b trial and received Breakthrough Therapy designation in early 2026.[^56] **Iscalimab** and **dazodalibep** (targeting the CD40/CD40L pathway) have both shown significant improvements in disease activity and symptoms in recent trials.[^57][^58] **JAK inhibitors** such as baricitinib, which block interferon signaling, are in earlier-stage study.[^59] These therapies offer real hope that the first disease-modifying treatment for Sjögren's may arrive within the next few years.

For **fatigue**, the news is harder. No medication is approved for Sjögren's fatigue, and the drugs most commonly tried — hydroxychloroquine and rituximab — did not improve fatigue in their pivotal trials.[^52][^55] Patients deserve honest acknowledgment of this gap rather than dismissal, and the most consistent help currently comes from exercise, sleep optimization, pacing, and treating co-occurring problems such as sleep apnea, depression, and thyroid disease.[^60][^61]

## Lifestyle and nutritional approaches

Although lifestyle interventions cannot cure Sjögren's, they can meaningfully shape daily function, quality of life, and possibly disease trajectory. The strength of evidence varies, and most of the most rigorous data come from related autoimmune diseases rather than Sjögren's specifically.

**Hydration and environmental control** are simple, low-risk, and often underused. Sipping water throughout the day, using a bedside humidifier (especially in winter), and avoiding drying environments such as overheated rooms, ceiling fans aimed at the face, and prolonged screen time without blinking breaks all help. Many patients find that scheduling "moisture rest" — fifteen minutes with closed eyes or a warm compress — reduces afternoon eye fatigue.

A **Mediterranean-style eating pattern** — rich in olive oil, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, fish, nuts, and herbs, with limited red meat and ultra-processed foods — has the strongest general evidence among dietary patterns for autoimmune disease.[^62][^63] Direct Sjögren's-specific data remain limited, but mechanistic studies suggest the pattern reduces systemic inflammation, supports the gut microbiome, and provides plentiful polyphenols and omega-3 fats that influence immune function.[^64]

**Omega-3 fatty acids** are worth a closer look, particularly for dry eye symptoms. Earlier smaller studies suggested benefit, but the large, well-designed **DREAM study** found that 3,000 mg per day of fish-derived omega-3s for one year did not significantly improve dry eye outcomes more than olive oil placebo.[^65][^66]

> **Key Fact:** The most rigorous study to date, the **DREAM trial**, did not find omega-3 supplements superior to olive oil for general dry eye disease. Sjögren's-specific evidence remains limited, and whether certain subgroups benefit is still an open question. Discuss any supplement decisions with your clinician.[^65]

**Vitamin D deficiency** is common in Sjögren's, and lower levels correlate with more severe disease, including peripheral neuropathy and lymphoma risk in some cohorts.[^67][^68] Correcting documented deficiency makes sense for bone health and possibly for immune modulation; routine high-dose supplementation without testing does not. This is a good example of where blood work and clinician guidance matter more than internet-sourced dosing.

**Sugar deserves special mention** because of the dental implications. Frequent sugar exposure, sticky carbohydrates, and acidic drinks (including sparkling water consumed throughout the day) can rapidly accelerate decay in a Sjögren's mouth. **Xylitol** — a sugar alcohol found in some gums and lozenges — appears to reduce caries-causing bacteria and stimulate saliva, with modest supporting evidence.[^69][^70]

The **gut microbiome** is emerging as a fascinating area of research. Multiple studies show that people with Sjögren's have reduced microbial diversity and overrepresentation of inflammation-associated bacteria, with the degree of dysbiosis correlating with disease activity.[^71][^72][^73] Mouse experiments have even shown that transferring gut microbes from Sjögren's patients into healthy mice can induce dry eye and reduce regulatory T cells.[^74] **Probiotics** for dry eye and Sjögren's are being studied, with small trials showing modest symptom improvement; the evidence is promising but not yet sufficient to recommend specific products.[^75]

**Exercise** has some of the most consistent evidence for symptom improvement. Multiple small trials in Sjögren's, including supervised walking programs, have shown improved aerobic capacity and reduced fatigue.[^60][^61][^76] **Pacing** — the practice of distributing activity to avoid the "boom and bust" cycle — is widely recommended in chronic fatigue conditions and applies well to Sjögren's. Low-impact movement such as walking, swimming, tai chi, yoga, and recumbent cycling tends to be tolerated better than high-intensity workouts during flares.

**Sleep** is often disturbed in Sjögren's, both from nighttime dryness and from underlying disease biology. Poor sleep correlates with worse patient-reported symptom scores.[^77] Simple sleep hygiene — consistent schedule, dark room, bedside water, and a humidifier — is reasonable, low-risk, and frequently underused.

**Mind-body practices** including mindfulness meditation, gentle yoga, and breathing exercises show modest but real effects on inflammatory markers, mood, and quality of life across chronic illnesses.[^78][^79] Stress and immune function move in both directions: chronic stress amplifies inflammatory signaling, and chronic illness amplifies stress. Breaking that loop in any way — therapy, prayer or contemplative practice, social connection, time in nature — appears to be genuinely health-promoting.

**Smoking** deserves a clear word. The epidemiology of smoking in Sjögren's is paradoxical (current smokers are sometimes diagnosed less often, possibly because smoking suppresses inflammatory markers used in diagnosis), but smoking unambiguously worsens dry eye, accelerates dental decay, increases cardiovascular risk, and raises lymphoma risk.[^80] Cessation is clearly indicated.

## Functional and integrative perspectives

Functional and integrative medicine offers several frameworks that some Sjögren's patients find helpful. These approaches deserve fair-minded discussion: in most cases, mainstream rheumatology evidence is limited, but mechanistic rationale and patient experience together justify continued exploration. None of these should replace standard care, and any meaningful change should involve your clinician.

The **intestinal permeability** ("leaky gut") hypothesis proposes that increased passage of bacterial and food-derived molecules across a more porous gut lining drives systemic immune activation. Researchers including Alessio Fasano have built a substantial mechanistic case for this concept in autoimmune disease broadly, and small Sjögren's-specific studies have linked increased permeability with altered regulatory T-cell function.[^81][^82] Mainstream rheumatology has not yet adopted "leaky gut" as a clinical diagnosis, and commercial tests have validity concerns, but research in this area is moving quickly.

**Food sensitivities and elimination diets** are a frequent topic. Sjögren's overlaps meaningfully with celiac disease, with up to 15 percent of Sjögren's patients showing celiac in some cohorts.[^83] A celiac workup makes sense for anyone with persistent gastrointestinal symptoms, unexplained anemia, or a family history. Beyond celiac, some patients report symptom improvement on a gluten-free diet even without a celiac diagnosis, possibly reflecting non-celiac gluten sensitivity, though rigorous evidence is limited. Restrictive elimination diets carry real risks, including nutritional deficiency and disordered eating patterns such as orthorexia.[^84][^85] If you and your clinician want to try a structured elimination, working with a registered dietitian is the safest path.

**Nightshade restriction** (avoiding tomatoes, peppers, potatoes, and eggplant) circulates widely in autoimmune communities. There are no clinical trials supporting this in Sjögren's or autoimmune disease generally. Some individuals genuinely feel better; others do not. It is a personal experiment, not a prescription.

**Environmental exposures** — heavy metals, mold, persistent chemicals — are part of many integrative medicine evaluations. Mainstream Sjögren's-specific evidence is very limited, though autoimmunity in general has documented associations with certain occupational exposures (silica, organic solvents) in conditions like scleroderma and lupus.[^86] Patient experience often runs ahead of research here. A reasonable position is to take exposures seriously without claiming more certainty than exists.

**Low-dose naltrexone (LDN)** is naltrexone, an opioid-receptor blocker, prescribed at roughly one-tenth of its standard dose (typically 1.5 to 4.5 mg). At low doses, it appears to briefly block opioid receptors, upregulating endogenous endorphins, and to dampen microglial inflammation via TLR4 antagonism.[^87] The strongest evidence is in fibromyalgia, where smaller trials showed pain reduction, although the largest randomized trial in 2024 did not meet its primary endpoint.[^88][^89] There is no Sjögren's-specific clinical trial. LDN is used off-label, must be compounded by a specialty pharmacy, and requires a prescribing clinician — but it is generally low-risk and is a reasonable conversation to have if pain and fatigue dominate the clinical picture.

**Acupuncture** has been studied for both dry mouth and chronic pain. Sjögren's-specific evidence for xerostomia is mixed; Cochrane and other systematic reviews have found insufficient evidence to recommend it strongly for dry mouth.[^90][^91] For chronic non-Sjögren's pain, however, a large individual-patient-data meta-analysis showed acupuncture is superior to sham and to no acupuncture.[^92] Risk is low. It is reasonable to discuss with a clinician, especially for musculoskeletal pain or treatment-resistant dry mouth.

## Where the field is still arguing

Honest patient education includes acknowledging where the science is genuinely contested.

The **hydroxychloroquine debate** is the most prominent example. JOQUER showed no significant short-term benefit, yet most rheumatologists still prescribe it, citing longer time horizons, biomarker improvements, and a favorable safety profile.[^52][^53] Patients should understand this complexity rather than feel betrayed by either prescribing it or stopping it. A thoughtful clinician will weigh symptoms, biomarkers, and individual response.

The **fibromyalgia overlap** is another active controversy. Roughly a third of Sjögren's patients meet criteria for fibromyalgia, and these patients often have markedly elevated patient-reported symptom scores without correspondingly elevated objective disease activity.[^36][^93] Some researchers interpret this as **central sensitization** — a nervous system that amplifies pain and fatigue signals — while others argue much of what looks like fibromyalgia in Sjögren's is actually underdiagnosed **small fiber neuropathy**, which is a peripheral nerve problem requiring different treatment.[^94] Both are likely true in different patients. The treatment implications are significant: central sensitization responds to medications like SNRIs, gabapentinoids, exercise, and cognitive behavioral therapy, whereas immune-mediated small fiber neuropathy may respond to immunomodulation. Neither story dismisses the patient experience.

**Small fiber neuropathy** itself is underrecognized. It produces burning, tingling, and pain that often does not show up on standard nerve conduction studies. The better test, **skin punch biopsy** for intraepidermal nerve fiber density, is not universally available.[^25][^95] Many patients with these symptoms have been told their nerves are fine when they are not.

Finally, the **diagnostic label problem** causes real harm. Patients with classic symptoms but seronegative blood work, or who fall just below formal classification thresholds, are sometimes told they don't "really" have Sjögren's. The 2016 ACR/EULAR criteria were designed for research, not strict clinical diagnosis, and many practicing rheumatologists treat patients who do not meet every criterion but clearly have the disease.[^38][^39] If you are in this gray zone, finding a rheumatologist familiar with Sjögren's matters.

## Living well with Sjögren's

Daily life with Sjögren's is largely about routines that protect the body's most vulnerable tissues and conserve energy.

**Eye care** generally means preservative-free artificial tears used proactively (not just when symptoms peak), warm compresses or eyelid hygiene for any associated meibomian gland dysfunction, scheduled screen breaks following the 20-20-20 rule (look 20 feet away for 20 seconds every 20 minutes), and moisture chamber glasses in dry or windy environments. Sunglasses outdoors reduce both light sensitivity and tear evaporation.

**Oral health** is non-negotiable. Most Sjögren's specialists recommend dental cleanings every three to four months, prescription-strength fluoride toothpaste daily, in-office fluoride varnish at each cleaning, xylitol gum or lozenges, and constant low-level hydration during meals to help with chewing and swallowing. Avoid sipping sugary or acidic drinks throughout the day, as the constant low-level acid bath is highly damaging to enamel in a low-saliva mouth.[^51]

**Fatigue management** is one of the most important skills to develop. The cornerstone is **pacing**: treating energy as a budget, planning demanding activities for higher-energy parts of the day, and building in scheduled rest before reaching exhaustion. Distinguishing fatigue causes matters too — Sjögren's fatigue, poor sleep, depression, anemia, hypothyroidism, and medication side effects are all treatable and require different responses. Tracking patterns in a simple notebook or app for a few weeks often reveals useful trends.

**Flare management** involves recognizing your personal triggers (often infection, stress, sleep loss, sun, or seasonal change), having a "flare plan" worked out with your rheumatologist in advance, and giving yourself permission to scale back commitments without guilt. Many patients find a flare protocol that includes earlier nights, simpler food, gentle movement, increased moisture support, and contact with their clinician if symptoms cross specific thresholds.

**Advocating for yourself in medical settings** is, unfortunately, a needed skill. Keep a brief symptom log, bring a written list of concerns to appointments, ask for explanations of test results in plain language, and seek a clinician who takes Sjögren's seriously. The **Sjögren's Foundation** (sjogrens.org) maintains patient resources, including provider directories and educational materials that many patients find empowering.

**Relationships and disclosure** are personal. Sjögren's is an "invisible illness" — most people you encounter will not realize you are sick — which creates both freedom and isolation. Some patients find it helpful to explain their condition simply ("My immune system is attacking my moisture glands, and I get very fatigued") to people who need to understand. Others prefer privacy. There is no single right approach. Connection with others who have the disease, whether through support groups or online communities, is one of the most consistent things patients describe as meaningful.

## When to seek prompt medical attention

Most Sjögren's symptoms are chronic and managed in the rheumatology clinic, but certain developments warrant prompt evaluation and should not be brushed aside.

Seek urgent care for new or worsening **vision changes** or significant **eye pain**, which may signal corneal ulceration or optic nerve involvement. **Unexplained lymph node swelling** that persists, especially with hardening or rapid growth, requires evaluation because of lymphoma risk. **Unexplained weight loss, fevers, or night sweats** ("B symptoms") together suggest a need for prompt workup. New **weakness, numbness, or other neurological symptoms** can reflect vasculitis, central nervous system involvement, or significant neuropathy. **Chest pain or shortness of breath** may indicate lung or heart involvement. **Kidney problems** — including unexplained low potassium, kidney stones, or muscle weakness from electrolyte imbalance — can reflect renal tubular acidosis.

> **Key Fact:** Sjögren's carries an approximately **5- to 10-fold higher lifetime risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma** compared with the general population, with a lifetime absolute risk in the range of 5 to 10 percent. The elevated risk is real, but most patients will never develop lymphoma. Persistent parotid swelling, low complement (C4), cryoglobulinemia, and palpable purpura are the strongest warning signs and warrant close follow-up.[^96][^97]

**Pregnancy** in women with anti-Ro/SSA antibodies requires specialist care. These antibodies can cross the placenta and, rarely, cause congenital heart block in the developing baby.

> **Key Fact:** The risk of congenital heart block in babies of anti-Ro/SSA-positive mothers is approximately **2 percent in a first pregnancy and 12 to 25 percent in subsequent pregnancies after an affected child.** Women with these antibodies should be followed by maternal-fetal medicine with serial fetal echocardiograms between 16 and 26 weeks of gestation. Hydroxychloroquine taken during pregnancy reduces recurrence risk significantly.[^98][^99][^100]

## A note on scope: how health coaches and wellness leaders can help

For health coaches, wellness ministry leaders, family, and friends supporting someone with Sjögren's, your role is meaningful and clearly defined. You can help with **education** — sharing accurate information about the disease, dispelling the idea that it is "just dry eyes," and validating the realness of fatigue and brain fog. You can support **lifestyle implementation** — helping someone build sustainable habits around hydration, sleep, exercise pacing, stress management, and nutrition. You can provide **emotional accompaniment** — listening without minimizing, showing up consistently, and helping the person feel seen.

What you cannot do is diagnose, interpret lab results, recommend specific therapeutic supplement doses, or advise on starting, stopping, or changing medications. Discussions of elimination diets, low-dose naltrexone, vitamin D dosing, or biologic therapies are conversations to encourage between the person and their clinician. The phrase "this is worth discussing with your rheumatologist" is one of the most valuable things you can offer.

The rheumatologist relationship is foundational. Coaching and lifestyle support build on top of medical care; they do not replace it. When in doubt, default to encouraging professional evaluation, especially for any of the warning signs listed above.

## A closing reflection

Living with Sjögren's is a long conversation between body, mind, and community. The disease will not be cured by perfect eating or by willpower, and people who try to muscle through it tend to crash. But neither is the situation hopeless: better treatments are arriving, the science is advancing rapidly, and the small daily practices of hydration, movement, rest, connection, and self-compassion genuinely shape how this disease feels day to day.

If you are newly diagnosed, give yourself time to grieve and to learn. If you have been managing this for years, know that the field is finally beginning to catch up to your experience. Whether you are the person living with Sjögren's or someone walking alongside them, the most important truth is also the simplest: you are not imagining it, you are not alone, and care that treats the whole person — body, mind, relationships, and meaning — is care worth seeking.
