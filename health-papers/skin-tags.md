## 1. Introduction — More Than Skin Deep

That soft little bump on your neck may be saying more than you think. Skin tags — those tiny, fleshy outgrowths that show up on necks, eyelids, armpits, and skin folds — are extraordinarily common, affecting roughly half of all adults at some point in life.[^1][^2] Most of us treat them as a cosmetic nuisance. Pull at them. Cover them up. Ask the dermatologist to snip them off. And then forget about them.

But a growing body of research suggests these unassuming growths are quietly telling a story about what is happening *inside* your body — particularly inside your blood vessels, your liver, and the cells that respond to insulin. A 2019 case-control study from Eastern India found that people with skin tags had an eleven-fold higher risk of metabolic syndrome compared with controls.[^3] A separate Egyptian study found that 75% of patients with skin tags had ultrasound evidence of fatty liver disease.[^4] And in children, skin tags now travel together with rising rates of obesity, prediabetes, and abnormal cholesterol.[^5]

None of this means you should panic at every little bump. Skin tags are benign. They are not cancer, and they almost never become cancer. But they may be a friendly tap on the shoulder from your skin — a signal worth paying attention to, especially if you have more than a few. This handout is an invitation to curiosity, not alarm. We will explore what skin tags actually are, what the research shows about why they form, what your options are if you want to remove them, and — most importantly — what you can do about the conditions inside your body that may be driving them in the first place.

> **KEY FACT:** A skin tag (medical name *acrochordon* or *fibroepithelial polyp*) is a small, soft, benign growth made of loose collagen, blood vessels, and overlying skin. Roughly 50 to 60% of adults develop at least one in their lifetime, and prevalence rises steeply after age 40.[^1][^2]

## 2. What Skin Tags Actually Are

Under a microscope, a skin tag is a beautifully simple thing. A core of loosely arranged collagen fibers, threaded with dilated tiny blood vessels and lymphatic channels, is wrapped in a thin, slightly thickened layer of normal-looking skin.[^1][^2] There are no hair follicles inside, no sweat glands, no oil glands — just connective tissue covered by skin, attached to the body by a narrow stalk. That stalk is what gives skin tags their distinctive "hanging" shape.

Researchers used to think skin tags formed because of breakdown of elastic fibers. That theory has not held up. A 2021 study in the *Journal of Investigative Dermatology* found that the outer skin layer of acrochordons often carries somatic mutations in growth-related genes including FGFR3, HRAS, KRAS, PIK3CA, and EGFR — the same genes mutated in another common benign growth, the seborrheic keratosis.[^6] In other words, skin tags appear to begin as small clonal patches of skin cells that have received a subtle "grow" signal, with the underlying connective tissue likely responding to that signal and to mechanical stretching.

Skin tags love friction zones. They cluster on the sides of the neck, in the armpits, in the groin folds, beneath the breasts, on the eyelids, and along the waistband.[^1][^7] They start small (often just a millimeter or two), sometimes grow into longer, dangling "filiform" tags, and occasionally enlarge into baggy fibromas a centimeter or more across.[^1] Once in a while a skin tag twists on its stalk, loses its blood supply, turns dark, and falls off on its own — a harmless event that can feel alarming if you do not know what is happening.

A quick word about look-alikes. Several other benign growths can resemble skin tags, and a few worrisome ones can pretend to be skin tags too. Moles are usually flat or dome-shaped, evenly pigmented, and made of clusters of melanin-producing cells. Warts are rough and cauliflower-like, caused by HPV. Seborrheic keratoses look "stuck on," waxy, and may be brown or black. Molluscum contagiosum lesions are small, pearly, and have a central dimple. Neurofibromas are soft and "buttonhole" inward when pressed, and can be a clue to a genetic condition called neurofibromatosis. Most of the time, you and your clinician can tell the difference easily. But certain features should always send a growth to a dermatologist for inspection — we will get to those in Section 7.

## 3. Who Gets Skin Tags — and Why It Matters

Skin tags are common in everyone, but they are not random. The research points to a small set of factors that strongly raise your chances, and a longer list of factors that may matter but are still being sorted out.

The single strongest and most replicated association is with insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes. Across studies from Brazil, India, Turkey, Egypt, Iran, and Mexico, people with multiple skin tags consistently show higher fasting insulin, higher HOMA-IR (a calculated measure of insulin resistance), higher fasting glucose, and higher rates of frank diabetes than matched controls.[^8][^9][^10][^11] In one Brazilian study, having a HOMA-IR above 3.8 was associated with an odds ratio of 7.5 for having multiple skin tags.[^8] In a Turkish study of nondiabetic men with eight or more neck tags, 77% had laboratory evidence of hyperinsulinemia or impaired glucose handling.[^11] In a large Indian case-control study, more than half of patients with skin tags had diabetes, compared with about one in ten controls.[^9]

Obesity and elevated BMI travel closely with this. The more body fat someone carries — especially around the waist — the more likely they are to have skin tags, and often the more they have.[^12][^13] In a study of children and teens, almost 8 in 10 overweight or obese youth had skin tags, and the number of tags correlated tightly with serum insulin levels.[^14]

Metabolic syndrome — the cluster of high blood pressure, high triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol, abdominal obesity, and elevated blood sugar — shows up disproportionately in people with skin tags. The Eastern India case-control study found an 11-fold higher risk; an Egyptian study found that 71% of skin tag patients met criteria for metabolic syndrome.[^3][^15] Hypertriglyceridemia and low HDL cholesterol are the two metabolic-syndrome components most consistently linked to skin tags across studies.[^16]

Hormonal changes also matter. Skin tags often appear or worsen during pregnancy, when estrogen, progesterone, leptin, and IGF-1 levels surge and friction across stretching skin increases.[^17] Women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) — a condition where insulin resistance and elevated androgens drive ovulation problems and skin changes — frequently develop skin tags alongside acanthosis nigricans, the velvety brown discoloration that often appears on the back of the neck.[^1] Rare thyroid hormone receptor mutations have also been linked to skin tags, suggesting that thyroid signaling plays at least a minor role in some cases.[^18]

The HPV (human papillomavirus) connection is one of the most interesting and most contested questions in this literature. Studies that tested skin tag tissue using PCR have found HPV DNA in anywhere from 48% to 88% of samples, mostly low-risk types 6 and 11.[^19][^20][^21][^22] A few studies have detected high-risk HPV types as well.[^23] On the surface that sounds compelling. But a 2004 landmark study showed that surface contamination accounts for much of the HPV "positivity" in benign skin growths — when researchers stripped the surface before biopsy, detection rates dropped dramatically.[^24] Most current reviews place HPV in the "possible cofactor" category: present in many skin tags, but not yet proven to cause them. This is reasonable scientific disagreement, not settled fact.

Finally, there are the simpler explanations: age (prevalence climbs steadily through the 50s and 60s), family history (skin tags often cluster in relatives), and friction itself, which is why skin tags so reliably show up wherever skin rubs on skin, on clothing, or on jewelry.[^1][^7] Each of these probably matters, layered on top of the metabolic background.

> **KEY FACT:** The link between skin tags and insulin resistance is well-established. The link with HPV is contested — possibly a cofactor, but not proven to be causal. The link with friction and obesity is well-established. Knowing which signals are strong and which are tentative helps you respond proportionately.

## 4. Skin Tags as a Metabolic Signal: What the Research Shows

If skin tags were just cosmetic, we would not need to talk much about them. The reason they matter clinically is that they appear to be a window — a small, visible flag — pointing toward what is happening at the cellular level with insulin.

Here is the proposed mechanism, distilled from a decade of dermatology and endocrinology research.[^25][^26] When the body becomes resistant to insulin (often because of excess visceral fat, chronic refined-carbohydrate intake, inactivity, poor sleep, or genetic predisposition), the pancreas compensates by making more insulin. Circulating insulin levels climb. Insulin is structurally similar to insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), and at high concentrations it can cross-react with IGF-1 receptors on skin cells. Skin tags themselves show significantly elevated IGF-1 and IGF-2 receptor expression compared with normal skin, along with about a sevenfold increase in tissue IGF-1.[^25][^26] The result is gentle but persistent stimulation of keratinocyte and fibroblast growth — exactly the cells that, with enough friction and time, accumulate into a skin tag.

There is also a partial dose-response. Larger numbers of skin tags tend to track with higher insulin levels and more components of metabolic syndrome.[^14][^11] One Turkish study found that having eight or more neck tags was a clinically useful threshold for suspecting impaired glucose handling.[^11] A Mexican study found correlations between tag count and serum insulin independent of BMI, age, and sex.[^14] Not every study replicates this dose-response — a small Turkish cohort did not see it[^25] — and some researchers have questioned whether skin tags alone discriminate prediabetes well in already-overweight populations.[^1] So skin tags should be thought of as one clue among many, not as a standalone diagnostic test.

A natural follow-up question: if I remove my skin tags, do my insulin and blood sugar improve? The honest answer is no. The literature uniformly treats skin tags as a marker of underlying metabolism, not a cause. Removal addresses the visible bump; it does not touch the systemic biology driving the bump.[^1][^25] That is why new skin tags often appear elsewhere after old ones are removed — the upstream conditions have not changed.

Many integrative and functional medicine practitioners take this connection one step further in their clinical work. They view multiple skin tags as a reason to look closely at fasting insulin, A1C, triglyceride-to-HDL ratio, liver enzymes, and inflammatory markers — even in patients whose fasting glucose looks "normal." This view aligns with the controlled research in pointing toward early metabolic screening; where it goes further than the controlled research is in claiming that aggressive lifestyle interventions can shrink or prevent skin tags. That specific dermatologic outcome has not been tested in a clinical trial. What has been clearly demonstrated is that the same lifestyle approaches reduce diabetes risk, improve insulin sensitivity, and lead to regression of acanthosis nigricans (a closely related insulin-driven skin condition) after weight loss.[^27]

So a sensible posture is this: if you have multiple skin tags — especially if you also have a waistline you are working on, a family history of diabetes, fatigue after meals, or other clues — a conversation with your primary care clinician about basic metabolic bloodwork is reasonable. Useful tests typically include fasting glucose, hemoglobin A1C, a fasting lipid panel, and (in many integrative practices) fasting insulin. This is a conversation, not a self-diagnosis.

> **KEY FACT:** Across more than a dozen case-control studies, people with multiple skin tags have shown anywhere from roughly two to eleven times the risk of metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, or insulin resistance compared with controls. Skin tags themselves are harmless. What they may be pointing toward is not.[^3][^9][^11][^15]

## 5. Removal Options: What You Should Know

Whether you remove a skin tag is largely up to you. If a tag bleeds, snags on clothing or jewelry, rubs painfully, or simply bothers you, removal is reasonable. If it is sitting quietly and not causing trouble, leaving it alone is equally reasonable. What is not reasonable is treating a lesion you cannot identify with certainty.

### Conventional Medical Removal

A clinician has several effective tools, and the head-to-head trials we have so far point toward the simpler options.

**Snip excision** — cutting the tag off at its base with fine scissors, often after a tiny dab of numbing cream — is the workhorse procedure. A 2025 randomized controlled trial published in *Archives of Dermatological Research* compared scissor excision with 532 nm laser on more than 1,200 tags across 68 patients. Snip excision achieved a 92.6% response rate versus 84.2% for laser, with lower pain scores, fewer pigmentation problems, and clear patient preference (63% favored snip excision; only 19% preferred laser).[^28] Bleeding from a small tag base usually stops with light pressure or a quick touch of aluminum chloride.

**Cryotherapy** — freezing the tag with liquid nitrogen — is fast and effective, particularly for small tags. A 2024 randomized trial in *Health Science Reports* compared cryotherapy with electrosurgery and found electrosurgery actually produced higher patient and physician satisfaction for skin tags.[^29] Cryotherapy can cause temporary pain, blistering, and — importantly — lighter patches of skin (hypopigmentation), a risk that is more pronounced and longer-lasting in people with darker skin tones.

**Electrosurgery and electrocautery** burn the tag off using a fine current, with immediate hemostasis. **Ligation** — tying off the blood supply with a sterile thread — causes the tag to wither and fall off in about a week. **Laser removal** (CO2, KTP, picosecond Nd:YAG) is sometimes used for cosmetic precision but, as noted, does not outperform snip excision in head-to-head trials and tends to cost more.[^28][^30]

In the United States, most insurance plans — including Medicare — treat skin tag removal as cosmetic unless the lesion is bleeding, painful, recurrently inflamed, restricting vision, or clinically suspicious for cancer.[^31][^32] Out-of-pocket costs typically run roughly $150–$600 per session depending on the number of lesions and the technique used.

### At-Home and Over-the-Counter Removal

The internet is full of suggestions for removing skin tags at home. The actual evidence is much thinner than the confidence with which these methods are promoted.

**Dental floss or string ligation** — tying off the tag base with thread — works on the same principle as in-office ligation, and small tags do sometimes fall off. There are no peer-reviewed efficacy trials of self-applied home ligation in humans. Documented risks include infection from non-sterile materials, incomplete tying that swells the tag without resolving it, hemorrhage from larger tags, and scarring.

**TagBand-style ligation rings** are marketed widely. A formal published RCT is still pending; for now, the marketing exceeds the data.

**Over-the-counter "freezing" pen devices** have at least one published RCT: in 56 volunteers, a nitrous-oxide pen cleared 64.3% of small skin tags (2–5 mm, on the neck and breast) over up to three treatments.[^33] That study was sponsored by the device manufacturer and was limited to small tags in non-sensitive sites. Adverse effects included redness and occasional hyperpigmentation.

**Topical remedies — apple cider vinegar, tea tree oil, salicylic acid wart removers, garlic, banana peel, iodine, vitamin E.** Here is the honest summary of where the evidence sits.

There are no peer-reviewed efficacy studies of apple cider vinegar (ACV) for skin tag removal. What does exist is a small case-report literature documenting chemical burns from ACV used under occlusion on the skin. Published cases include an 8-year-old with full-thickness epidermal necrosis after overnight ACV application, a 14-year-old with nasal erosions from internet-recommended ACV nevus removal, and an 11-year-old whose ACV use produced histologic changes that mimicked melanoma on biopsy.[^34][^35][^36][^37]

Tea tree oil has documented antimicrobial properties but no published efficacy data for acrochordon removal.[^38] It is also the essential oil most frequently implicated in published cases of allergic contact dermatitis, with patch-test positivity rates of 0.1–3.5% in routine clinics.[^39] Severe reactions including spreading dermatitis have been reported.[^40]

Salicylic acid–based wart removers like Compound W are not designed for skin tags. Published case reports document partial-thickness chemical burns from off-label use, including two sisters who developed elbow burns after using a plantar wart remover on non-wart skin.[^41]

In August 2022, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration issued warning letters to several companies and to Amazon for selling unapproved mole and skin-tag removal products. The FDA's position is unambiguous: no over-the-counter or prescription drug is approved for skin tag removal in the United States, and reported harms include permanent skin injuries, infections, scarring, and delayed cancer diagnosis when malignant lesions were treated as benign.[^42]

This does not mean home remedies are inherently dangerous. It means the evidence base for them is genuinely limited, and the documented harms — while not common — are real. A person of sound judgment can decide that a small, clearly identified skin tag in a low-risk area is worth a careful at-home attempt; the same person should be reluctant to try the same approach on the eyelid, on a lesion that looks unusual, or on the kind of growth a doctor has not laid eyes on.

> **KEY FACT:** Skip home removal when a growth is near the eye, near the genitals, larger than a pencil eraser, bleeding spontaneously, changing color, or anything other than clearly and obviously a typical skin tag. The FDA has explicitly warned that no OTC product is approved for skin-tag removal and that misuse has led to scarring, infection, and delayed diagnosis of skin cancer.[^42]

## 6. Lifestyle, Nutrition, and Functional Approaches That May Help

This is the most actionable section of this handout. While no clinical trial has yet measured whether lifestyle change shrinks skin tags or prevents new ones from forming, the evidence is robust that lifestyle change improves the underlying biology — insulin resistance, central fat, inflammation, hormonal balance — that drives skin tag formation in the first place. And we know that a related insulin-driven skin condition, acanthosis nigricans, regresses meaningfully with weight loss and metabolic improvement.[^27] So while we cannot promise your skin tags will vanish, we can say with confidence that the things that improve your metabolism are good for the rest of you.

### Insulin Sensitivity and Blood Sugar Regulation

The dietary patterns with the strongest, most consistent evidence for improving insulin sensitivity share a common thread: more plants, less refined sugar and starch, more fiber, and intentional moderation of overall energy intake.

The Mediterranean dietary pattern — rich in vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil, and fish — reduced new-onset type 2 diabetes by about 52% in the PREDIMED trial and by 31% in PREDIMED-Plus, even in people who were not actively trying to lose weight.[^43][^44] The DiRECT trial showed that a structured, low-calorie dietary approach achieved type 2 diabetes remission in 46% of participants at one year, with weight loss explaining most of the effect.[^45] Five-year follow-up data confirmed that remission can be durable when weight loss is maintained.[^46] Low-glycemic-index eating patterns modestly but reliably lower hemoglobin A1C, and higher intake of soluble fiber (oats, beans, lentils, psyllium) improves both fasting glucose and HOMA-IR.[^47][^48] Sugar-sweetened beverages, by contrast, raise type 2 diabetes risk by about 26% in the highest-intake group versus the lowest, in pooled meta-analyses.[^49] All of these findings sit firmly in the well-established evidence category.

Time-restricted eating — confining food intake to a window of 6 to 10 hours per day — is an interesting newer area. Small trials show meaningful improvements in insulin sensitivity, blood pressure, and oxidative stress when the eating window is placed earlier in the day, sometimes even without weight loss.[^50][^51] A 12-week RCT in overweight adults with type 2 diabetes found a 10-hour eating window reduced A1C by 1.5%.[^52] But a 2020 trial of 16:8 restriction in a broader population (TREAT) found minimal metabolic benefit and raised modest concerns about lean-mass loss.[^53] This is emerging evidence — promising but not yet settled.

Physical activity earns a place on every short list of insulin-sensitizing strategies. Aerobic exercise, resistance training, and high-intensity interval work all improve glucose handling through somewhat different mechanisms. A meta-analysis of HIIT studies found significant reductions in insulin resistance and modest A1C improvement.[^54] Resistance training has comparably strong effects, partly because skeletal muscle is the body's largest disposer of dietary glucose.[^55] Even light movement matters: in a randomized crossover trial, walking just 10 minutes after meals lowered postprandial blood sugar by about 12% — and by 22% after the evening meal.[^56] Interrupting prolonged sitting every 20 minutes with two minutes of light walking reduced post-meal glucose and insulin spikes by roughly a quarter.[^57][^58] You do not need a gym membership to benefit; you need a pair of shoes and a willingness to move.

### Weight Management

The Diabetes Prevention Program showed that losing about 7% of body weight, combined with 150 minutes of weekly activity, reduced new-onset type 2 diabetes by 58% over nearly three years.[^59] A pooled analysis found that 5–10% weight loss yielded 43% prediabetes remission and a 73% relative reduction in T2D risk.[^60] In the Look AHEAD trial, modest weight loss meaningfully improved A1C, blood pressure, and cholesterol.[^61] We do not have a trial telling us whether this degree of weight loss reduces new skin tag formation in adults. We do have observational evidence in adolescents and prospective cohort data after bariatric surgery showing that insulin-driven skin changes — particularly acanthosis nigricans — regress as metabolism improves.[^27] That makes biological reduction of new skin tags plausible, even if not yet proven.

> **KEY FACT:** A weight loss of just 5 to 7% of body weight, sustained over the long term, can reduce the risk of progressing from prediabetes to diabetes by more than half.[^59] You do not need to reach an "ideal" weight to gain meaningful metabolic benefit.

### Hormonal Health

For women with PCOS, the same insulin-sensitizing patterns described above are foundational. Inositol (myo-inositol, often combined with d-chiro-inositol) has emerging evidence from meta-analyses for improving insulin resistance, restoring menstrual regularity, and supporting ovulation in PCOS.[^62][^63] Adequate intake of selenium, iodine, and protein supports thyroid hormone production; any concerns about thyroid function should be discussed with a clinician and confirmed with labs rather than self-treated.

### Gut Health and the Microbiome

The microbiome is one of the most exciting and most overhyped areas of metabolic medicine. What we know with reasonable confidence is that the microbes in your gut influence how you handle insulin, store fat, and regulate inflammation, partly through short-chain fatty acids like butyrate that are produced when bacteria ferment dietary fiber.[^64] A first-in-human pilot trial of pasteurized *Akkermansia muciniphila* improved insulin sensitivity by about 29% in overweight adults.[^65] A randomized trial of high fermented-food intake — yogurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, kombucha — increased microbiome diversity and reduced 19 inflammatory proteins over ten weeks.[^66] No study to date has tested microbiome interventions specifically for skin tags. What can be said honestly: eating more fiber and including fermented foods is low-risk and likely beneficial; targeted probiotic capsules for metabolic health are emerging evidence at best.

### Nutrient Considerations

Several nutrients play meaningful roles in insulin signaling. Specific dosing is a conversation for your clinician; what follows is a summary of where the evidence sits, not a prescription.

Magnesium deficiency is common in people with insulin resistance, and supplementation improves HOMA-IR meaningfully in those who are deficient.[^67] Berberine, an alkaloid from plants like *Berberis*, has shown impressive A1C reductions in meta-analyses — sometimes comparable to metformin — though the underlying trial quality varies.[^68] Zinc supplementation modestly improves fasting glucose and A1C, with the largest effects in zinc-deficient populations.[^69] Chromium has been studied for decades; meta-analyses are mixed, and the evidence is best described as contested.[^70] Vitamin D is genuinely contested too: a large NIH-funded trial (D2d) found no significant reduction in diabetes incidence from supplementation in a generally replete population, though there may be benefit for people who are deficient at baseline.[^71][^72]

> **KEY FACT:** Better-studied supportive nutrients for insulin signaling include magnesium and zinc, where deficiency is meaningfully linked to insulin resistance. Berberine has emerging clinical-trial evidence. Chromium and vitamin D show contested or limited effects in people who are not deficient. Supplements are tools to be discussed with a clinician — not substitutes for the dietary and movement patterns that do the heavy lifting.

### Stress, Cortisol, and Sleep

Chronic stress is not just an emotional inconvenience — it is a metabolic event. The HPA (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal) axis releases cortisol, which over time promotes visceral fat storage, insulin resistance, and inflammation.[^73][^74] The relationship runs in both directions: stress drives metabolic dysfunction, and metabolic dysfunction worsens the physical experience of stress.

Sleep matters enormously and is frequently neglected. A controlled study of healthy young men showed that just one week of sleeping five hours per night reduced insulin sensitivity by 11 to 20%.[^75] Habitual short sleep is associated with about a 28% increase in type 2 diabetes risk, along with increased visceral fat and altered appetite hormones.[^76] Mindfulness-based interventions show meaningful effects on A1C in people with type 2 diabetes — typically reductions of 0.3 to 0.75%, comparable to the early phase of some glucose-lowering medications.[^77] All of this falls in the well-established category for mechanism and emerging-to-well-established for intervention.

> **KEY FACT:** Short sleep, chronic stress, and elevated cortisol each independently worsen insulin sensitivity. Getting 7 to 9 hours of consistent sleep and finding a regular practice that downshifts your nervous system — prayer, meditation, slow walks, journaling, breathing exercises, time with people you love — may matter as much for your metabolism as your diet.[^75][^76]

## 7. When to See a Doctor — Red Flags and Referral Triggers

A normal skin tag is soft, skin-colored or slightly darker, pedunculated (on a stalk), and stays the same size for months or years. When a growth does not match that description, the right move is to have it examined.

Be especially attentive if you notice rapid growth over weeks, irregular or jagged borders, multiple colors within a single lesion (especially dark brown, black, or blue), bleeding without obvious trauma, ulceration or a non-healing surface, persistent itching or pain, or appearance in a sun-exposed area, the sole of the foot, or under a nail. Several skin cancers can masquerade as skin tags, including basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and the particularly dangerous amelanotic melanoma — a melanoma that lacks pigment and can look like a benign pink growth. A 2024 systematic review reported misdiagnosis rates of acral amelanotic melanoma as high as 87.5%, often delayed by more than a year because the lesion was mistaken for a benign growth or a non-healing injury.[^78] When in doubt, biopsy is the answer. Skin tag removal with pathology examination costs almost nothing extra and provides certainty.

Pay extra attention if the number of skin tags is climbing quickly, if tags are appearing in unusual locations, or if they appear alongside other clues of metabolic dysfunction — a velvety darkening on the back of the neck (acanthosis nigricans), unexplained weight changes, frequent thirst or urination, recurring yeast infections, or a family history of early diabetes or heart disease. These deserve a metabolic workup rather than just a cosmetic snip.

Skin tags in children deserve a closer look than they often get. A 2024 retrospective cohort study in *Pediatric Dermatology* compared 55 children with acrochordons to NHANES controls and found significantly higher rates of obesity, type 2 diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension in the skin tag group.[^5] Multiple skin tags on a child are not a cosmetic problem to be ignored — they are an invitation to a thoughtful pediatric metabolic conversation. In rare cases, multiple "skin-tag-like" growths in a child can be the presenting sign of nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (Gorlin syndrome), in which seemingly benign growths are actually small basal cell carcinomas.[^79]

Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome sometimes gets mentioned in this context, but the connection is largely misunderstood. The skin lesions of Birt-Hogg-Dubé are fibrofolliculomas — small hair-follicle hamartomas that can look superficially like skin tags but are biologically distinct. True Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome is rare and is associated with lung cysts and an elevated risk of kidney tumors.[^80] If a clinician suspects this pattern based on the appearance of multiple distinctive facial papules, family history of pneumothorax, or renal findings, genetic testing for the FLCN gene is the next step. This is uncommon and is not a reason for the average person with a few skin tags to worry.

> **KEY FACT:** Not every skin-colored bump is a skin tag. Rapid growth, dark or mixed colors, bleeding without trauma, an ulcer that does not heal, or appearance in a sun-exposed area should be examined by a dermatologist or your primary care clinician. Diagnostic certainty is cheap; missed melanoma is not.

## 8. Skin Tags During Pregnancy

Pregnancy is a fertile time for skin tags, quite literally. Rising estrogen and progesterone, elevated leptin and human placental growth hormone, increased IGF-1, and the simple mechanical stretching of expanding skin folds all push the skin toward tag formation.[^17] Most new pregnancy skin tags appear in the second and third trimesters and cluster in classic friction zones — the sides of the neck, under the breasts, in the armpits, and along the bra line.

The good news: many pregnancy-associated skin tags regress at least partially in the months after delivery as hormones renormalize. The realistic news: some persist indefinitely. There are no large, well-conducted studies that give us a reliable percentage either way.[^17]

If a pregnancy skin tag is genuinely bothersome — repeatedly catching on clothing, bleeding, or causing distress — it is safest to wait until after delivery for elective removal, unless your obstetric and dermatologic providers agree there is a good reason to act sooner. Cryotherapy, snip excision, and electrosurgery are not generally considered dangerous in pregnancy, but they are also rarely urgent, and most clinicians prefer to defer cosmetic procedures. Home removal of any kind during pregnancy is best avoided, both because of infection risk and because of the chemical exposure concerns associated with topical products of uncertain composition.

If anything about a growth changes during pregnancy — rapid enlargement, color change, bleeding — that is exactly the time to have it looked at rather than wait.

> **KEY FACT:** Pregnancy commonly brings new skin tags, driven by hormonal and mechanical changes. Many regress postpartum, but some persist. Defer elective removal until after delivery when possible, and avoid home removal during pregnancy.[^17]

## 9. The Emotional and Social Dimension

There is a temptation, in a clinical handout, to wave away the cosmetic concern and focus only on the metabolic implications. That would be a mistake. Visible skin conditions — even small, benign ones — carry a real psychological weight. A 13-country European study of nearly 5,000 dermatology outpatients found significantly elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and even suicidal ideation among people with visible skin conditions compared to controls.[^81] These are not trivial feelings.

If your skin tags bother you because of how they look, that concern is legitimate. It is allowed to be both a medical conversation ("what does this mean about my metabolism?") and a cosmetic one ("I do not love how this looks under my collar"). Both motivations are valid reasons to talk to your clinician about removal — though insurance coverage, as we noted, typically requires the medical justification.

It also helps to keep some perspective. Half of the adults around you have at least one skin tag. They are part of being a person with skin that has lived a little. Loving photographs of older relatives often show necks dotted with tiny tags, and we hardly notice. The cultural pressure to have flawless skin is not a moral standard; it is a marketing strategy. You do not owe anyone perfect skin. You do owe yourself attention to what your skin might be telling you about the body underneath.

A brief reflection, for anyone for whom it resonates: the body you are in — including its bumps, freckles, tags, and scars — is worthy of care. Caring for it is not vanity; it is stewardship. The point is not to chase a body without flaws. The point is to be paying enough attention to notice when something changes, and to respond with curiosity and kindness rather than anxiety or neglect.

When you talk with a clinician about skin tags, do not let yourself be dismissed with "they're just cosmetic." That answer may be true for the specific lesion. But if you have many, or if they appeared quickly, or if you have other reasons to suspect a metabolic story, it is fair to ask: *Given what we know about the link between skin tags and insulin resistance, would it make sense to check my fasting glucose, A1C, fasting insulin, and a lipid panel?* That is a reasonable question, and a good clinician will welcome it.

## 10. Putting It Together: A Practical Path Forward

If you read nothing else in this handout, read this. Here is what to actually do with what you now know.

Do not panic, but do not ignore them either. A skin tag is not cancer. It is not contagious. It is not an emergency. But it may be one of the body's gentler ways of telling you that the conditions inside your blood vessels and cells are worth paying attention to — especially if you have several, if they are multiplying, or if you have other reasons to suspect metabolic strain.

Use them as a conversation starter. At your next primary care visit, mention them. Specifically, ask whether basic metabolic screening would be appropriate. For most adults with multiple skin tags, that conversation can reasonably include a fasting glucose, hemoglobin A1C, fasting lipid panel, and — in many integrative or functional medicine practices — a fasting insulin level. These are inexpensive, widely available tests that may catch insulin resistance years before frank diabetes develops.

Treat the soil, not just the weeds. Removing a skin tag deals with the visible bump but leaves the underlying metabolic environment unchanged. The patterns that improve insulin sensitivity — moving your body daily, walking after meals, eating more plants and fewer refined sugars, sleeping seven to nine hours, managing stress, achieving even modest weight loss — are the same patterns that reduce your risk of diabetes, heart disease, and many cancers. They are good for your skin, your heart, your mood, and your future.

Choose removal methods thoughtfully. If you want a tag gone, an in-office snip, freeze, or cautery procedure is supported by good evidence, fast, and safe. Home ligation of a small, clearly identifiable tag in a low-risk area is a reasonable option for some people. Home application of corrosive substances — apple cider vinegar, salicylic acid wart removers, essential oils — is poorly supported by evidence and carries documented risks of burns, scarring, allergy, and missed cancer diagnosis. Skip home removal entirely when a lesion is near the eye, on the genitals, large, changing, or in any way uncertain.

Follow up if anything changes. A growth that suddenly enlarges, bleeds without provocation, develops color, or refuses to heal is not behaving like a skin tag and deserves to be examined. Catching skin cancer early is one of the highest-leverage things you can do for your long-term health.

The most encouraging thing about skin tags may be the one we said at the beginning: they are tiny, common, harmless, and visible. They are an easy signal for the body to send, and an easy one for you to notice. If you choose to read them as an invitation — to a better night's sleep, a more thoughtful plate of food, a walk after dinner, a conversation with your doctor, or simply a more attentive relationship with your own body — they have served you well. That little bump on your neck may turn out to have been a small, gentle, friendly nudge in the direction of a longer, healthier, more vibrant life.
