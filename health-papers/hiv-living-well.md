*A health education handout for wellness coaches and the people they serve*

When Maria was diagnosed with HIV at 34, the first thing she pictured was a hospital bed and a short, frightening future — the images she had absorbed from television decades earlier. Her clinician gently corrected that picture. Today, Maria takes one pill a day, her virus is undetectable, she cannot pass HIV to her partner, and her doctors expect her to live into her eighties. Her story is now the rule, not the exception. HIV in the 2020s is, for most people who can reach and stay in care, a manageable long-term health condition — closer to well-controlled diabetes or high blood pressure than to the crisis of the 1980s. This handout is built around that reality. It is not about merely surviving HIV. It is about living well with it: understanding the medicine that makes long life possible, and understanding how nutrition, movement, rest, connection, and meaning can help a person thrive alongside that medicine — never instead of it.

## What Is HIV? (And What It Is Not)

HIV stands for human immunodeficiency virus. It is a virus that attacks the body’s immune system — specifically a type of white blood cell called the CD4 cell (sometimes called a “T-helper cell”), which acts like a field commander coordinating the body’s defenses. When HIV enters the body, it uses these very cells to make copies of itself, gradually reducing their numbers if left untreated. Two numbers describe how a person is doing. The **CD4 count** measures how many of these immune commanders remain; a healthy adult typically has somewhere between 500 and 1,500 cells per cubic millimeter of blood. The **viral load** measures how much HIV is circulating in the blood. The goal of treatment is to push the viral load down so low that standard tests cannot detect it — “undetectable” — while the CD4 count recovers and stays strong [^1].

Without treatment, HIV slowly erodes the immune system over years. When the CD4 count falls very low (below 200) or certain serious infections appear, a person is said to have AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome) — the most advanced stage of HIV infection [^1]. It is important to understand that HIV and AIDS are not the same thing: AIDS is what can happen when HIV goes untreated for a long time. With modern treatment, the overwhelming majority of people living with HIV never progress to AIDS.

Understanding how HIV spreads is the antidote to a great deal of unnecessary fear. HIV is transmitted through specific body fluids — blood, semen, pre-seminal fluid, rectal fluids, vaginal fluids, and breast milk — and only when those fluids enter the bloodstream or reach a mucous membrane. In the United States, the most common routes are sex without a condom or prevention medication and the sharing of drug-injection equipment [^2]. What deserves equal emphasis is how HIV is **not** spread. HIV cannot be transmitted by hugging, shaking hands, or closed-mouth (“social”) kissing; it is not spread through sharing toilets, dishes, drinking glasses, or office furniture; and it is not carried by mosquitoes, ticks, or other insects. The virus does not survive long outside the human body and cannot reproduce outside a human host. Saliva, sweat, and tears do not transmit HIV [^2]. These facts matter not only for accuracy but for dignity: the myth that HIV is “catching” through everyday contact has fueled decades of stigma that, as we will see, causes real and measurable harm.

How common is HIV? In the United States, more than 1.2 million people are estimated to be living with HIV, with nearly 39,000 new diagnoses in 2024 [^3]. Globally, an estimated 40.8 million people were living with HIV at the end of 2024 [^4]. The epidemic has changed dramatically since its early years. The first cases of what would become known as AIDS were reported in the U.S. in June of 1981, and through the 1980s and early 1990s an HIV diagnosis was widely regarded as a death sentence [^5]. The arrival of effective combination therapy in 1996 transformed the disease. Globally, AIDS-related deaths have been reduced by 70 percent since their peak in 2004 — from about 2.1 million deaths in 2004 to around 630,000 in 2024  [^4]. The arc of this story — from terror to a treatable chronic condition — is one of the most remarkable achievements in modern medicine.

> ### Key Facts at a Glance
> 
> - **More than 1.2 million** people are living with HIV in the United States, and **40.8 million** worldwide as of 2024 [^3][^4].
> - A person who starts treatment with a healthy immune system can expect a life expectancy approaching that of the general population [^6].
> - **Undetectable = Untransmittable:** a person with a durably undetectable viral load cannot transmit HIV to sexual partners [^7].
> - HIV is **not** spread by hugging, kissing, sharing dishes, toilet seats, or insect bites [^2].
> - In 2024, **77 percent** of people living with HIV worldwide — about 31.6 million of 40.8 million — were accessing treatment, and AIDS-related deaths have dropped **70 percent** since their 2004 peak [^4].
> - About **8,000** people in the U.S. die each year with HIV-related illness as a contributing cause — a fraction of the toll of the epidemic’s early years [^5].

## Modern HIV Treatment: The Antiretroviral Revolution

The cornerstone of living well with HIV is antiretroviral therapy, almost always shortened to **ART**. ART is a combination of medications that interrupt the virus’s life cycle at different stages — some block the enzyme HIV uses to copy its genetic material, others block the enzyme it uses to insert itself into human cells, and still others stop the virus from assembling new copies. Because the medicines attack from several directions at once, the virus cannot easily adapt to escape them. ART does not eliminate HIV from the body, but it suppresses the virus so thoroughly that the immune system can recover and the person can stay healthy [^6].

One of the most important scientific findings of the past decade carries a simple, hopeful name: **U=U, or Undetectable = Untransmittable.** A large body of research has established that a person living with HIV who takes ART and maintains an undetectable viral load does not transmit HIV to their sexual partners [^7]. This is not a hopeful guess; it rests on major studies including HPTN 052, PARTNER, PARTNER 2, and Opposites Attract. In the PARTNER 2 study of gay male couples, partners had condomless sex an estimated 76,991 times over 1,596 couple-years;  combined with the Opposites Attract data, the research covered roughly 89,000 condomless sex acts with zero linked HIV transmissions when the partner with HIV was virally suppressed [^7][^8]. The medical significance is enormous: treatment is also prevention. The social significance is just as large. For people who have lived under the shadow of being told their bodies were dangerous to others, U=U is liberating — it restores the possibility of intimacy, relationships, and parenthood without fear.

Treatment today is far simpler than it once was. Many people manage HIV with a **single tablet taken once a day** that contains a complete regimen. For those who prefer not to take daily pills, a **long-acting injectable** option became available when the FDA approved the combination of cabotegravir and rilpivirine in 2021; it can be given as injections as infrequently as every two months  and has been shown in clinical trials to be as effective as daily oral therapy at maintaining viral suppression [^9]. Long-acting options can be especially valuable for people who struggle with daily dosing because of stigma, unstable housing, or other barriers.

The same medicines that treat HIV also prevent it. **Treatment as Prevention (TasP)** is the U=U principle applied at the population level — when more people are treated and suppressed, fewer new infections occur. **PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis)** is medication taken by people who do not have HIV but are at increased risk; according to the CDC, when taken as prescribed PrEP reduces the risk of getting HIV from sex by about 99 percent and from injection drug use by at least 74 percent  [^10]. **PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis)** is a short course of medication started within 72 hours of a possible exposure that can prevent infection from taking hold  [^2]. Coaches do not prescribe any of these, but knowing they exist helps a coach point people toward the right conversations with a clinician.

**Adherence** — taking medication consistently — is the engine that makes all of this work. When ART is taken regularly, viral suppression is durable and the immune system stays protected. When doses are missed, the virus can rebound and, over time, develop resistance to the drugs. Barriers to adherence are rarely about willpower; they include cost, side effects, mental health challenges, substance use, unstable housing, transportation, and stigma [^9]. This is precisely where a wellness coach can be enormously helpful — not by managing the medication, but by supporting the daily routines, problem-solving, and encouragement that make consistent adherence easier.

A persistent and damaging myth is that ART is harsh and debilitating. That belief is rooted in the early era of HIV treatment, when the available drugs could cause significant side effects. Modern regimens are dramatically better tolerated. Most people on contemporary ART experience few or no day-to-day side effects, and when side effects do occur they are often mild or manageable with a change of regimen [^9]. The contrast between historical and current ART is one of the most important — and most under-appreciated — facts a coach can help communicate, because fear of “toxic drugs” leads some people to delay or avoid the very treatment that would let them thrive.

This brings us to a point that must be stated plainly. Some people, drawn by the appeal of “natural” healing, wonder whether they can manage HIV through diet, supplements, or other lifestyle measures **instead of** ART, or whether it is safe to delay starting treatment. The evidence here is not ambiguous, and this is an area where clear caution is warranted rather than open-ended exploration. The landmark SMART study enrolled 5,472 participants at 318 sites across 33 countries  and tested a strategy of interrupting ART based on CD4 counts. The trial was stopped early because the group that interrupted treatment had opportunistic disease or death at a rate of 3.3 events per 100 person-years, compared with 1.3 in the continuous-therapy group — a hazard ratio of 2.6 (95% confidence interval, 1.9 to 3.7)  [^11]. Interrupting or stopping ART is associated with a heightened risk of opportunistic infections, advanced HIV disease, and death — including in people who later resume treatment  [^4]. Lifestyle measures are genuinely valuable, but they are a complement to ART, never a substitute for it. No diet, herb, supplement, or detox protocol suppresses HIV. A coach’s role is to enthusiastically support healthy living **and** to reinforce, every time it comes up, that staying on ART is the foundation everything else is built on.

## Lifestyle, Nutrition, and Functional Health in HIV

If ART is the foundation, lifestyle is the structure built upon it — the set of daily choices that influence energy, mood, inflammation, long-term disease risk, and overall quality of life. None of what follows replaces medical care; all of it can meaningfully enhance well-being for a person whose HIV is being treated.

### Nutrition

Good nutrition matters for everyone, but it carries particular weight for people living with HIV. Even with well-controlled HIV, the body sustains a low level of chronic immune activation and inflammation, and nutrition is one lever that influences that process. A growing body of research supports the role of a balanced, nutrient-rich diet in supporting immune function, reducing inflammation, and managing the metabolic side effects that can accompany long-term therapy [^12]. Reviews of nutrition in HIV highlight dietary patterns such as the Mediterranean diet and components like omega-3 fatty acids and adequate vitamins as supportive of overall health in people living with HIV  [^12]. A whole-food, largely plant-forward eating pattern — rich in vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and healthy fats — is a reasonable, evidence-aligned foundation that also helps address the cardiovascular and metabolic risks that matter so much in HIV.

It helps to apply honest **spectrum framing** to specific supplements, distinguishing strong evidence from emerging, limited, or insufficient evidence — and always pairing that with a coaching-appropriate response:

- **Vitamin D:** There is reasonably strong evidence that vitamin D deficiency is common among people living with HIV and relevant to both bone health and immune regulation [^13]. The coaching-appropriate response is to encourage people to have their levels checked by their provider and to address deficiency under medical guidance rather than guessing at doses.
- **Zinc and selenium:** Evidence is emerging but mixed. Deficiencies in these minerals have been associated with poorer outcomes, and some supplementation studies suggest benefit, but findings on hard outcomes like mortality remain inconsistent  [^13]. The coaching-appropriate response is to emphasize getting these minerals from food first and to discuss any supplementation with a clinician.
- **Omega-3 fatty acids:** Evidence is emerging and reasonably encouraging, particularly for managing the elevated triglycerides that some people on ART experience [^12]. The coaching-appropriate response is to encourage omega-3-rich foods (such as fatty fish, walnuts, and flax) and to leave decisions about concentrated supplements to the care team.

A point of particular importance is **food safety**. Anyone whose immune system is not fully restored — and especially anyone with a low CD4 count — is more vulnerable to foodborne illness. Practical guidance includes thoroughly cooking meats and eggs, washing produce, avoiding unpasteurized dairy and juices, and practicing careful kitchen hygiene. This is concrete, non-medical, genuinely useful coaching territory.

Finally, the conversation around **weight and body composition** has shifted. In the early epidemic, the dominant nutritional fear was HIV-associated wasting — dangerous loss of weight and lean tissue. Wasting still occurs, particularly in advanced or untreated disease, and early studies found that mortality correlated with loss of lean tissue  [^14]. But in the era of effective ART, the more common concerns for many people have shifted toward weight gain, metabolic syndrome, and the cardiovascular risks that accompany them [^12]. Nutrition coaching today often focuses on the same goals that benefit the general population: stable healthy weight, good metabolic health, and cardiovascular protection.

### Physical Activity

Exercise is one of the best-supported lifestyle interventions in HIV. A systematic review of exercise training in people living with HIV on ART found that aerobic exercise was associated with improvements in aerobic capacity, quality of life, and depressive symptoms, while resistance training improved strength; combined aerobic and resistance training improved fitness, strength, physical functioning, quality of life, and self-efficacy  [^15]. Some studies even documented improvements in CD4 count and viral load measures, though the most consistent and reliable benefits are in fitness, strength, body composition, mental health, and quality of life  [^15].

**Resistance (strength) training** deserves particular emphasis because it gets particular attention in the HIV literature. Meta-analyses of combined aerobic and resistance programs show meaningful gains in muscle strength and aerobic capacity  [^16]. Building and preserving muscle directly counters two concerns relevant to HIV: loss of lean tissue and the frailty that can accompany accelerated aging. For most people living with HIV, a program that includes both cardiovascular activity and progressive strength work two or more times per week is safe and beneficial — though anyone starting from a sedentary baseline or with other health conditions should check in with their provider first.

**Fatigue** is a common and under-recognized challenge in HIV, with many causes. Rather than pushing through exhaustion, the strategy of **pacing** — balancing activity with rest, breaking tasks into manageable pieces, and building gradually — helps people stay active without crashing. A coach can be a steady partner in finding the right rhythm.

### Sleep and Stress

Sleep disturbance is strikingly common among people living with HIV. Systematic reviews report sleep problems in 40 to 100 percent of people living with HIV depending on how they are measured, compared with roughly 13 to 30 percent in the general population,  with one meta-analysis estimating self-reported sleep disturbance at about 58 percent [^17]. The reasons are layered: the immune response to the virus and its effects on the central nervous system, psychological factors such as depression and anxiety, side effects of some medications, and the stress of living with a stigmatized condition  [^17]. Poor sleep is not a trivial complaint; it worsens fatigue, mood, and metabolic health, and it can undermine the daily routines that support adherence. Sleep hygiene coaching — consistent schedules, a wind-down routine, limiting screens and caffeine, and a comfortable sleep environment — is squarely within a coach’s scope, and cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia is an effective, non-drug first-line treatment a provider can arrange  [^17].

Chronic stress is more than an emotional burden; it has biological consequences. Sustained stress elevates cortisol and feeds the cycle of inflammation and immune activation that drives many of the long-term complications of HIV  [^18]. **Mind-body approaches** — mindfulness, meditation, and yoga — are an area of emerging evidence. In one small randomized controlled trial of 48 adults, an eight-week mindfulness-based stress reduction program appeared to buffer the decline in CD4 T-cell counts seen in a comparison group, independent of medication use  [^19]. Network meta-analyses of exercise interventions have included yoga and tai chi, though the methodological quality of those particular studies has been rated lower than for aerobic and resistance training  [^16]. The honest framing is this: mind-body practices show emerging and promising evidence for stress, mood, and quality of life, with weaker evidence for direct effects on the immune system. The coaching-appropriate response is enthusiastic support for these low-risk, well-tolerated practices for their well-being benefits, without overpromising effects on the virus itself.

### Substance Use and HIV

This is a topic to handle factually and without shame. **Alcohol** affects HIV care on two fronts: heavy or problematic drinking is consistently associated with poorer ART adherence, and alcohol places additional strain on the liver — a particular concern for people who also have hepatitis co-infection [^20]. Moderation, or support for cutting back, is a constructive coaching conversation.

**Smoking** is, remarkably, one of the single greatest threats to the health of a person whose HIV is well treated. In a large Danish cohort study, a 35-year-old living with HIV who smoked had a median life expectancy of 62.6 years, compared with 78.4 years for a never-smoker with HIV; the analysis estimated 12.3 years of life lost in association with smoking versus 5.1 years lost in association with HIV itself,  and smokers had roughly a fourfold higher mortality rate than nonsmokers  [^21]. Across these and other cohorts, smokers had far higher rates of death from cardiovascular disease and non-AIDS cancers,  with lung cancer occurring almost exclusively among smokers  [^21]. The encouraging corollary is that quitting works, and cardiovascular risk declines with time since quitting. Smoking cessation may be the highest-impact lifestyle change a coach can support for a person living with HIV who smokes.

Finally, some **recreational drugs can interact with ART**, and some interactions are dangerous — certain party drugs can reach toxic levels when combined with specific HIV medications [^20]. The coaching posture here is non-judgmental and practical: encourage honesty with the medical and pharmacy team so that interactions can be checked, and refer rather than advise on the specifics.

### Gut Health and the Microbiome

The gut is a frontier of HIV research. Early in infection, HIV damages the immune tissue of the gut and disturbs the balance of the trillions of microbes that live there — a state called dysbiosis. This disruption is thought to allow microbial products to “leak” across the gut wall, fueling the chronic inflammation that persists even when the virus is well suppressed  [^22]. Researchers are actively studying whether modifying the microbiome — through diet, prebiotics, probiotics, or other means — can reduce that inflammation and improve health. There are intriguing signals: some studies of probiotics report reductions in immune activation and improvements in gut barrier function,  and some evidence suggests that certain modern ART regimens (integrase-inhibitor-based) support better recovery of the gut microbiome  [^22]. But the honest verdict is that the evidence on probiotic and prebiotic interventions in HIV remains inconclusive and mixed, with systematic reviews citing insufficient evidence and risk of bias  [^22]. This is genuinely frontier science. The coaching-appropriate response is to support gut-friendly whole-food eating (which is beneficial regardless) while being honest that probiotic supplements are not yet proven to change HIV outcomes, and that any product should be discussed with the care team — particularly for anyone with a compromised immune system, since live-organism supplements carry theoretical risks.

## Controversial and Commonly Discussed Topics

Coaches will encounter strong claims, hopeful rumors, and genuine confusion. The goal of this section is navigation, not adjudication — to give coaches honest, sourced framing so they can help people think clearly.

### “Curing” HIV — What Does the Science Actually Show?

There is real, thrilling science here, and there is also dangerous misinformation, and the two must be kept apart. A small number of people have been functionally cured of HIV. The first was the **“Berlin Patient,”** and the second widely confirmed case was the **“London Patient,”** who remained free of detectable replicating virus long after stopping ART [^23]. Both received stem-cell transplants from donors with a rare genetic mutation (CCR5-delta32) that makes immune cells resistant to HIV  — but crucially, both received those transplants to treat life-threatening cancers, not to treat HIV [^23]. Additional cases have since been reported, including the Düsseldorf, New York, City of Hope, Geneva, and a second Berlin patient  [^24].

These cases are scientifically priceless, but they are **not a treatment anyone can ask for.** Stem-cell transplantation is a dangerous, sometimes life-threatening procedure reserved for people who need it for cancer; it would be unethical and far too risky to perform on an otherwise healthy person with well-controlled HIV  [^23]. Researchers are pursuing more practical paths toward a cure, including gene-editing approaches (such as engineering a person’s own cells to resist HIV), “shock and kill” strategies to flush out the hidden viral reservoir,  and **broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs)** — powerful antibodies that can neutralize many strains of HIV and are being studied for treatment, prevention, and cure  [^25]. It is essential to distinguish a **functional cure** (long-term control of the virus without daily medication) from a **sterilizing cure** (complete eradication of the virus from the body). Neither is available as a routine treatment today, and the latter remains elusive because HIV hides in a long-lived reservoir of cells [^25].

This is the backdrop against which a coach must address claims that HIV can be “cured” by a special diet, herbal protocol, detox, or alkaline regimen. **There is no evidence that any such approach cures, eliminates, or controls HIV.** Acting on these claims carries serious, documented danger: people who stop or delay ART to pursue them risk viral rebound, immune collapse, opportunistic infection, drug resistance, and death [^11]. The appropriate coaching response is compassionate and unambiguous — honor the hope behind the question, never shame the person, and clearly redirect to the truth that ART is the only proven way to control HIV, while a cure remains a subject of legitimate ongoing research, not a product for sale.

### Cannabis and HIV

Cannabis is widely used by people living with HIV, often to manage appetite, nausea, and pain. The evidence is genuinely mixed across these uses. For **neuropathic pain** (nerve pain), several controlled trials and meta-analyses support a real benefit, and HIV-related nerve pain is one of the better-studied medical uses of cannabis  [^26]. For **appetite and nausea**, there is substantial evidence that THC stimulates appetite and reduces nausea,  though much of the foundational weight-gain research dates to the 1990s and it is not certain that people on modern ART experience the same benefit  [^27]. Applying spectrum framing: there is reasonably good evidence for benefit in neuropathic pain and for appetite/nausea symptom relief; there are real **risks**, including respiratory harm from smoking, cannabis hyperemesis syndrome with heavy use, possible memory effects, and the fact that potential drug-drug interactions between cannabinoids and ART exist but are not robustly characterized; and there remains much that is **unknown** [^27]. The coaching-appropriate response is to avoid both cheerleading and shaming, to encourage anyone using cannabis medicinally to tell their provider and pharmacist so interactions can be checked, and to defer dosing and product decisions to clinicians, particularly where medical cannabis is legally available and clinically supervised.

### Herbal Supplements and ART Interactions

Here directness is not just appropriate — it is a safety obligation. **St. John’s Wort**, an herbal product widely sold for low mood, is one of the most dangerous things a person on ART can take. St. John’s Wort is a potent inducer of a liver enzyme system called CYP3A4 and of a transport protein called P-glycoprotein (P-gp).  In plain terms, it speeds up the body’s breakdown and removal of many medications — including many antiretrovirals — so that drug levels in the blood fall sharply [^28]. In a clinical study, St. John’s Wort reduced blood levels of the HIV medication indinavir by an average of 57 percent, with some individuals seeing reductions as large as 99 percent  [^28]. A drop of that magnitude can cause treatment failure and allow the virus to develop resistance, and a real-world case documented a patient’s viral load rebounding after starting St. John’s Wort  [^28]. The guidance is clear and not exploratory: **people taking ART should not take St. John’s Wort.**  A coach who hears that a client is using or considering it should treat this as an immediate prompt to involve the prescribing clinician or pharmacist.

St. John’s Wort is the starkest example, but it is not the only herb that can interfere with medications through the same CYP3A4/P-gp pathways — others studied for similar effects include concentrated garlic supplements and various other botanicals  [^28]. The broader principle is essential and should be communicated without hedging: “natural” does not mean “safe,” and supplements are not inert. The single most important coaching habit is to encourage every client to keep a complete, current list of everything they take — prescriptions, over-the-counter products, vitamins, and herbs — and to review it with a pharmacist or physician who can check for interactions. Coaches educate and encourage that conversation; they do not adjudicate which supplements are safe with a given regimen.

### HIV and Mental Health

Mental health is inseparable from HIV care. People living with HIV experience depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress at rates well above the general population. A large meta-analysis of 240 studies found pooled prevalence estimates of roughly 31 percent for depression, 29 percent for anxiety, 20 percent for suicidal ideation, and 20 percent for PTSD among people living with HIV  [^29]. These are not signs of weakness; they are common, treatable conditions shaped by biology, life circumstance, and social stress.

**Stigma is a health determinant, and the research shows it in hard numbers.** HIV-related stigma is not merely hurtful — it measurably worsens health. Studies have found that higher levels of HIV-related stigma are associated with lower odds of achieving viral suppression, and that this effect operates substantially through depression and reduced adherence to treatment  [^30]. In one study of women living with HIV, 95 percent reported some level of HIV-related stigma, and higher stigma was associated with lower odds of being virally suppressed  [^30]. A separate meta-analysis found that people without depression were significantly more likely to achieve viral suppression than those with depression  [^31]. The chain is clear: stigma fuels depression and isolation, which undermine adherence and self-care, which worsen viral control and physical health. Naming and addressing stigma is therefore not a “soft” add-on to HIV care — it is central to it.

The protective factors are equally real. **Social support, community, a sense of meaning and purpose, and belonging** buffer against the harms of stigma and stress and are associated with better outcomes  [^18]. This is where wellness coaching, peer support, and faith and community networks can play a genuinely health-promoting role — by reducing isolation, restoring dignity, and helping people connect to something larger than the diagnosis.

### Long-Term Survivors and Aging With HIV

A generation of people has now lived with HIV for 30 years or more — many of them long-term survivors who outlived a prognosis that once measured life in months. Their existence is a triumph, and it has surfaced a new set of health questions centered on aging. People living with HIV appear to experience **accelerated aging**: age-related conditions tend to appear earlier and more often than in the general population  [^32]. The leading explanation is chronic immune activation and inflammation — sometimes called “inflammaging” — that persists even when the virus is well suppressed, driven in part by the long-lived reservoir, co-infections, and the immune disruption HIV causes [^18][^32].

The practical consequence is a higher and earlier burden of **comorbidities**: cardiovascular disease, reduced bone mineral density (osteopenia and osteoporosis) with higher fracture risk, kidney and liver disease, metabolic syndrome, and HIV-associated neurocognitive changes  [^32]. This is, in one sense, sobering news. But it is also where lifestyle becomes most powerful, because nearly every one of these conditions is influenced by the same modifiable factors that benefit everyone — not smoking, staying physically active, eating well, sleeping enough, managing stress, and staying engaged in care [^18]. For a person aging with HIV, lifestyle is not a side note; it is a primary tool for adding healthy years, working hand in hand with medical management of blood pressure, cholesterol, bone health, and the rest.

## When to Seek Medical Care — Referral Triggers

A core competency for any coach is knowing the limits of coaching and recognizing when a person needs prompt medical attention. The following are clear triggers to direct someone to their provider, urgent care, or emergency services as appropriate.

**Seek immediate or emergency attention for:**

- High fever, severe shortness of breath, chest pain, confusion, seizures, fainting, or sudden severe headache or vision changes.
- Any thoughts of suicide or self-harm, or a mental health crisis — treat this as an emergency and connect the person to crisis services right away.

**Contact the HIV provider promptly for:**

- New or worsening medication side effects — rash (which can occasionally signal a serious reaction), persistent nausea or vomiting, yellowing of the skin or eyes, unusual fatigue, numbness or tingling, or mood changes after a regimen change.
- Signs that could suggest an **opportunistic infection** or advancing disease: persistent fever or night sweats, unexplained weight loss, a cough that won’t resolve, white patches in the mouth (thrush), persistent diarrhea, new skin lesions, or trouble swallowing.
- New or worsening **depression or anxiety**, sleep that has collapsed, or escalating substance use.

**Always a referral trigger — no exceptions:**

- Any indication that a person is considering **stopping or interrupting ART**, delaying starting treatment, or replacing it with a “natural” alternative. This is never something to manage within coaching; it requires immediate involvement of the medical team [^11].
- Any plan to start an herbal supplement — **St. John’s Wort above all** — that could interact with ART [^28].

## What a Coach or Wellness Leader Can Appropriately Do

Wellness coaches are valuable members of the care team precisely when they understand and respect the boundaries of their role. Clarity about scope protects clients and strengthens the coach’s relationship with clinical providers.

**Coaches can appropriately:**

- **Educate** using accurate, current information — explaining concepts like U=U, the difference between HIV and AIDS, how transmission does and does not happen, and why adherence matters.
- **Support lifestyle change** — nutrition, physical activity, sleep, stress management, and substance-use reduction — within general wellness guidance.
- **Provide emotional support** and a non-judgmental, dignifying presence that counters isolation and stigma.
- **Connect people to resources** — peer support, community organizations, mental health services, food and housing assistance, and clinical care.
- **Encourage adherence** by helping with routines, reminders, problem-solving around barriers, and motivation — without managing the medication itself.
- **Help clients prepare for medical visits** — assisting them in articulating symptoms, questions, and concerns so they get the most from time with their provider.

**Coaches should refer rather than advise on:**

- **ART regimens, dosing, and any start/stop decisions** — these belong solely to the prescribing clinician.
- **Interpreting lab values** such as CD4 count and viral load — a coach can encourage a client to ask their provider what the numbers mean, but should not interpret them.
- **Opportunistic infections** or any new physical symptoms suggesting illness.
- **Interactions between supplements or herbs and ART** — flag the concern and route it to a pharmacist or physician.
- **Mental health crises** — refer immediately to qualified professionals and crisis services.

The unifying idea is **coordinated care.** A coach is one member of a team that includes physicians, nurses, pharmacists, mental health professionals, dietitians, and peer supporters. At their best, coaches amplify the work of the clinical team — reinforcing the medical plan, strengthening the daily habits that make treatment succeed, and tending to the human, social, and emotional dimensions of health that clinicians often lack time to address. A coach is never a replacement for medical care, and the most valuable coaches are those who know exactly when to hand off.

## A Word on Stigma, Dignity, and Hope

Stigma remains one of the most stubborn and harmful realities of HIV — and, as the research makes clear, one of the most consequential for health. People living with HIV continue to encounter discrimination in healthcare settings, workplaces, and relationships, and internalized stigma quietly erodes mental health, adherence, and viral control [^30]. Confronting stigma is therefore not a matter of politeness; it is a matter of health outcomes.

**Language is one of the tools we have.** Person-first language — “a person living with HIV” rather than “an HIV patient” or, worse, outdated and dehumanizing terms — affirms that a person is more than a diagnosis. Avoiding words like “infected,” “victim,” or “sufferer” in favor of neutral, respectful terms is a small change that signals a large truth: dignity is not conditional on health status. Coaches model this language and, in doing so, help shift the culture around them.

The deeper antidotes to stigma are connection and meaning. A sense of purpose, the experience of belonging to a supportive community, and hope for the future are not vague comforts — they are protective factors associated with resilience and better health [^18]. Helping a person rebuild or strengthen these is some of the most important work a coach can do.

And there is genuine reason for hope. In the span of a single generation, HIV has been transformed from a uniformly fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition; daily pills have given way, for some, to injections every other month; “undetectable equals untransmittable” has rewritten what is possible in intimacy and family life; and real, if still distant, cure research advances each year. The arc of HIV treatment bends unmistakably toward longer, healthier, fuller lives. For the people coaches walk alongside, the message is both honest and hopeful: with treatment as the foundation and healthy living as the structure built upon it, living well with HIV is not only possible — it is, increasingly, the expected path.
