Somewhere around 54 million Americans — and roughly 500 million people worldwide — are walking around with bones that are thinner than they should be. Most of them have no idea.[^1] [^2] No pain. No symptoms. No obvious warning. Bone loss is slow, silent, and gradual — right up until the moment it isn't. A hip fracture, a collapsed vertebra, a wrist that snaps during a minor fall. That's often how people find out.

But here's what changes everything: bone is not a static structure. It is living tissue, constantly tearing itself down and rebuilding — a process called remodeling. What you eat, how you move, how you sleep, how much stress you carry — all of it feeds or undermines this process, daily. That means the story of bone loss is not simply a story of getting older. It is, to a significant degree, a story of choices and circumstances — some of which can be changed.

This handout is for anyone who wants to understand what is actually happening in their bones, what they can do about it, and how to become a more informed participant in those conversations with their healthcare provider.

## 1. Osteopenia, Osteoporosis — What Do the Words Actually Mean?

When a DEXA scan (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry — the standard tool for measuring bone density) gives you a result, it comes with a number called a **T-score**. This score compares your bone density to a reference population of healthy young adults at their peak bone mass.

- A T-score of **0 to -1.0** is considered normal.
- A T-score of **-1.0 to -2.5** is called **osteopenia** — below-average density, but not in the fracture danger zone yet.
- A T-score of **-2.5 or lower** is classified as **osteoporosis** — bone tissue has become thin enough that the risk of fracture from everyday activity increases meaningfully.[^3]

There is also a **Z-score**, which compares your bone density to others of the same age and sex. Z-scores matter too — a person can have a T-score that looks alarming but a Z-score that suggests their bones are actually normal for their age. Clinicians use both, and context matters.

Clinicians also use a tool called **FRAX** (Fracture Risk Assessment Tool), developed by the World Health Organization, which combines bone density scores with other personal risk factors to estimate a person's 10-year probability of a major fracture. FRAX helps translate raw numbers into real-world clinical decisions — particularly when deciding whether someone's osteopenia warrants pharmaceutical treatment or whether lifestyle approaches are sufficient.[^4]

A newer measurement called the **Trabecular Bone Score (TBS)** can be derived from existing DEXA data and offers additional information about bone quality beyond density — measuring the texture and microarchitecture of trabecular (spongy inner) bone. TBS is increasingly used in combination with DEXA to refine fracture risk assessment, particularly in people with normal or borderline BMD who may have poorer bone quality than their T-score suggests.[^5]

> **Key Fact 1:** Globally, roughly 20% of the population has osteoporosis and another 40% has osteopenia — but most are never screened until a fracture occurs. In 2021 alone, low bone density was linked to nearly 460,000 deaths and 17.3 million disability-adjusted life years worldwide.[^6]

## 2. How Bone Actually Works

Picture bone not as the rigid, inert material it looks like in a textbook, but as a living tissue in constant conversation with your body. Every day, specialized cells called **osteoclasts** tunnel through old or damaged bone, dissolving it. Close behind them come **osteoblasts**, cells that fill in those tunnels with fresh bone matrix and mineralize it with calcium, phosphorus, and other minerals.[^7]

This process — resorption followed by formation — is called the **remodeling cycle**. Roughly 10% of the skeleton is remodeled in any given year.[^8] That means bones are genuinely responsive to input. What you do now matters. What you eat now matters.

The molecular system that governs much of this balance is called the **RANK/RANKL/OPG pathway**. Here is the short version: osteoblasts produce a signaling protein called RANKL, which activates osteoclasts and tells them to resorb bone. They also produce a competing protein called OPG (osteoprotegerin), which blocks RANKL and slows resorption. When the RANKL-to-OPG ratio tips toward resorption, bone is lost. When OPG dominates, bone is preserved. Exercise, certain hormones, and several nutrients influence this ratio — which is why lifestyle genuinely moves the needle.[^9]

**Peak bone mass** — the maximum density your skeleton achieves — occurs around age 25 to 30. After that, the question is not whether you'll lose some bone over time (you will), but how quickly and how much. Women are disproportionately affected after menopause because estrogen plays a key role in suppressing osteoclast activity. When estrogen drops sharply, osteoclasts run faster than osteoblasts can keep up. In the first five to seven years after menopause, bone loss can accelerate to 3–5% annually.[^10] Men lose bone more slowly — and later — which is partly why osteoporosis in men is so often diagnosed after a fracture, not before.[^11]

One useful way to think about it: your skeleton is a **bone bank**. The more you deposit in early adulthood, the more cushion you have for the inevitable withdrawals of aging. But — and this is worth saying clearly — even starting to rebuild and protect bone at age 60 or 70 makes a meaningful difference. It is never too late.

> **Key Fact 2:** Bone density can change measurably — in either direction — within 6 to 12 months with targeted interventions. Bone is not fixed. The skeleton's annual turnover rate of approximately 10% means there is real opportunity for meaningful change, regardless of where you are starting from.[^12]

## 3. Risk Factors — The Obvious and the Overlooked

Most people can name the basics: postmenopausal women, older age, low calcium intake. Those are real. But the full risk landscape is considerably wider.

### Modifiable factors

**Sedentary lifestyle** is perhaps the most underappreciated bone risk. Bone responds to mechanical loading — without it, osteoclast activity outpaces bone formation. **Smoking** reduces calcium absorption, lowers estrogen levels, and independently impairs osteoblast function.[^13] **Excessive alcohol** disrupts calcium absorption and inhibits osteoblast activity; heavy use (more than two to three drinks daily) increases fracture risk significantly, while light-to-moderate drinking appears less harmful.[^14]

**Very low body weight** or a history of **disordered eating** is a major risk factor — bones need adequate caloric intake and a healthy body mass to be properly built and maintained. The same applies to **extreme calorie restriction**, even without a clinical eating disorder diagnosis.

Some of the most underappreciated risks come from **medications** people take for entirely unrelated conditions:

- **Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)** — among the most widely prescribed drugs in the world — suppress gastric acid in a way that reduces calcium absorption. Long-term use (particularly over seven years) has been associated with increased vertebral, hip, and wrist fracture risk in observational studies, though a clear causal mechanism remains under investigation.[^15]
- **Corticosteroids** (oral prednisone, long-term inhaled steroids at high doses) are one of the best-documented pharmaceutical causes of bone loss, acting through multiple pathways including suppression of osteoblast activity and calcium absorption.[^16]
- **SSRIs and other antidepressants**: An association between SSRI use and reduced bone density has been identified in multiple studies, though the mechanism is still being clarified. Serotonin receptors are present in bone cells, and this interaction appears to influence bone remodeling.[^17]
- **Anticonvulsants**: Several anti-seizure medications accelerate vitamin D metabolism, leading to inadequate levels and impaired calcium absorption.[^18]
- **Excess thyroid hormone** (either from untreated hyperthyroidism or over-replacement with thyroid medication) accelerates bone resorption.[^19]

### Non-modifiable factors

Age, sex, genetics, a family history of fracture, and early menopause (before age 45) are all factors that cannot be changed but should be accounted for in screening decisions.

### Often overlooked factors

Several conditions that impair **nutrient absorption** significantly increase bone risk:
- **Celiac disease**: undiagnosed or undertreated celiac causes intestinal damage that reduces absorption of calcium, vitamin D, magnesium, and vitamin K — all bone-critical nutrients.[^20]
- **Crohn's disease and other inflammatory bowel conditions** carry similar risks, compounded by inflammation-driven bone loss.
- **Post-bariatric surgery**: Gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy dramatically alter the absorption of calcium and vitamin D; bone loss post-surgery can be rapid and significant without careful monitoring and supplementation.[^21]
- **Low testosterone in men**: Hypogonadism is a leading driver of bone loss in men, yet it is frequently undiagnosed.

**Chronic low-grade inflammation** is an increasingly recognized bone risk. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly through the NF-κB signaling pathway, directly activate osteoclasts. Conditions as varied as rheumatoid arthritis, chronic kidney disease, and metabolic syndrome all carry excess bone risk partly through this mechanism.[^22]

**Gut microbiome dysbiosis** is an emerging area: research now shows that microbiome composition influences calcium absorption, vitamin synthesis, immune signaling, and osteoclast activity — all relevant to bone.[^23]

### Plant-based diets and bone: a nuanced picture

For many people — particularly those following plant-based dietary patterns — the relationship between diet and bone health deserves careful, honest attention. Plant-based diets offer genuine protective advantages: they tend to be rich in potassium, magnesium, vitamin K1, and anti-inflammatory compounds, all of which support bone.[^24] But they carry specific risks when not carefully planned.

**Calcium** is the most obvious concern. Dairy products supply highly bioavailable calcium; plant sources vary enormously. Calcium from kale, bok choy, broccoli, and calcium-set tofu is reasonably well absorbed. But calcium from foods high in **oxalic acid** (spinach, Swiss chard, beet greens) or **phytic acid** (raw whole grains, legumes) is substantially less bioavailable — sometimes as little as 5% is absorbed from spinach.[^25] A plant-based eater who assumes they're getting adequate calcium from large amounts of high-oxalate vegetables may be significantly underestimating their actual intake.

**Vitamin B12 deficiency** is a well-documented bone risk, particularly relevant for vegans and some vegetarians. B12 deficiency raises homocysteine levels. Elevated homocysteine is independently associated with increased osteoclast activity, suppressed osteoblast function, and impaired collagen crosslinking — meaning bone becomes structurally weaker, not just thinner.[^26] This connection makes B12 status a genuine bone health concern, not just an energy concern.

**Protein**: adequate protein is essential for bone matrix and collagen synthesis. Studies of vegan populations that meet protein needs show bone density closer to omnivores; those with lower protein intake show greater divergence.[^27]

Large cohort data from Europe's EPIC-Oxford study found that vegans had modestly higher fracture risk compared to meat-eaters — but this risk largely disappeared when calcium intake was adequate.[^28] Well-constructed plant-based diets that include calcium-rich foods, fortified products, adequate protein, and B12 supplementation appear to be compatible with good bone health. The key is intentional construction, not the mere absence of animal products.

> **Key Fact 3:** Many people are surprised to learn that common medications — not just lifestyle factors — significantly affect bone. Long-term PPI use has been associated in observational studies with up to a 44% increased risk of vertebral fractures, while long-term corticosteroid use can reduce bone density by up to 10–20% within the first year of treatment.[^15] [^29]

## 4. Diagnosis and Testing — Reading the Numbers Wisely

The **DEXA scan** remains the gold standard for bone density measurement. It is painless (no needles, minimal radiation — less than a chest X-ray), takes 10–15 minutes, and gives density measurements at the hip, spine, and sometimes forearm — the three sites most prone to osteoporotic fracture.

Current U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) guidelines recommend DEXA screening for all women aged 65 and older, and for younger postmenopausal women whose 10-year fracture risk (as measured by FRAX) equals or exceeds that of a 65-year-old woman with no additional risk factors. Men with significant risk factors should also be screened, though formal universal screening recommendations for men are less consistent across guidelines.[^30]

**Bone turnover markers (BTMs)** — including P1NP (a marker of bone formation), CTx (a marker of bone resorption), and osteocalcin — are blood and urine tests that measure the rate of bone remodeling rather than the amount of bone. They are useful for monitoring treatment response (bone density changes slowly; BTMs respond within weeks to months), detecting secondary causes of bone loss, and tracking whether lifestyle interventions are working. Some practitioners use them proactively in early monitoring even before bone density becomes a concern. Mainstream use is primarily in monitoring treated patients; their utility in broader screening contexts is still being defined.[^31]

## 5. Nutrition — The Building Blocks

### Calcium — getting the story right

Calcium is the most abundant mineral in bone, and adequate intake is genuinely important. The controversy is not whether calcium matters, but how to get it and what happens when you get too much of it from supplements.

**Dietary calcium** — from food — is generally well-tolerated and appears to carry fewer concerns than supplemental calcium. The ongoing debate about **supplemental calcium and cardiovascular risk** began with a 2010 meta-analysis by Bolland and colleagues that suggested calcium supplements (without vitamin D) were associated with a roughly 27% increased risk of myocardial infarction.[^32] Subsequent analyses have challenged those findings on methodological grounds — including the exclusion of studies where no cardiovascular events occurred, which inflates risk estimates — and the picture remains genuinely contested.[^33] The current consensus among major osteoporosis organizations is to prioritize dietary calcium and use supplements only to fill gaps, choosing calcium citrate (better absorbed, especially if stomach acid is low) over calcium carbonate when supplementing.[^34]

**Top plant-based calcium sources with good bioavailability:** calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks, kale, bok choy, broccoli, almonds, edamame, white beans, calcium-rich mineral water. High-oxalate greens like spinach, despite their calcium content, contribute far less absorbed calcium than their labels suggest.

### Vitamin D

Vitamin D deficiency is genuinely widespread — affecting an estimated 40% of American adults and more in northern latitudes or populations with limited sun exposure.[^35] Without adequate vitamin D, the intestine cannot absorb calcium efficiently regardless of how much calcium is consumed. Most research supports targeting blood levels (25-hydroxyvitamin D) in the range of 30–50 ng/mL for bone health, though there is ongoing debate about optimal ranges.[^36]

D3 (cholecalciferol) is generally preferred over D2 (ergocalciferol) for supplementation, based on evidence that D3 raises blood levels more effectively and sustains them longer.

What is less commonly appreciated: **magnesium is required to activate vitamin D**. Without adequate magnesium, supplemental vitamin D cannot be converted to its active hormonal form — meaning people who supplement with vitamin D but are magnesium-deficient may see little benefit.[^37]

### Magnesium — the overlooked partner

Approximately half of Americans consume less magnesium than recommended.[^38] Its importance for bone is multidirectional: magnesium is stored in bone tissue (about 60% of total body magnesium is in the skeleton), it activates vitamin D, it regulates parathyroid hormone, and it directly influences the activity of both osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Magnesium deficiency alters the crystal structure of bone hydroxyapatite, reducing stiffness and load-bearing capacity. It also promotes low-grade inflammation — itself a driver of bone loss.[^39]

Good dietary sources include dark leafy greens, nuts, seeds, whole grains, dark chocolate, and legumes.

### Vitamin K2 — calcium's traffic director

Vitamin K2 (particularly the MK-7 form found in fermented foods like natto) activates two key proteins relevant to bone: **osteocalcin**, which incorporates calcium into bone matrix, and **matrix Gla protein (MGP)**, which prevents calcium from depositing in soft tissues like arteries. Without adequate vitamin K2, both proteins remain in their inactive, uncarboxylated form — meaning calcium may not reach bone even when blood levels are adequate.[^40]

Clinical evidence on K2 and bone is promising but not uniformly conclusive. A three-year randomized trial found that low-dose MK-7 helped decrease bone loss in postmenopausal women.[^41] A separate three-year RCT in women with osteopenia found that MK-7 supplementation increased osteocalcin carboxylation but did not significantly affect bone mineral density or microarchitecture when added to calcium and vitamin D.[^42] The discordance between studies reflects genuine uncertainty about dose, formulation, and the populations most likely to benefit. What appears consistent is that K2 status is relevant to bone — and many people have suboptimal intake.

Food sources include natto (extraordinarily rich in MK-7), certain fermented cheeses, and egg yolks. A single serving of natto contains more K2 than most Westerners consume in a week.

⚠️ **Important safety note:** Vitamin K2 directly interacts with warfarin (Coumadin) and other vitamin K antagonists. Co-administration can counteract the anticoagulant effect and increase clotting risk. Anyone on warfarin must consult their physician before consuming vitamin K2 supplements or regularly eating natto.[^43]

### Protein

The older framing that "protein leaches calcium from bones" via acid-ash hypothesis is not well supported at normal dietary intake levels. Current evidence suggests adequate protein is essential for bone — it provides the amino acid scaffolding for bone collagen and appears to support muscle-bone cross-talk.[^44] That said, some data suggest very high protein intakes (particularly above the range achieved through typical whole-food diets) may increase urinary calcium excretion, which would be worth ensuring is matched by adequate calcium intake.[^45] The practical takeaway: adequate protein supports bones; variety matters more than source; deficiency is more common and more harmful than excess in most dietary contexts.

### Collagen peptides — growing evidence

Collagen makes up approximately 90% of bone's organic matrix. Supplemental hydrolyzed collagen peptides are absorbed and distributed to connective tissues, where they appear to stimulate osteoblast activity through signaling involving integrin receptors and pathways including ERK/MAPK. A 2025 meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found that collagen peptide supplementation, particularly when combined with calcium and vitamin D, was associated with meaningful improvements in bone mineral density at the spine and femoral neck, as well as favorable effects on bone turnover markers.[^46] Earlier randomized trials also showed clinically relevant BMD gains with specific bioactive collagen peptides over 12 months.[^47] This is not an established standard of care — larger long-term RCTs on fracture outcomes are still needed — but the early mechanistic and clinical data are genuinely interesting and do not suggest harm.

### Dried plums — surprisingly strong evidence

One food finding that consistently surprises people: dried plums (prunes) have accumulated a meaningful body of evidence as a bone-protective food. The 2024 Penn State Prune Study — the largest and most rigorous trial to date, with 235 postmenopausal women — found that consuming 50 grams of prunes daily for 12 months prevented total hip bone mineral density loss, with effects persisting through the end of the trial. Advanced 3D imaging also showed preservation of cortical bone density and estimated bone strength at the tibia.[^48] Mechanisms appear to involve polyphenol-driven anti-inflammatory effects, favorable shifts in bone turnover markers, and possibly microbiome modulation.[^49]

> **Key Fact 4:** A single serving of natto — a traditional Japanese fermented soybean food — may contain 500–1000 mcg of vitamin K2 (MK-7), compared to the estimated daily average intake of 5–30 mcg in Western countries. Meanwhile, 50 grams of dried plums daily (about 5–6 prunes) preserved hip bone density in a 12-month randomized trial — not a supplement, not a drug, a whole food.[^48] [^43]

### Other bone-relevant nutrients

**Boron, silicon, zinc, and manganese** all play roles in collagen synthesis and bone matrix formation. Silicon (particularly as orthosilicic acid) has shown emerging evidence in collagen synthesis relevant to bone, though large-scale clinical trials are limited.[^50] Zinc is co-factor for enzymes involved in bone matrix synthesis, and low serum zinc has been associated with osteoporosis. These nutrients matter, and whole-food diets tend to supply them adequately; targeted supplementation without deficiency evidence is a different calculus.

**Vitamin C** is required for collagen synthesis — a fact worth noting for anyone at risk of subclinical deficiency.

**The alkaline diet claim**: the idea that certain foods create "acid" conditions that leach calcium from bones has gained popular currency but is not well supported by the evidence. The body tightly regulates blood pH regardless of diet. What *is* well-supported is that plant-rich diets benefit bones — but the mechanism appears to be the nutrients those diets deliver (potassium, magnesium, antioxidants, fiber), not their impact on blood pH.[^51]

## 6. Exercise — The Most Underused Medicine for Bone

This deserves to be said plainly: **exercise is probably the most powerful modifiable influence on bone health, at every age.** Not supplements. Not diet alone. Exercise.

Here is why: bone remodeling is driven by mechanical load. When bones are stressed beyond their everyday demands — through impact, resistance, or high-force movement — they respond by stimulating osteoblast activity and suppressing osteoclast activity. Brief, high-load stimuli appear more osteogenic than long, steady-state effort.

This is the reason swimming and cycling, despite being excellent for cardiovascular health, do not build bone meaningfully. They impose no significant gravitational or impact load on the skeleton.[^52] If cardiovascular fitness is the goal, these activities are excellent — but bone protection requires something more.

**Weight-bearing activity** (walking, hiking, dancing, stair climbing) is a floor, not a ceiling. It is better than nothing, but it is not enough on its own for people at meaningful bone risk.

**Resistance training** — progressive loading of muscles against weights, bands, or body weight — is among the best-studied interventions for bone. Muscle contractions generate tensile force on bone at attachment points, and resistance training consistently improves BMD at clinically relevant sites (spine, hip) in meta-analyses of older adults.[^53] The ACSM recommends resistance training two to three times per week at moderate to high intensity for bone protection.

**High-impact activities** — jumping, running, plyometrics — are among the strongest bone-stimulating inputs available. Brief loading spikes at high acceleration appear particularly osteogenic.[^54] For people without fracture risk, even 50–60 jumps daily may be sufficient to stimulate a measurable bone response. For people with osteoporosis or high fracture risk, high-impact exercise requires medical guidance to avoid fracture.

**Progressive high-intensity resistance and impact training (HiRIT)** — a supervised program involving heavy lifting and high-impact movement — has shown meaningful improvements in lumbar spine BMD in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis in recent randomized trials, challenging the assumption that people with osteoporosis must avoid intensity.[^55]

**Balance and fall prevention** deserve equal attention. A fracture requires both fragile bone and a fall. Programs that improve proprioception, coordination, and stability are among the most effective interventions for fracture prevention, independent of what they do to bone density. **Tai chi**, in particular, has been studied extensively: a 2024 meta-analysis of 22 randomized controlled trials found that tai chi significantly improved balance and physical function in older adults, with meaningful potential for fall risk reduction.[^56] Several meta-analyses have found fall risk reductions of 20–30% or more with sustained tai chi practice.[^57]

**One important caution**: The **female athlete triad** (now better described as **Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport, or RED-S**) presents a counterintuitive pattern: very lean, highly trained women — particularly in endurance sports — can have significantly lower bone density than sedentary women of the same age. The mechanism is energy deficit: when caloric intake does not match the demands of training, hormonal disruption follows, including suppression of estrogen, which accelerates bone loss. High training volume is not protective if the body is underfueled.[^58]

> **Key Fact 5:** Swimming and cycling are among the best exercises in the world for cardiovascular health — but they do not build bone, because they impose no gravitational or compressive load on the skeleton. By contrast, even 10 minutes of resistance training three times per week has shown measurable effects on bone turnover markers within weeks. What you do *matters*; not all exercise is equal for bone.[^52]

## 7. Lifestyle — The Whole Picture

### Sleep

Bone remodeling is not continuous throughout the day — it peaks during sleep. Cortisol (the body's primary stress hormone) is naturally low at night, allowing osteoblasts to work more freely. Chronic sleep disruption elevates nighttime cortisol, which suppresses bone formation and accelerates resorption. Emerging research has linked short sleep duration with lower BMD, though causal relationships are still being established.[^59]

### Stress and cortisol

Chronic psychological stress has direct skeletal consequences. Cortisol suppresses osteoblast differentiation, increases RANKL expression (driving bone resorption), and reduces calcium absorption from the gut. This is one of the more underappreciated pathways connecting chronic stress to physical deterioration. Anti-inflammatory lifestyle practices — including adequate sleep, movement, time in nature, and supportive relationships — affect bone biology through these pathways, not just through subjective wellbeing.[^60]

### Smoking

The bone harm of smoking is well-documented and mechanistically understood: nicotine directly inhibits osteoblast function; smoking lowers estrogen levels in women; it reduces intestinal calcium absorption; and it promotes an inflammatory environment that accelerates bone resorption. Bone density in long-term smokers is consistently lower than in non-smokers, and fracture risk is elevated even after controlling for other factors.[^13]

### Alcohol

The relationship is dose-dependent. Heavy drinking (three or more drinks per day) clearly impairs osteoblast activity and reduces bone density. Light to moderate intake does not appear to carry the same bone risk and may in some studies show a modest neutral or slightly favorable pattern, though this should not be interpreted as a recommendation.[^14]

### Gut health and the bone connection

The gut-bone axis is an emerging and genuinely exciting area of research. Gut microbiome composition influences bone through multiple pathways: production of **short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)** like butyrate and propionate that inhibit osteoclast differentiation and promote osteoblast activity; regulation of intestinal calcium absorption; modulation of inflammatory signaling that affects bone remodeling; and synthesis of vitamin K and certain B vitamins.[^61] [^62]

Preclinical data on probiotics and prebiotics for bone are promising, and early clinical studies have shown improvements in bone turnover markers in postmenopausal women. This is still an emerging field — the optimal probiotic strains, doses, and populations are not yet defined — but it is one where the evidence trajectory is moving in a consistent direction.[^63]

### Inflammation

Chronic low-grade inflammation — driven by poor diet, chronic stress, excess body fat, disrupted sleep, gut dysbiosis, and other modern lifestyle factors — activates osteoclasts through multiple pathways including NF-κB, TNF-α, and interleukin signaling. The anti-inflammatory lifestyle (diverse whole foods, movement, adequate sleep, stress management) is simultaneously an anti-bone-loss lifestyle.[^22]

## 8. Conventional Medical Treatments — Honest, Balanced Coverage

Understanding the medications available for osteoporosis helps people become better-informed patients and ask better questions — not to make medical decisions without their doctor, but to engage more meaningfully in those conversations.

### Bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, zoledronic acid)

Bisphosphonates are the most widely prescribed class of osteoporosis medications globally. They work by inhibiting osteoclast activity — essentially putting the brakes on bone resorption, which allows the slower process of bone formation to gradually increase density relative to baseline. They have strong evidence for reducing fracture risk, particularly at the spine and hip.[^64]

The important nuances:

- **Atypical femoral fractures (AFFs)**: With prolonged use, bisphosphonates can over-suppress bone remodeling to the point where microscopic damage accumulates without repair. This appears to contribute to an unusual fracture pattern in the mid-femur (thigh bone). AFF frequency rises from about 2.5 per 10,000 person-years at five years of use to approximately 13.1 per 10,000 person-years at eight or more years of use.[^65]
- **Osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ)**: A rare but serious complication, occurring primarily after invasive dental procedures. In the osteoporosis context (as opposed to the higher doses used for cancer), the incidence is very low, but it is real and warrants awareness.[^65]
- **Drug holidays**: Because bisphosphonates bind to bone and remain there even after the pills stop, their protective effects persist for a time after discontinuation. Current guidelines from major professional organizations suggest that for most patients at moderate risk who have taken oral bisphosphonates for five years (or intravenous bisphosphonates for three years), a drug holiday of two to five years is reasonable — balancing continued protection against the risk of long-term adverse effects.[^66]

### Denosumab (Prolia)

Denosumab is a monoclonal antibody that directly targets RANKL — the signaling protein that activates osteoclasts. By blocking RANKL, it effectively suppresses bone resorption. It requires an injection every six months and has demonstrated good fracture risk reduction.

The critical and often under-communicated risk: **rebound bone loss on discontinuation.** When denosumab is stopped, osteoclast activity surges — sometimes dramatically. Without a transition to bisphosphonate therapy, up to 10% of patients who discontinue denosumab experience multiple vertebral fractures within 12 months of their last dose.[^67] This rebound phenomenon has led professional guidelines to emphasize that denosumab should only be started if a plan for what comes after is already in place. It is not a drug that can simply be stopped.[^68]

### Anabolic agents (teriparatide, romosozumab)

Unlike the drugs above, which primarily slow bone resorption, anabolic agents actively stimulate bone formation. Teriparatide (Forteo) is a synthetic form of parathyroid hormone; romosozumab (Evenity) works by blocking sclerostin, a protein that inhibits bone formation. These are typically reserved for people with severe osteoporosis or multiple fractures and are significantly more expensive. Both have demonstrated meaningful increases in BMD and fracture risk reduction.

### Hormone therapy (estrogen/HRT)

Estrogen has well-established bone-protective effects and is the most physiologically direct way to address the bone loss that follows menopause. The Women's Health Initiative (WHI) trial, published in 2002, raised concerns about cardiovascular and breast cancer risk associated with combination estrogen-progestin therapy, leading to a dramatic decline in HRT prescribing.[^69] 

Subsequent re-analysis of that data — along with the accumulation of new evidence — has substantially nuanced the picture. The **timing hypothesis** has emerged: women who begin HRT close to menopause (within the "window" of approximately ten years) have a different and more favorable risk-benefit profile than those who begin much later. Among women aged 50–59 in the WHI's estrogen-only arm, HRT was associated with reduced myocardial infarction risk and all-cause mortality.[^70] A 2026 real-world study of over 137,000 postmenopausal women found that early HRT initiation was associated with significantly lower long-term osteoporosis risk and a 13% reduction in fracture risk.[^71]

HRT remains an active, evolving area. Current guidance from major professional organizations considers it appropriate for postmenopausal women with bone loss, particularly those who also have menopausal symptoms, and particularly when initiated within ten years of menopause or before age 60. Individual risk assessment is essential; clinician conversation is required.

**Bioidentical hormones** — formulations of estradiol and progesterone chemically identical to what the ovary produces — are used by many women and some practitioners, often through compounding pharmacies. Transdermal estradiol in particular has shown favorable cardiovascular profiles compared to oral conjugated equine estrogens in some studies.[^72] Evidence on compounded preparations specifically is more limited than evidence on FDA-approved formulations, and practitioners who recommend them do so based on mechanistic reasoning and available data on the individual molecules rather than extensive compounded-formulation trial data. This is an area where honest conversation about evidence gaps is appropriate.

**Progesterone and bone**: Some practitioners contend that natural progesterone (specifically progesterone, not synthetic progestins) supports osteoblast activity through progesterone receptors on bone cells. There is mechanistic plausibility — osteoblasts do express progesterone receptors — but the controlled clinical trial evidence is limited and the results are mixed. This remains a contested area; neither dismissal nor confident affirmation is warranted based on current data.[^73]

> **Key Fact 6:** Stopping denosumab without a planned transition to another bone-protecting medication can trigger a rebound surge in bone resorption that leads to multiple spontaneous vertebral fractures within 12 months — a risk that is well-documented but frequently not discussed with patients when starting the drug.[^67] If you or someone you support is on denosumab, the question of what comes next is worth raising proactively with a clinician.

## 9. Functional and Integrative Approaches — Evidence Landscape

### Approaches with a growing evidence base

**Comprehensive micronutrient assessment and repletion** — beyond just calcium and vitamin D, looking at the full picture of magnesium, vitamin K2, zinc, boron, vitamin C, B12, and vitamin D together — reflects a systems view of bone that is well-supported by the biology. Each of these nutrients plays a distinct mechanistic role, and deficiency in any one of them can impair the work of the others. This is not fringe thinking; it is the direction evidence-informed nutrition is moving.

**Gut health optimization**: given what we now know about the gut-bone axis, interventions that improve microbiome diversity — higher fiber intake, fermented foods, prebiotics, reduction of inflammatory dietary patterns — are rationally expected to support bone health. Clinical evidence specifically for bone is still emerging, but the mechanistic basis is solid and the risks of improving gut health are essentially nil.[^63]

**Addressing underlying hormonal imbalances**: Thyroid function, adrenal health, sex hormone status, and insulin sensitivity all intersect with bone metabolism. A functional evaluation of these systems in someone with concerning bone density is well within reasonable integrative practice.

**Red light / photobiomodulation therapy**: Very early preclinical and small clinical data suggest near-infrared light may influence osteoblast activity and bone healing. Evidence is genuinely preliminary — this is an area to watch rather than an established intervention, and it should be described as such.

### Approaches with limited but not dismissed evidence

**Strontium**: Pharmaceutical strontium ranelate was available in some countries as an osteoporosis treatment and showed meaningful fracture risk reduction in large trials, but was withdrawn from European markets due to cardiovascular concerns at high doses. Over-the-counter strontium supplements (typically strontium citrate at much lower doses) are widely used, and some practitioners report clinical benefit, but controlled trial evidence at supplemental doses is limited. Bone density increases observed with strontium supplementation may partly reflect strontium's incorporation into bone (which makes bone appear denser on DEXA) rather than true bone strengthening — a complicating factor in interpreting results.

**Maca and adaptogenic herbs**: Some adaptogens have been proposed for hormonal support that indirectly benefits bone. Evidence is largely mechanistic or based on small studies; it is neither sufficient to recommend confidently nor sufficient to dismiss entirely.

### Approaches requiring caution

**Very high-dose vitamin D without monitoring**: Vitamin D toxicity is real, occurring at chronically high supplemental doses (typically above 10,000 IU/day sustained, though individual variation exists). Paradoxically, very high vitamin D levels can increase calcium in the blood in ways that are harmful. Regular blood-level monitoring when supplementing is appropriate.[^74]

**High-dose vitamin A as retinol**: This is a well-documented bone risk that most supplement users are unaware of. Retinol (preformed vitamin A, found in cod liver oil and some supplements) at chronically high doses interferes with vitamin D activity and has been associated with reduced bone density and increased fracture risk in multiple studies. This risk does not apply to beta-carotene (the plant-based precursor).[^75]

**Mega-dose supplemental calcium without cofactors**: High supplemental calcium without adequate magnesium, vitamin K2, and vitamin D may be ineffective or potentially harmful — the question of where that calcium actually goes (to bone, or to soft tissue) depends substantially on cofactor status.

> **Key Fact 7:** The gut microbiome directly influences bone density. Microbial production of short-chain fatty acids — primarily from fermenting dietary fiber — inhibits osteoclast differentiation and enhances calcium absorption from the gut. In early clinical studies, probiotic and prebiotic supplementation improved bone turnover markers in postmenopausal women. The gut-bone connection is one of the most rapidly developing areas in bone biology, with implications for how we think about dietary fiber, fermented foods, and intestinal health.[^61] [^62]

## 10. When to Refer — Scope Boundaries for Coaches

Health and wellness coaches working in this space can contribute enormously to bone health outcomes. At the same time, some situations clearly require clinical oversight.

**A coach can appropriately:**
- Educate clients about bone biology, risk factors, and the evidence landscape across nutrition, movement, lifestyle, and emerging approaches
- Encourage age-appropriate DEXA screening and help clients understand their results
- Support and reinforce lifestyle changes known to influence bone health (diet, exercise, sleep, stress)
- Help clients prepare informed questions before clinician appointments
- Discuss what the evidence does and doesn't show about various approaches, with appropriate calibration of certainty
- Coach-safe language for discussing contested topics: "The evidence on this approach is growing but not yet conclusive — here is what studies have shown so far..."

**A coach should connect clients with a clinician when:**
- A client has had a fragility fracture (a fracture from a minor fall or trauma that normal bone wouldn't sustain)
- T-score is below -2.5 or FRAX indicates high fracture risk
- A client is currently taking medications that affect bone (corticosteroids, PPIs, anticonvulsants, thyroid medications) and the bone impact hasn't been addressed with their prescribing physician
- A client is considering stopping or modifying prescribed bone medications — this is a clinical decision
- A client has signs of malabsorption conditions (celiac, Crohn's, post-bariatric) that haven't been evaluated for bone impact
- A client shows signs of secondary osteoporosis triggers: unexplained bone loss, symptoms of thyroid or parathyroid dysfunction, kidney disease
- A client is considering high-dose vitamin D, vitamin A, or other potentially risky supplement protocols

The goal is not to be the last word on bone — it is to help clients engage more actively, more informedly, and more effectively with the full system of care available to them.

## 11. Practical Action Steps

Bone health is a lifelong project. It doesn't start at 65 — it starts as a child and never stops. The earlier someone builds good bone habits, the more runway they have. But the evidence is genuinely clear: starting at 60, or 70, still moves the needle in a meaningful direction.

**At every age:**
- Get enough calcium from food first; top up with supplements only if needed, choosing calcium citrate for better absorption
- Know your vitamin D status and maintain levels in a healthy range with sunlight or supplementation; take D3 over D2
- Address magnesium — most adults fall short; food sources (leafy greens, nuts, seeds, whole grains) are the best route
- Include vitamin K2 foods when possible (fermented foods, some cheeses); supplementation requires careful attention if on anticoagulants
- Eat adequate protein; don't let fear of protein displace the amino acids bones need
- Build diverse, fiber-rich eating patterns that support gut microbiome health
- Move — with impact, with resistance, with variety; don't let cardiovascular exercise displace bone-loading activity
- Prioritize sleep and address chronic stress as bone health priorities, not just mood priorities
- If you smoke, stopping is one of the most impactful bone decisions you can make
- Know what medications you take and ask your doctor if any affect bone; this includes PPIs, corticosteroids, antidepressants, and anticonvulsants

**If you're over 50:**
- Get a baseline DEXA scan if you haven't; know your numbers
- Calculate your FRAX score — it puts numbers in context
- Add dedicated balance and fall-prevention training; proprioception declines with age and is trainable
- Review all medications with your physician or pharmacist for bone impact
- If you've had a low-trauma fracture, seek evaluation — this is a clinical priority

**If you're working with someone on medication:**
- If they're on denosumab, make sure they and their doctor have a clear plan for what happens if/when it stops
- If they're on bisphosphonates long-term, the drug holiday question is worth discussing with their clinician
- If they're on corticosteroids chronically, bone monitoring and protection should already be part of their care plan — and if it isn't, it should be

The body you're in is not fixed. The skeleton is alive and responsive. Every meal, every workout, every good night's sleep is a contribution to a structure that will either support you well for decades — or break down prematurely. That is worth taking seriously, and it is worth starting now.
