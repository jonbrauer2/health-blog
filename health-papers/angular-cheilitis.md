## TL;DR

- Angular cheilitis is a common, treatable, and largely preventable inflammation of the corners of the mouth, usually caused by a mix of saliva-driven skin damage and infection with *Candida* yeast and/or *Staphylococcus aureus* bacteria — not by simple dry lips.
- It is frequently a “messenger” for something deeper: nutritional shortfalls (iron, B vitamins, zinc), denture or saliva problems, or systemic disease like diabetes — and 80% of successfully treated people relapse at least once if those root causes are not addressed.
- The smartest approach treats both the surface (antifungal/antibacterial creams, barrier protection) and the source (nutrition, denture care, blood sugar, immune health), and escalates to a clinician when lesions persist beyond 2–3 weeks, are one-sided, or bleed on their own.

## Key Findings

- **It’s mostly an infection on top of an irritation.** *Candida* can be detected in about 93% of angular cheilitis lesions, and most cases are polymicrobial — *Candida albicans* and *S. aureus* together in roughly 60–75% of patients.
- **Nutrition matters more than people think.** Up to about 25% of cases are linked to iron or B-vitamin deficiency.
- **It is a recurring condition by nature.** A classic five-year follow-up study found 80% of successfully treated patients relapsed at least once.
- **Certain groups are far more vulnerable:** denture wearers (up to ~28%), older adults (~11%), people with diabetes, HIV, inflammatory bowel disease, and those with restricted or poorly planned diets.
- **Plant-forward eating is healthy but needs attention to B12, iron, zinc, and riboflavin** — gaps are about planning, not about plants being “bad.”
- **Treatment evidence runs on a spectrum** from well-supported (antifungals, nutritional repletion) to emerging (probiotics, honey, coconut oil) — the honest framing matters.

## Details

### 1. The corner-of-the-mouth mystery

Picture this. You wake up one morning and the corner of your mouth feels tight and sore. By lunchtime there’s a little red crack. You assume your lips are just dry, so you slather on lip balm — and yet a week later it’s worse, maybe splitting and stinging every time you yawn, smile, or bite into a sandwich. Sound familiar? You may be dealing with angular cheilitis.

Angular cheilitis (also called angular stomatitis, perleche, commissural cheilitis, or cheilosis) is an inflammation at one or both corners of the mouth — the spots clinicians call the “labial commissures.” The word *perleche* comes from the French for “through licking,” a nod to one of its most common triggers.[ ^1] It is described as the most common bacterial and fungal infection of the lips,  yet it is widely misunderstood and frequently mistaken for ordinary chapped lips or a cold sore.[^2]

Here’s the encouraging part: angular cheilitis is highly treatable and, in most cases, curable, and it rarely causes lasting harm. The catch is that it loves to come back if you treat only the surface and ignore *why* it showed up. This guide will help you understand both.

### 2. What is angular cheilitis, really?

Angular cheilitis shows up as red, swollen, often tender patches right at the corners where your upper and lower lips meet. In mild cases it looks like pinkish redness. As it progresses, the skin can become macerated (water-logged and soft), then crack, fissure, scale, crust, and sometimes bleed. Severe or chronic cases can develop deep fissures, ooze, or form granulation tissue.[^1] The chief sensations are dryness, soreness, burning, or itching, and the discomfort tends to worsen when you open your mouth wide.[^3]

The corners of the mouth are a uniquely vulnerable piece of real estate. They are the hinge of the oral opening, so they move and stretch constantly, and they sit at the transition between facial skin and the moist lining of the mouth.[^1] When saliva pools there and stays, its digestive enzymes start to break down the skin barrier itself — and once that barrier is broken, the microbes that normally live in your mouth move in.[^4]

**How is it different from look-alikes?**

- **Cold sores (herpes labialis):** Cold sores are caused by the herpes simplex virus and usually appear as fluid-filled blisters that tingle first, then blister, then crust. They are typically on the lip itself (often just one side) and are contagious. Angular cheilitis sits specifically in the corners, is usually on both sides, develops gradually, and is *not* contagious.[^5][^6]
- **Simple dry/chapped lips (cheilitis simplex):** Plain chapped lips affect the whole lip surface and respond to ordinary lip balm. Angular cheilitis concentrates in the corners and characteristically does *not* improve with lip balm alone — a key tell.[^7]
- **Lip/oral cancer:** This is rare but important. A persistent, one-sided lesion that does not respond to treatment should always be evaluated, because oral squamous cell carcinoma can mimic an innocent-looking sore.[^1]

**Who gets it, and how often?** In the general U.S. population, angular cheilitis affects approximately 0.7% of people, but it climbs steeply in certain groups. It shows a “bimodal” pattern — appearing most often in children and again in adults aged 30 to 60.[ ^2] Older adults have roughly an 11% prevalence, and denture wearers face a three-fold higher incidence, reaching up to about 28%.[^2] Men are affected about twice as often as women, though that difference primarily reflects denture use and other health conditions rather than sex itself.[^2]

**Acute vs. chronic.** Some cases flare up quickly and resolve with treatment. Others become chronic or refractory — lingering or returning again and again. Recurrence is the rule rather than the exception when the underlying drivers go unaddressed.[^8]

### 3. Root causes and contributing factors

Angular cheilitis is what doctors call a *multifactorial* condition: it usually takes more than one thing going wrong at once. Think of it as a chain — saliva softens the skin, the barrier breaks, microbes invade, and an underlying vulnerability (a nutrient gap, a weak immune system, an anatomical problem) keeps the door open.

**Microbial causes (the most common).** *Candida* yeast — especially *Candida albicans* — is the single most common culprit. It’s a normal resident of the mouth in roughly 40–60% of healthy people, which is why finding it isn’t automatically proof it’s the villain; it’s detected in about 93% of angular cheilitis lesions but is the sole pathogen in only 20–50%.[ ^1] The yeast’s invasive, thread-like “hyphal” form is the troublemaker. *Staphylococcus aureus* is the lone bacterial cause in about 20% of cases, and because it often hides in the nostrils, treatment sometimes needs to target the nose too.[^1] Beta-hemolytic streptococci are isolated in 8–15% of cases, less commonly as a sole pathogen. Most cases are mixed (polymicrobial), with *Candida* and *Staph* together in 60–75%.[^1]

**Nutritional deficiencies (high priority).** This is one of the most important and most overlooked drivers. Up to about 25% of angular cheilitis cases are tied to iron or B-vitamin deficiency.[^9] The usual suspects:

- **Iron deficiency / iron-deficiency anemia** — a well-documented cause that can impair immunity and wound healing.[^10]
- **Riboflavin (B2)** — classic deficiency sign; cracks at the mouth corners are a textbook symptom.[^11]
- **Niacin (B3)** — deficiency (pellagra) can cause cheilitis; in some analyses it’s a more frequent cause than riboflavin deficiency.[^12]
- **Pyridoxine (B6)** — deficiency causes cheilosis and seborrhea-like changes around the mouth.[^12]
- **Folate (B9) and cobalamin (B12)** — deficiencies can cause megaloblastic anemia, glossitis, and angular cheilitis.[^12]
- **Zinc** — deficiency impairs wound healing and skin integrity; cheilitis is part of the picture.[^13]

We’ll return to nutrition in depth in Section 6, including how this plays out in plant-based eating — directly and fairly.

**Immune compromise.** Anything that weakens local or whole-body immunity raises risk: HIV/AIDS (up to 10% of people with HIV develop oral thrush, with or without associated angular cheilitis), diabetes, cancer and its treatments, chronic inhaled or oral steroids, and the natural decline of immune vigor with age.[^1][^2]

**Structural and mechanical factors.** Anything that keeps the corners moist or deepens the folds: ill-fitting or worn dentures (which reduce the “vertical dimension” of the lower face and cause the upper lip to overhang the lower), drooling, chronic lip-licking, deep “marionette” folds at the corners, mouth breathing, and thumb-sucking or pacifier use in children.[^1][^3]

**Skin conditions.** Allergic and irritant contact dermatitis cause up to 22% of cases — common triggers include nickel in braces, toothpaste and mouthwash ingredients, lip cosmetics, and expired lip balm.[^1] Atopic dermatitis (eczema), seborrheic dermatitis, and psoriasis can also involve the corners of the mouth.[^14]

**Medications.** Oral retinoids like isotretinoin (and acitretin) dry the lips and corners; chemotherapy agents and targeted cancer drugs (e.g., sorafenib) can trigger it; and antibiotics can promote *Candida* overgrowth by disrupting normal flora.[^10][^1] Hypervitaminosis A (too much vitamin A) is another cause.[^1]

**Lifestyle.** Smoking impairs healing and local immunity, alcohol contributes to nutritional gaps, and anything that creates excess moisture or maceration at the corners feeds the cycle.[^1]

### 4. Diagnosis: what to expect from a clinical evaluation

Most of the time, angular cheilitis is diagnosed simply by looking at it — a clinical diagnosis based on the location and appearance.[^15] But because it’s multifactorial, a thoughtful clinician digs deeper, especially if it’s stubborn.

**The work-up may include:**

- **A careful history** — denture use, dental work, lip-licking habits, diet, medications, and symptoms suggesting systemic disease (such as diarrhea or abdominal pain pointing to Crohn’s disease, or dry eyes and mouth pointing to Sjögren’s syndrome).[^15]
- **Swabs/cultures** — to identify whether *Candida*, *Staph*, *Strep*, or a mix is involved. People with dentures often have the denture swabbed too, since it can be a reservoir.[^5]
- **Blood work** when nutritional or systemic causes are suspected: complete blood count (CBC) with red-cell size (MCV), iron studies with ferritin, folate, vitamin B12 (sometimes with homocysteine or methylmalonic acid for accuracy), riboflavin (B2) and B6 status, serum zinc, and fasting blood glucose or HbA1c.[^15][^9]

**Why getting it right matters.** Treating the wrong organism — say, reaching for an antifungal when *Staph* is the real driver, or using a steroid alone on an active infection — can prolong or worsen the problem. Clinicians are specifically warned not to use topical steroid monotherapy without ruling out infection.[^16]

**When to see a clinician.** Seek evaluation if lesions persist beyond 2–3 weeks of reasonable self-care, bleed spontaneously, are one-sided and unresponsive, or come with signs of systemic illness.[^15] A one-sided lesion that won’t heal deserves prompt attention to rule out malignancy.[^1]

**When to escalate to a specialist.** Severe or treatment-resistant cases warrant referral to a dermatologist or oral medicine/oral pathology specialist; denture-related cases call for a dentist or prosthodontist; suspected systemic disease may need rheumatology or gastroenterology.[^8]

### 5. Treatment approaches by evidence status

Rather than sorting treatments into “works” versus “doesn’t work,” it’s more honest — and more useful — to place them on a spectrum from well-supported to emerging.

**Well-supported.**

- **Antifungal creams** — nystatin, clotrimazole, miconazole, and ketoconazole are mainstays, typically applied for about two weeks.[^1] A systematic review and meta-analysis found miconazole more effective than nystatin for oral thrush, with a possibly lower relapse rate for the gel form.[^17] The most direct evidence in angular cheilitis itself is older and small: in a double-blind comparison within one study, lesions caused by *Candida* healed within 28 days with nystatin while placebo-treated lesions persisted.[^18]
- **Antibacterial creams** — mupirocin or fusidic acid for staphylococcal involvement, with the option to treat the nostrils to clear the bacterial reservoir.[^1]
- **Combined antifungal/low-potency steroid** — e.g., miconazole or iodoquinol or nystatin paired with hydrocortisone (or nystatin-triamcinolone) to calm inflammation while treating infection; miconazole-plus-hydrocortisone is favored by some clinicians as a first-line because miconazole also has mild activity against gram-positive bacteria.[^1]
- **Nutritional repletion** — when a deficiency is found, correcting it resolves the deficiency-driven disease. In one report, an iron-deficient woman’s angular cheilitis cleared on oral iron and did not recur over two years of follow-up.[^10]

**Supportive / reasonable evidence.**

- **Barrier protection** — petroleum jelly, emollients, or zinc oxide to shield the corners from saliva; this is essential and often sufficient for idiopathic cases.[^1]
- **Oral and denture hygiene** — cleaning dentures and disinfecting them overnight (e.g., in dilute hypochlorite or chlorhexidine) removes a key *Candida* reservoir.[^8]
- **Dietary modification and blood sugar control** — glycemic control in diabetes directly lowers angular cheilitis frequency, as HbA1c correlates with disease.[^8]
- **Preventive chewing gum** — a clinical trial found xylitol or chlorhexidine/xylitol gum reduced angular cheilitis in people 60 and older.[^19]

**Emerging / limited evidence.**

- **Probiotics** — a systematic review and meta-analysis concluded probiotics were superior to placebo in preventing and treating oral candidiasis in the elderly and denture wearers, though more evidence is needed comparing them to standard antifungals.[^20] One triple-blind randomized trial found *Lactobacillus rhamnosus* reduced the severity of denture stomatitis.[^21]
- **Topical honey (medical-grade/Manuka)** — lab studies show honey can inhibit *Candida albicans*, but there is no clinical trial of honey on angular cheilitis specifically, and the anti-yeast effect in the lab appears largely due to sugar concentration rather than a Manuka-unique compound. Patient-reported benefit exists, but the evidence is preliminary.[^22]
- **Coconut oil / food-based antifungals** — lauric and caprylic acids from coconut oil show antifungal activity against *Candida albicans* in the lab, but human clinical evidence is lacking.[^23]

**Not recommended.**

- **OTC corticosteroids alone**, applied without addressing the underlying infection — this can worsen or prolong the condition, and chronic facial steroid use carries its own risks.[^16]
- **Ignoring an identified nutritional deficiency** — surface treatment without repletion sets up relapse.[^10]

### 6. Nutrition as both a root cause and a solution

If there’s one section worth lingering on, it’s this one. Angular cheilitis is sometimes the first visible sign of a deficiency, which means your mouth’s corners can act as an early-warning system for the rest of your body.

**The key nutrients, what they do, and where to find them:**

- **Iron.** Iron carries oxygen and supports immunity and wound repair; deficiency is the most common cause of anemia worldwide and a well-documented cause of angular cheilitis.[^10] The RDA is 8 mg/day for adult men and postmenopausal women, and 18 mg/day for premenopausal women (27 mg in pregnancy).[^24] Sources: red meat, poultry, and seafood (heme iron, well absorbed); beans, lentils, tofu, spinach, and fortified grains (non-heme iron, absorbed better with vitamin C). Deficiency can look like fatigue, pale skin, and cracked mouth corners — sometimes before anemia even shows up.[^10]
- **Riboflavin (B2).** Essential for energy metabolism and skin health; deficiency (ariboflavinosis) classically causes cracked lips and angular stomatitis.[^11] The RDA is about 1.1 mg/day for women and 1.3 mg/day for men.[^25] Sources: milk, yogurt, eggs, almonds, mushrooms, green leafy vegetables, and fortified grains. Note that riboflavin is destroyed by light — which is why milk in clear containers loses it.[^11]
- **Niacin (B3).** Supports energy metabolism in fast-renewing tissues like skin; severe deficiency is pellagra (“the three D’s” — dermatitis, diarrhea, dementia — plus cheilitis).[^12] Sources: meat, poultry, fish, peanuts, and fortified grains.
- **Pyridoxine (B6).** Deficiency causes cheilosis and seborrhea-like rash around the mouth and is more likely with heavy alcohol use or certain medications.[^12] Sources: poultry, fish, potatoes, chickpeas, and bananas.
- **Folate (B9) and B12.** Both can cause megaloblastic anemia with glossitis and angular cheilitis. Folate is plentiful in leafy greens, legumes, and fortified grains. B12 is the critical one for plant-based eaters (see below). The RDA for B12 is 2.4 mcg/day for adults.[^26]
- **Zinc.** Crucial for wound healing and immune function; deficiency causes angular cheilitis along with dermatitis, hair loss, and diarrhea.[^13] The RDA is 11 mg/day for men and 8 mg/day for women.[^27] Sources: meat, shellfish, legumes, seeds, and whole grains (though phytates in plants reduce absorption).

**Plant-based and whole-food diets — an honest look.** Plant-forward eating offers real benefits, and being vegetarian or vegan does not, by itself, cause deficiency. The issue is planning. Several nutrients tied to angular cheilitis warrant deliberate attention on a plant-based diet:

- **B12** is the big one — it occurs naturally only in animal foods, so vegans who don’t supplement or eat fortified foods are at real risk. About 43% of vegans have been found deficient in some studies, and supplementation status (not how long you’ve been vegan) is what predicts adequacy.[^28]
- **Iron** from plants (non-heme) is less readily absorbed; premenopausal vegetarian women in particular can struggle to meet needs from food alone. Pairing iron-rich plants with vitamin C, and limiting tea/coffee at meals, improves absorption.[^29]
- **Zinc** absorption is reduced by phytates in grains and legumes; soaking, sprouting, and fermenting help.[^27]
- **Riboflavin** is abundant in dairy for vegetarians, but vegans should lean on fortified foods, almonds, mushrooms, and leafy greens.[^11]

The takeaway is not “don’t eat plants.” It’s “eat plants wisely” — build in fortified foods, supplement B12 reliably, and pay attention to iron and zinc. StatPearls explicitly lists strict vegans (and their exclusively breastfed, unsupplemented infants) among the populations vulnerable to deficiency-related angular cheilitis, which is a reason for thoughtful planning, not alarm.[^1]

**Supplementation and the “food first” principle.** Food first is a sound default: whole foods deliver nutrients alongside fiber and other beneficial compounds. But supplementation is a necessary bridge in specific situations — confirmed deficiency, absorption problems (aging, low stomach acid, PPI use, celiac or Crohn’s disease, bariatric surgery), or dietary patterns that reliably fall short (B12 on a vegan diet). Clinical repletion protocols use doses such as oral elemental iron for iron deficiency, riboflavin around 5–15 mg/day, B6 around 50 mg/day, and B12 by mouth or injection depending on the cause.[^30] These should be guided by lab testing and a clinician, since more is not always better (excess iron, zinc, and vitamin A all carry risks).[^27]

### 7. Lifestyle factors that perpetuate or prevent recurrence

- **Oral and denture care.** Brush and clean well; if you wear dentures, clean them daily and disinfect them overnight, ensure they fit (an ill-fitting denture both injures the corners and harbors yeast), and remove them at night.[^8]
- **Saliva management.** Tackle the moisture cycle: address mouth breathing, manage drooling, and break the lip-licking habit (it feels soothing but makes things worse).[^1]
- **Skin and lip care.** Use a barrier ointment (petroleum jelly or zinc oxide) to protect the corners, and choose lip products free of irritants if contact dermatitis is suspected.[^1]
- **Blood sugar regulation.** For people with diabetes, tighter glycemic control measurably reduces angular cheilitis recurrence.[^8]
- **Immune support.** Adequate sleep, stress management, and physical activity support the immune system that keeps *Candida* in check — a sensible, low-risk foundation.
- **Smoking and alcohol.** Smoking suppresses innate immunity and oral defenses and increases susceptibility to *Candida*, while also impairing wound healing through reduced tissue blood flow; quitting helps healing.[^31][^32] Reducing alcohol helps both nutrition and tissue repair.

### 8. Why it keeps coming back

Recurrence is angular cheilitis’s signature frustration. A prospective five-year follow-up of 48 treated patients found that 80% reported recurrence on one or more occasions during the observation period — and, notably, recurrence was not correlated with the initial *Candida* or *Staph* infection.[^33] Why?

- **The root cause was never addressed.** If a denture still doesn’t fit, a deficiency is uncorrected, or blood sugar stays high, the door stays open.[^8]
- **Candida reservoirs persist.** Dentures and the oral cavity can harbor yeast in biofilms — protective communities that resist treatment — so the surface clears but the source remains.[^34]
- **Unrecognized systemic disease.** Undiagnosed diabetes, celiac disease, or inflammatory bowel disease can quietly drive recurrence. Recurrent angular cheilitis in someone *without* dentures should specifically prompt a look for HIV or diabetes.[^15]

The practical lesson: treat the whole person, not just the corner of the mouth. Lasting relief comes from pairing surface treatment with root-cause repair and, for chronic cases, ongoing preventive measures like barrier ointments or intermittent antifungal use.[^8]

### 9. Controversial and contested areas

**“It’s just dry lips.”** This is the single most common misconception — held by patients and sometimes by clinicians. The tell is that true angular cheilitis sits in the corners and doesn’t resolve with lip balm; if your “chapped lips” won’t heal at the corners, it’s worth a second look.[^7]

**Nutritional deficiency in a well-fed population.** It’s tempting to assume deficiency is a developing-world problem. But subclinical deficiency is real even in wealthy countries, driven by poor absorption (low stomach acid with aging or PPI use, celiac or Crohn’s disease, bariatric surgery), restrictive diets, and aging. PPIs reduce the stomach acid needed to release B12 from food, and reviews have found changes in B12 biomarkers (elevated homocysteine and methylmalonic acid) in long-term users — though studies are not unanimous.[^35] B12 deficiency affects an estimated 5–15% of older adults, often due to food-bound malabsorption rather than low intake.[^35]

**The plant-based debate.** Is angular cheilitis more common in vegetarians and vegans? The honest answer: the risk runs through specific nutrients (B12 above all, plus iron and zinc), and it’s driven by planning and supplementation, not by plant foods being inherently deficient. Well-supplemented vegans have B12 status comparable to omnivores.[^28] The solution is straightforward: reliable B12, attention to iron and zinc absorption, and fortified foods.[^29]

**Oral microbiome dysbiosis as an emerging factor.** Newer research frames oral *Candida* problems as partly a matter of microbial balance — *Candida* interacts with oral bacteria, and an imbalanced (dysbiotic) microbiome with reduced diversity may let yeast switch from harmless resident to invader.[^36] This is a promising lens that helps explain why probiotics show benefit, but the specific mechanisms in angular cheilitis remain incompletely understood — interesting and plausible, not yet settled.[^37]

**OTC antifungal self-treatment: practical reality vs. clinical concern.** For an otherwise healthy adult with classic, mild, two-sided angular cheilitis, a short course of an OTC antifungal (like clotrimazole or miconazole) plus a barrier ointment and good denture/lip hygiene is reasonable. The concerns are real, though: self-treating the *wrong* cause can prolong things, repeated empiric antimicrobial use can foster resistance, and steroid-containing creams used alone can backfire. The sensible rule: try basic self-care for 2–3 weeks, but see a clinician if it doesn’t clear, recurs often, is one-sided, bleeds, or comes with other symptoms.[^15][^16]

### 10. Key facts worth sharing

- **Candida is detectable in about 93% of angular cheilitis lesions** — but it’s the sole cause in only 20–50%, which is why this is usually a mixed-microbe problem.[^1]
- **Up to 25% of cases are linked to iron or B-vitamin deficiency** — your mouth corners can be an early-warning system.[^9]
- **80% of treated patients relapse over ~5 years** if root causes aren’t addressed.[^33]
- **Denture wearers face up to ~28% prevalence**, roughly triple the risk.[^2]
- **Recurrent angular cheilitis without dentures is a red flag** that should prompt screening for diabetes or HIV.[^15]
- **Lip balm alone won’t fix it** — if it’s truly angular cheilitis, the corners need barrier protection plus treatment of the infection and cause.[^7]
- **Glycemic control measurably reduces recurrence** in people with diabetes — HbA1c tracks with disease frequency.[^8]
- **Smoking makes the mouth more susceptible to Candida** by suppressing local immune defenses.[^31]

### 11. When to seek professional help

**See a clinician if:**

- The lesion lasts beyond 2–3 weeks despite sensible self-care.[^15]
- It bleeds on its own, is deeply fissured, or is getting worse.[^1]
- It’s on one side only and not responding (rule out trauma, herpes, syphilitic lesion, or — importantly — malignancy).[^1]
- It keeps coming back, especially if you don’t wear dentures (screen for diabetes, HIV).[^15]
- You have other symptoms suggesting systemic disease (unexplained fatigue, weight loss, diarrhea, dry eyes/mouth).[^15]

**Who to see and what to ask for:**

- **Primary care clinician** — first stop; ask about cultures/swabs and blood work (CBC with MCV, ferritin and iron studies, B12 and folate, B2/B6, zinc, fasting glucose/HbA1c).[^15]
- **Dentist or prosthodontist** — for denture fit and disinfection, or bite/vertical-dimension problems.[^8]
- **Dermatologist or oral medicine/oral pathology specialist** — for severe, chronic, or treatment-resistant cases, or anything suspicious for cancer.[^8]
- **Registered dietitian** — to address confirmed deficiencies and build a sustainable eating plan, especially for plant-based or restricted diets.[^8]

**Urgent red flags:** a non-healing one-sided lesion (possible oral cancer), signs of significant immune compromise, or systemic symptoms all warrant prompt evaluation rather than continued self-treatment.[^1]

### 12. A note on whole-person health

It’s striking that something as small as a cracked corner of the mouth can reflect so much about the whole body — what we eat, how we sleep, the medicines we take, the conditions we carry, even how well our days are balanced between effort and rest. The body is constantly repairing itself, and it does that best when it has what it needs: real nourishment, adequate rest, manageable stress, and the steadiness that comes from being cared for and connected to others.

Healing the corners of your mouth, then, is partly practical — a cream, a better-fitting denture, an iron supplement — and partly about tending the conditions that let your body do what it already wants to do. Small signs are worth listening to. Often they’re an invitation to care for the whole person, gently and consistently.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Sensible self-care (first 2–3 weeks), for mild, classic, two-sided cases:**

- Apply a barrier ointment (petroleum jelly or zinc oxide) to the corners several times daily, especially before sleep.
- Use an OTC antifungal (clotrimazole or miconazole) twice daily.
- Stop lip-licking; if you wear dentures, clean them daily, remove them at night, and disinfect them overnight.
- *Threshold to advance:* no clear improvement within 2–3 weeks, or any worsening.

**Stage 2 — Clinical evaluation:**

- See a primary care clinician for an exam, swabs/cultures, and targeted blood work (CBC with MCV, ferritin/iron studies, B12, folate, B2, B6, zinc, fasting glucose/HbA1c).
- Get the right prescription for the right organism — antifungal, antibacterial, or a combined antifungal/low-potency steroid — rather than guessing.
- *Threshold to advance:* persistence despite correct treatment, frequent recurrence, or abnormal labs.

**Stage 3 — Root-cause and specialist care:**

- Correct any confirmed deficiency with clinician-guided repletion, and re-test to confirm correction.
- Optimize blood sugar if diabetic; review medications (PPIs, steroids, retinoids) with your clinician.
- Refer as needed: dentist/prosthodontist (dentures), dermatologist or oral medicine (resistant/suspicious lesions), dietitian (nutrition).
- For chronic relapsers, adopt maintenance prevention: regular barrier ointment, intermittent antifungal use, denture hygiene, and consider xylitol gum in older adults.
- *Red-flag override:* any one-sided, non-healing, or spontaneously bleeding lesion → prompt evaluation to rule out malignancy, regardless of stage.

## Caveats

- Angular cheilitis is multifactorial; no single cause or cure fits everyone, and the most effective plans address both surface and source.
- The treatment evidence specific to angular cheilitis (as opposed to oral candidiasis generally) is older and limited in scale — much of it traces to a small body of work from the 1980s — so many recommendations are extrapolated from related oral *Candida* conditions.
- “Emerging” remedies (probiotics, honey, coconut oil) have plausible mechanisms and some supportive lab or related-condition data, but lack robust trials *in angular cheilitis itself*; they are reasonable adjuncts, not replacements for proven care.
- Nutrition guidance here is general; supplement doses and testing should be individualized with a clinician, since excesses (iron, zinc, vitamin A) carry their own risks.
- This guide is educational and not a substitute for personal medical advice. Persistent, one-sided, or bleeding lesions always warrant professional evaluation.
