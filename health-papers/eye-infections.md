Most eye infections are minor and clear up with time and gentle care. A small number can threaten sight within days, so the most useful skill is learning to tell the two groups apart.

## A Morning Many Families Know

It's 6:45 on a school morning. Your seven-year-old is standing in the bedroom doorway with one eye squeezed shut, the lashes glued together with yellow crust. Across town, a college student who has been stretching her monthly contact lenses "one more week" notices a scratchy eye. By lunch it hurts, the office lights feel painfully bright, and her vision looks smeared. She figures it's allergies.

Both have a "red eye," and that is about all they share. The child most likely has ordinary conjunctivitis, or pink eye, which usually settles within one to two weeks with patience, clean washcloths, and handwashing.[^1] The student may be developing an infection of the cornea, the clear front window of the eye, which eye specialists treat as an emergency because it can scar vision within days.[^2]

This guide is for anyone with a sore eye right now, and for the parents, caregivers, and friends helping them decide what to do next.

## How the Eye Protects Itself

Every blink works like a windshield wiper, sweeping dust and microbes toward a small drain in the inner corner. The tear film carries lysozyme, which breaks down bacterial walls, lactoferrin, which starves bacteria of iron, and antibodies called IgA that tag invaders for removal.[^1] Oil glands along the lid edge, called meibomian glands, add a layer that slows evaporation, and harmless microbes on the eye surface help crowd out troublemakers.[^2]

The conjunctiva is the thin, clear membrane covering the white of the eye and lining the lids. It is rich in blood vessels and immune cells, and when it is inflamed those vessels swell, turning the eye pink.[^1] The cornea has no blood vessels, so it relies on tears and a tightly sealed outer layer of cells. Once that layer is scratched, dried out, or starved of oxygen, as under a lens worn overnight, bacteria, amoebas, fungi, and viruses can invade. Infection of the cornea is called keratitis.[^2] That is why contact lenses, dry eye, eyelid inflammation, and diabetes all raise the risk of serious infection.[^2]

When the U.S. Food and Drug Administration warned companies about unapproved eye products in 2023, it said that drugs applied to the eyes "bypass some of the body's natural defenses."[^3] Skin has thick layers and a hardy microbial shield, and the eye surface has neither, so sterility matters far more for the eye than for a scraped knee.

> **Key Fact:** The FDA has cautioned that products applied to the eye bypass some of the body's natural defenses, which is why sterility and proper formulation matter so much for anything placed on the eye surface.[^3]

## The Main Types, and How They Tend to Feel

### Viral conjunctivitis

Viruses cause most infectious pink eye in adults. Reviews estimate up to about 80% of acute cases are viral, usually from adenovirus, a common cold-type virus.[^1] It often starts in one eye and spreads to the other within days, with watery discharge, grittiness, and often a sore throat or runny nose. A classic clue is a small, tender lymph node just in front of the ear.[^1] It typically lasts one to three weeks, and the JAMA review by Azari and Barney describes a contagious period of roughly 10 to 14 days.[^1]

> **Key Fact:** Adenoviral pink eye can stay contagious for roughly 10 to 14 days, much longer than the "three to seven days" people often assume, so careful handwashing and not sharing towels matter for the whole illness.[^1]

### Bacterial conjunctivitis

Bacterial pink eye is more common in children. The Cochrane review team notes that bacteria cause most infectious conjunctivitis in children and about half in adults.[^4] The hallmark is thick yellow or green discharge that keeps returning and lids stuck together in the morning.[^1] It usually clears on its own; the Cochrane team cites an earlier review in which about 65% of cases resolved within two to five days without antibiotics.[^4]

### Gonorrhea and chlamydia in the eye

A small group of eye infections is sexually transmitted. Gonococcal conjunctivitis comes on fast with a flood of pus and can damage the cornea within a day or two, while chlamydial conjunctivitis lingers for weeks. Both need medicine by mouth or injection, not only drops, and partners need treatment too.[^1][^2]

### Allergic and irritant look-alikes

Plenty of "pink eye" isn't infection. Allergic conjunctivitis usually affects both eyes, flares with pollen, pets, or dust, and its signature is itching.[^1] Smoke, chlorine, and cosmetics redden eyes too. When the pollen count drops or the irritant is gone, whatever remedy was in use tends to get the credit.

### Why even eye doctors often guess

Clinicians can't reliably tell viral from bacterial conjunctivitis by appearance, and in studies Azari and Barney reviewed, such judgments often didn't match lab results.[^1] A rapid in-office adenovirus test, AdenoPlus, can identify many viral cases in minutes and help avoid unneeded antibiotics.[^1]

### Eyelid troubles: styes, chalazia, and blepharitis

A stye, or hordeolum, is a painful red bump at the lid edge from an infected oil gland or lash follicle. A chalazion is a firmer, usually painless lump from a clogged gland. Blepharitis is ongoing inflammation of the lid margins, with crusty lashes and burning, and most styes and chalazia settle over days to weeks.[^2] One cause of blepharitis now gets more attention: Demodex, tiny mites living in lash follicles. A study sponsored by Tarsus Pharmaceuticals, maker of a prescription mite treatment, reviewed the charts of 1,032 patients at six U.S. eye clinics and found signs of Demodex blepharitis in 58% of patients visiting for any reason.[^5]

### Keratitis: when the cornea is infected

Keratitis usually brings real pain, strong light sensitivity, blurred vision that doesn't clear with blinking, and a feeling that something is stuck in the eye.[^2] In contact lens wearers, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* can cause a corneal ulcer that worsens over hours. Acanthamoeba, an amoeba found in tap water, pools, hot tubs, and soil, causes a rarer but stubborn infection, and a UK study documented an outbreak starting in 2010–2011 with cases about three times higher than before, mostly in lens wearers.[^6] Fungal keratitis often follows a scratch from plant material while gardening.[^2] Herpes simplex keratitis, caused by the cold sore virus, can recur and is a leading infectious cause of corneal blindness in wealthy countries.[^7]

> **Key Fact:** A red eye combined with real pain, strong light sensitivity, or blurred vision that doesn't clear with blinking is not "just pink eye." It needs a same-day eye exam.[^2]

### Herpes and shingles around the eye

Anyone with a history of cold sores who develops a painful red eye should mention it to a clinician right away. Shingles can involve the eye when the rash appears on the forehead, upper lid, or nose, a condition called herpes zoster ophthalmicus. A rash on the tip or side of the nose, known as Hutchinson's sign, strongly suggests the eye is involved and calls for prompt antivirals and an eye exam.[^2]

### Swollen eyelids with fever, newborns, and more

Preseptal cellulitis is an infection of the eyelid skin in front of the eye. Orbital cellulitis is an infection behind the eye's protective barrier, often spreading from the sinuses, causing pain with eye movement, bulging, double vision, and fever; it is most common in children and is an emergency.[^2] Conjunctivitis in the first four weeks of life, called ophthalmia neonatorum, can come from gonorrhea, chlamydia, other bacteria, or herpes passed on at birth, and it always needs a medical visit.[^2] Trachoma, a chlamydial infection spread by hands, cloths, and flies where water is scarce, is a public health problem in 32 countries and, according to the World Health Organization, is responsible for blindness or visual impairment in about 1.9 million people.[^40] Red eyes can also signal a body-wide infection: conjunctivitis is a classic early sign of measles, and COVID-19 sometimes causes mild pink eye.[^2]

## Conventional Care: What It Offers and Where It Falls Short

### Antibiotics for bacterial pink eye

The 2023 Cochrane review pooled 21 trials with 8,805 people and found antibiotic drops or ointments increased the chance of clinical recovery by about 26% over placebo, with moderate-certainty evidence and rare serious complications in either group.[^4] Antibiotics help modestly, but many people recover without them, which is why watchful waiting is reasonable for mild cases.

Two British trials support that approach. Everitt and colleagues compared immediate antibiotics, a delayed prescription to fill only if needed, and no prescription, and found similar symptom duration, with fewer antibiotics used in the delayed and no-prescription groups.[^8] In children, Rose and colleagues found that by day seven, 86% given chloramphenicol drops and 83% given placebo had recovered, and concluded most children get better on their own.[^9] Overuse has costs: each unnecessary course gives bacteria a chance to develop resistance, and products containing neomycin commonly cause allergic lid reactions that can be mistaken for worsening infection.[^2]

### Viral pink eye: still mostly supportive care

No FDA-approved antiviral for adenoviral conjunctivitis was identified for this guide, and cool compresses, lubricating drops, and time remain standard.[^10] The most closely watched attempt to change this combined dilute povidone-iodine, an antiseptic, with the steroid dexamethasone, developed by Shire, later acquired by Takeda. In a phase 2 trial of 144 adults in India, day-six clinical resolution was 31.3% with the combination, 10.9% with vehicle, and 18.0% with povidone-iodine alone.[^10] The main phase 3 adenoviral trial planned about 930 participants but was stopped early after roughly 217 enrolled, and its registry record states the sponsor discontinued development "with reason unrelated to safety"; its reported day-six resolution counts (3 of 32 with the combination versus 1 of 14 with placebo) were too small to show anything.[^11] A companion adenoviral trial was also stopped early, and placebo did numerically better in the small numbers analyzed.[^12] A completed bacterial conjunctivitis trial missed its primary goal, with 50.5% resolving on the combination versus 42.8% on placebo, a difference that wasn't statistically significant.[^13] The promising early result was not confirmed, and no such drop was identified as FDA-approved as of this writing.

### Why steroid drops need an eye exam first

Steroid drops calm inflammation fast, and a leftover bottle is tempting. But steroids can badly worsen herpes simplex keratitis, and in a rabbit model, topical corticosteroids increased adenovirus replication and prolonged shedding.[^14] An eye that feels better on a borrowed steroid may be getting worse underneath. Leftover or borrowed drops, especially steroid-containing ones, should never be used without an exam.

### Herpes eye disease and shingles

The landmark Herpetic Eye Disease Study found that a year of low-dose oral acyclovir cut ocular herpes recurrences from 32% to 19%, making preventive antivirals one of the best-supported tools in eye care.[^7] For shingles of the eye, prompt antiviral pills are standard. The Zoster Eye Disease Study (ZEDS) randomized 527 people to a year of daily valacyclovir or placebo and published in 2025. It missed its primary 12-month goal, but secondary results favored valacyclovir at 18 months and showed fewer repeat episodes, and the investigators concluded the results "support consideration" of a year of suppressive treatment.[^15]

### Demodex blepharitis

In July 2023 the FDA approved lotilaner ophthalmic solution 0.25% (Xdemvy), the first drug for Demodex blepharitis, based on the Saturn-1 and Saturn-2 trials. Saturn-2 randomized 412 patients and found lotilaner superior to vehicle at six weeks.[^5] Both trials were sponsored by the manufacturer, Tarsus Pharmaceuticals, and several authors disclosed financial ties to it.[^5]

### Contact lens keratitis and eyelid cellulitis

Contact lens keratitis is treated urgently, usually with frequent strong antibiotic drops after the cornea is cultured, because *Pseudomonas* can damage the cornea within a day. Acanthamoeba may need months of treatment and accounts for about half of lens users who suffer sight loss after keratitis.[^6] Orbital cellulitis in children is treated with intravenous antibiotics and sometimes surgery, because delay risks vision and spread of infection.[^2]

### School and daycare exclusion

Many families are told a child can't return until they've "been on antibiotics for 24 hours." Guidance summarized by Children's Mercy Kansas City notes there is no supporting evidence for excluding children with pink eye who have no fever or behavior change, reserving exclusion mainly for outbreaks.[^16] A survey of state health departments found wide variation: of 43 responding states, eight allowed return once antibiotics started and twelve required 24 hours of treatment.[^17] Rules like these push clinicians to prescribe antibiotics that may do little for viral cases.

## Comfort and Home Care That Has Good Support

### Compresses, cool and warm

A clean cloth soaked in cool water over closed lids is a well-established comfort measure for viral and allergic conjunctivitis, easing swelling and itch at almost no risk.[^1] Warm compresses are the usual first step for styes, chalazia, and blepharitis, softening thickened oil in blocked glands. A Cochrane review of non-surgical stye treatments found few good trials, so support rests more on physiology and long, low-risk experience than on controlled studies.[^18] Test the warmth on your inner wrist first.

### Lid hygiene

Diluted baby shampoo was the standard lid cleanser for years and does help. In a double-masked trial by Sung and colleagues, both a dedicated eyelid cleanser and diluted baby shampoo improved blepharitis, but the cleanser did better on several measures, and baby shampoo reduced the function of goblet cells, which make the mucus that keeps the eye surface wet.[^19] Hypochlorous acid lid sprays have growing but still limited research. Dedicated cleansers sit in the moderate-evidence range, and baby shampoo helps with some drawbacks.

### Artificial tears, and why single-use vials matter

Lubricating drops ease grittiness from any cause. Preservative-free single-use vials are discarded after one use, limiting contamination, while multi-dose bottles should never touch the eye or be shared. Pharmacy products aren't automatically safe either. In 2022–2023, an extensively drug-resistant *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* strain was linked to contaminated over-the-counter artificial tears, and by May 2023 the CDC had recorded 81 patients in 18 states, with 14 cases of vision loss, four eyes surgically removed, and four deaths.[^20] The question for any eye product is whether it is made and regulated as a sterile product.

### Stopping the spread and resting your lenses

Handwashing is the best-supported way to stop pink eye from spreading, since adenovirus and bacteria travel mainly on hands and shared objects.[^1] Use separate towels, change pillowcases often, wipe down phones and remotes, don't share eye makeup, and throw away mascara and applicators used during an infection. Stop wearing contact lenses during any eye infection, discard the lenses and case you were using, and wait until the eye is fully comfortable or a clinician clears you.[^2] Rest, fluids, and patience with the accompanying cold are part of recovery too.

## Prevention and Everyday Habits

### Contact lens habits

Sleeping in lenses is widely recognized as the leading avoidable risk factor for bacterial keratitis.[^2] For Acanthamoeba, water is the main hazard: the UK outbreak study found poor lens hygiene, water contact, and reusable rather than daily disposable lenses all raised risk, and called for "no water" messages on lens cases.[^6] That means never rinsing lenses or cases in tap water, no swimming or hot tubs in lenses, replacing the case regularly, and never topping off old solution. Costume lenses bought without a prescription carry the same risks with no fitting or instructions.

> **Key Fact:** Contact lens use is a leading cause of corneal infection in otherwise healthy eyes, and in the UK outbreak study, water exposure and reusable lenses were among the main risks for Acanthamoeba keratitis, one of the most sight-threatening of these infections.[^6]

### Makeup, lashes, the garden, and vaccines

Replace mascara every few months, never share eye cosmetics, and remove makeup before sleep. Eyelash extensions and adhesives have been linked to irritation, allergy, and lid infections, so get painful lids checked. Wear safety glasses when pruning or clearing brush, because fungal keratitis often starts with a plant scratch.[^2] The CDC recommends two doses of recombinant shingles vaccine for adults 50 and older and for adults 19 and older with weakened immune systems, and preventing shingles prevents shingles of the eye.[^21] Good blood sugar control supports corneal healing and immunity, smoking dries and irritates the eye surface, and adequate sleep supports immunity, though sleeping in lenses does the opposite.[^2]

### Nutrition, honestly weighed

Vitamin A is the clearest nutrition story in eye health. Where people are deficient, lack of it causes xerophthalmia, a drying and scarring of the eye surface, and makes measles far more dangerous, so its role there is well established. For most people the best sources are foods such as sweet potatoes, carrots, dark leafy greens, and orange fruits. Zinc is often promoted for eye infections, but the evidence is limited. Omega-3s show why honest reporting matters: long recommended for dry eye, which can make the surface more vulnerable to infection, they did no better than an olive oil placebo over a year in the large DREAM trial.[^22] That is "studied and not found better than placebo," which differs from "never studied." A varied, plant-forward diet supports general health, but no eating pattern has been shown to prevent eye infections directly.

> **Key Fact:** In the DREAM trial of 535 people with dry eye, omega-3 supplements worked no better than an olive oil placebo. It's a useful reminder that a sensible-sounding idea still has to be tested.[^22]

### Herpes recurrence triggers and L-lysine

Many people with ocular herpes are told that stress sets off flare-ups. The Herpetic Eye Disease Study tracked 308 people with weekly diaries and did not find psychological stress predictive of recurrence, nor sunlight, illness, contact lens wear, or menstrual periods.[^23] Stress still matters for well-being, but self-blame for a flare-up isn't warranted. L-lysine is widely promoted for herpes; evidence for oral cold sores is limited and mixed, and for eye herpes it has essentially not been studied. It shouldn't replace clinician-recommended antivirals, which are proven to reduce eye recurrences.[^7]

## Traditional and Natural Remedies, Evaluated in Context

A popular natural-remedy website built on reader testimonials offers dozens of pink eye suggestions. Its "top 10" list includes black, green, and chamomile tea, coffee, apple cider vinegar, honey, colloidal silver, coconut oil, castor oil, and activated charcoal, presented as alternatives to antibiotics, especially for viral cases.[^24] Readers also report sea salt, boric acid, lemon juice, Lugol's iodine, chlorine dioxide, raw milk, saliva, urine, onion vapor, ozone drops, and eyebright. The site's general advice, including handwashing, fresh pillowcases, a warm washcloth for discharge, and seeing a doctor for fever or vision changes, matches mainstream guidance, as does its statement that antibiotics don't work on viruses.

These testimonials are real experiences and deserve respect. Still, eye infections are easy to misjudge. Most viral and many bacterial cases improve on their own. People reach for a remedy when symptoms peak, so improvement tends to follow whatever they try, a pattern statisticians call regression to the mean. Allergic eyes get mistaken for infected ones, and comfort isn't the same as clearing an infection. When a story describes antibiotics "failing" before a home remedy "worked," that is exactly what you'd expect if the infection was viral and ending on schedule.

Each remedy below was weighed by asking whether it touches only the closed lid or goes onto the eye surface (the most important question), whether it is sterile, whether it is caustic or gritty, whether it can trigger allergy, what studies show, how much benefit could be natural recovery or comfort, and what harm follows if it delays recognizing keratitis, herpes, or a newborn infection.

### Tea bag compresses: black, green, and chamomile

In laboratory studies, green tea catechins blocked adenovirus infection in cell cultures, a genuine clue, though a lab dish isn't an eye and no clinical trials of tea compresses were found.[^25] A clean, cooled tea bag on closed lids acts as a moist, cool compress: low risk and plausibly soothing, with insufficient evidence for comfort and no study of whether it treats infection. Squeezing tea into the eye is different, because home-brewed tea isn't sterile and can carry leaf fragments. Chamomile carries a specific caution: a case series described allergic conjunctivitis after chamomile eyewashes in people sensitive to related plants like ragweed.[^26] Isolated reports also describe corneal ulcers in people who kept using tea bags, which illustrates the real risk of delay. Coffee, also on the list, has no studies for eye infections, and hot liquid near the eye risks burns, especially for children.

### Apple cider vinegar

The website suggests heavy dilution, but some testimonials describe full-strength vinegar in the eye. Vinegar is acetic acid, which can chemically injure the eye surface, and raw vinegar's "mother" isn't sterile. The sting that readers interpret as "working" is the eye signaling injury. Apple cider vinegar on the eye surface is actively discouraged.

### Honey: from the kitchen to the clinic

Honey shows a folk idea being properly studied. Kitchen or raw honey isn't sterile, can carry bacterial spores, and must never be given to infants. Medical-grade manuka honey made for the eye is another matter: a randomized trial by Albietz and Schmid found manuka honey eye products improved dry eye from meibomian gland dysfunction,[^27] and Craig and colleagues found a manuka honey lid cream helped blepharitis.[^28] These products sit in the emerging-evidence range for lid and dry eye conditions, not as treatment for infectious conjunctivitis.

### Coconut oil and castor oil

Lauric acid in coconut oil kills some microbes in the lab, but no conjunctivitis trials were found; it is low risk on closed lids and unstudied for clearing infection. Castor oil is an ingredient in some pharmaceutical eye emulsions, and a review by Sandford and colleagues concluded it has therapeutic potential for blepharitis and meibomian gland dysfunction, with evidence still developing.[^29] Sterile pharmaceutical formulations are very different from dropping kitchen or cosmetic castor oil into an infected eye.

### Activated charcoal: poultices and "slurry" drops

Charcoal has a long history in hydrotherapy, and a widely used home-remedies handbook by Agatha and Calvin Thrash describes charcoal poultices and even filtered charcoal "slurry" as eye drops.[^41] A warm, moist poultice over closed lids works as a compress and may feel soothing, but no evidence was found that charcoal "draws out" infection through lid skin. The poultice sits in the insufficient range and is low risk if gentle, clean, and not sealed tightly. Eye specialists advise against patching eyes with contact lens–related or bacterial corneal problems, because a warm, closed space can favor germs, so an overnight occlusive dressing over an infected eye is a specific concern.[^2] One reader strained the slurry repeatedly and found it still black, which shows the problem with drops: fine particles act like grit on the cornea, and home preparations aren't sterile. Charcoal slurry drops are actively discouraged. A testimonial calling charcoal "10 times more effective than antibiotics" in a remote village is sincere but uncontrolled, and eye outbreaks in such settings are often trachoma or epidemic viral conjunctivitis, which wax and wane on their own.

### Salt water, boric acid, and baby shampoo

Sterile saline from a sealed pharmacy container is fine for rinsing. Homemade salt water is different: tap water can carry Acanthamoeba, a serious concern for lens wearers,[^6] wrong concentrations irritate, and boiled water doesn't stay sterile once stored. Boric acid appears at low concentrations in some approved eyewashes, but homemade solutions risk wrong strengths and contamination, and the powder is poisonous if a child swallows it. Both homemade preparations are discouraged on the eye surface. As for baby shampoo, online warnings of permanent eye damage go further than the research, which shows irritation and short-term surface changes rather than lasting harm.[^19]

### Iodine: same idea, very different products

Dilute povidone-iodine is used by eye surgeons and has been tested in trials for conjunctivitis, including an earlier pediatric study by Isenberg and colleagues.[^42] Lugol's iodine, a mix of elemental iodine and potassium iodide, was never made for the eye and can be caustic. The folk idea is sound, but the home version is unsafe, and Lugol's iodine in the eye is actively discouraged.

### Eyebright and breast milk

Stoss and colleagues followed 65 people using single-dose Euphrasia (eyebright) drops and reported most recovered fully, but with no comparison group, that design can't separate remedy from time.[^30] Eyebright sits in the limited-evidence range, and only sterile, pharmaceutically prepared single-dose products should be considered. Breast milk is a widely shared remedy for babies' sticky eyes. In a laboratory study, Baynham and colleagues found human milk inhibited some common conjunctivitis bacteria but not others, and they did not consider it a substitute for proper treatment.[^31] Clinical evidence is limited and mixed, and a newborn with eye discharge in the first four weeks needs medical evaluation first.

### Saliva, urine, raw milk, chlorine dioxide, and ozone

Saliva can carry herpes simplex virus and mouth bacteria straight to the eye, and urine and raw milk aren't sterile. All three are actively discouraged. Chlorine dioxide is sold as "MMS." In a 2019 warning, then–acting FDA Commissioner Ned Sharpless said that taking these products "is the same as drinking bleach." It should never go on or near the eye. Ozonated-oil eyelid formulations have emerging European research for lid conditions, but home ozone dripped into the eye is not the same thing and has not been studied.

### When the pharmacy shelf isn't the dividing line

In September 2023, the FDA sent warning letters to eight companies, including CVS Health and Walgreens, for marketing unapproved eye products for conditions like pink eye, some citing sterility problems and several labeled to contain silver.[^3] The real dividing line isn't natural versus pharmacy but whether a product is regulated and sterile.

## Colloidal Silver: A Closer Look

### What people report

Readers describe putting silver drops in the eye for a few days, some reporting clearing within hours to two days, sometimes after antibiotics "failed."[^24] Products mentioned range from about 10 to 500 parts per million (ppm), including homemade silver and mixtures with honey, yogurt, or vinegar. One reader's eye swelled and worsened after repeated drops, which another commenter attributed to "die-off." Several users advise stopping after about two days, and one describes a family member whose vision blurred after overuse and recovered within days. The site hosting these stories also sells its own colloidal silver product,[^24] the same kind of financial interest this guide notes for drug companies.

### Silver's biology is real

Silver does kill microbes. In laboratory work, silver nanoparticles damaged adenovirus type 3 particles and inhibited infection in cell culture.[^32] In a separate cell-culture study of the COVID-19 virus, particles about 10 nanometers across neutralized the virus at 1 to 10 ppm, while toxicity to cells appeared at 20 ppm and above.[^33] That was a different virus in a lab dish and can't be applied directly to eyes, but it shows how narrow the gap between helpful and harmful can be, and why particle size, concentration, and formulation matter.

### What users noticed, and the "die-off" idea

When readers describe silver "taking off cells" of the eye, or blurring after heavy use, they may be seeing a real, dose-dependent effect on the corneal surface, consistent with lab findings that silver harms cells at higher concentrations. The Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction is real, but it is a whole-body reaction with fever and chills, seen mainly when treating spirochete infections like syphilis, and it isn't known to explain a worsening red eye. Calling rising pain or swelling "die-off" can lead someone to keep applying an irritant or miss early keratitis. A worsening eye is a reason to stop and be seen.

### Why silver's reputation isn't baseless

In the early 1880s, the German obstetrician Carl Credé began placing silver nitrate drops in newborns' eyes. According to Schaller and Klauss, within a few years gonococcal eye infection fell from 10% of births to 0.3%.[^34] Silver nitrate also caused chemical irritation and was largely replaced by gentler antibiotic ointments like erythromycin. Mild silver protein drops were common in the early 1900s, and silver is still built into some antimicrobial contact lens cases.

> **Key Fact:** Credé's silver nitrate eye drops, introduced in the early 1880s, dramatically reduced newborn blindness from gonorrhea, but they irritated the eye and were largely replaced by gentler antibiotic ointments.[^34]

### Long-term deposits, regulation, and research

Repeated use over months or years can leave silver deposits in eye tissue, called ocular argyrosis, and the FDA notes granular deposits in the conjunctiva and cornea may reduce night vision.[^3] In 1999, the FDA ruled that over-the-counter colloidal silver products are not recognized as safe and effective for any disease,[^35] and several products in the 2023 warning letters were labeled to contain silver.[^3] No controlled human trials of colloidal silver for conjunctivitis were found, so the question is essentially unstudied, which differs from having been studied and found ineffective.

### Where silver lands

Silver has a plausible antimicrobial mechanism, no human clinical evidence for conjunctivitis, unstandardized products not made for the eye, credible signals of surface toxicity (including from users themselves), and the risk of delaying recognition of a serious infection. The fairest label is insufficient evidence with credible harm signals, and it is not recommended as self-treatment on the eye surface. People who have used it haven't been foolish; they have been working with an idea that was never properly tested for this use.

## Other Contested Topics

### Newborn eye prophylaxis

In the U.S., newborns routinely receive erythromycin ointment to prevent gonococcal eye infection. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force reaffirmed this in 2019, finding substantial benefit and small harms, and many states require it by law with varying provisions for parents who decline.[^36] Some families question it, especially in natural-birth communities. In 2015 the Canadian Paediatric Society stopped recommending universal prophylaxis, arguing that gonococcal eye infection had become rare, erythromycin's effectiveness is uncertain, and screening and treating pregnant women works better.[^37] That statement has since been retired from the society's website, so families should ask about current local practice. Credible bodies disagree here, but they share one non-negotiable point: any newborn eye redness or discharge needs urgent evaluation, whatever was given at birth.

> **Key Fact:** Eye discharge or redness in a baby under four weeks old always needs a medical visit, promptly and regardless of whether the baby received eye ointment at birth.[^2]

### Vitamin A and measles

The World Health Organization recommends vitamin A for children with measles, especially where deficiency is common, to reduce eye damage and blindness. During the 2025 U.S. measles outbreaks, Covenant Children's Hospital in Lubbock, Texas, reported fewer than 10 cases in unvaccinated children who had been hospitalized for measles complications and also showed signs of vitamin A toxicity, including abnormal liver function. Its chief medical officer said the children were probably not taking the vitamin under a doctor's supervision.[^38] Vitamin A given under medical supervision can reduce measles complications but does not prevent infection; the measles-mumps-rubella vaccine is the tool designed to prevent it.

### Tea tree oil and lotilaner for Demodex

Tea tree oil is the closest thing to a natural-product success story in eye care. It kills Demodex mites in the lab and became a mainstay of lid treatment. A 2020 Cochrane review found its benefit uncertain, rated very low certainty for reducing mites, and noted that lower concentrations may be preferable to avoid eye irritation.[^39] It can sting and inflame the eye surface, which is why products use carefully formulated concentrations and eye use should be clinician-guided. Lotilaner has stronger trial evidence but was tested by its maker and costs more.[^5]

### Hydrotherapy, antibiotic pressure, and "terrain"

Warm and cool compresses have real value for comfort and lid conditions,[^18] but claims outrun evidence when fomentations are said to "draw out" corneal infection, which needs targeted treatment. If a workplace or daycare demands an antibiotic, you might say, "Our doctor said this looks viral and antibiotics won't help. Could you accept a note instead?"[^16] Some wellness traditions emphasize the body's "terrain" over the germ. The reasonable part is real: dry eye, lens habits, blood sugar, and immune status shape who gets infected.[^2] But *Pseudomonas*, Acanthamoeba, gonorrhea, and herpes can damage a healthy, well-nourished eye quickly.

## Special Situations

Newborns and infants with any eye redness or discharge in the first 28 days need a same-day visit.[^2] For school-age children, most pink eye is mild, and a child without fever who feels well can usually stay in school with good hand hygiene, but eyelid swelling with fever or pain moving the eye needs emergency care.[^16] Older adults have more dry eye and slower immune responses, and a very painful red eye with halos or nausea may be glaucoma.[^2] Contact lens wearers with a painful red eye should remove their lenses, get seen today, and bring the lenses and case. People with diabetes or weakened immune systems should seek care early, because infections can move faster and heal slower.[^2] People with cold sores or past eye herpes need a prompt exam for a new painful red eye, should never use leftover steroid drops, and can ask whether preventive antivirals make sense.[^7] During pregnancy, check with a clinician before any medicated drops or pills, and keep essential oils like tea tree oil away from the eyes. After eye surgery or injections, rising pain, redness, or blurring can signal endophthalmitis, a rare but serious infection inside the eye, so call the surgeon the same day.[^2] For travelers and international service workers, trachoma persists in parts of Africa, the Middle East, Asia, and Latin America; the WHO's SAFE strategy combines Surgery, Antibiotics, Facial cleanliness, and Environmental improvement, and handwashing, personal towels, and never using tap water for lens care all help.[^40]

## If You're Helping Someone Else

A helper can notice warning signs, encourage timely care, arrange a ride or appointment, support hygiene and comfort measures, and share honest information about home remedies. It helps to bring key details to the visit: contact lens use, cold sore or eye herpes history, recent colds, surgery, or injury, and what has already been tried. A helper shouldn't suggest a diagnosis, recommend or share eye drops, or encourage a remedy while the eye is getting worse.

You might say, "That sounds really uncomfortable. Since you're having trouble with the light and your lenses, this is one of those times an eye doctor should look today. Want help finding who's open?" For someone reaching for home comfort, try, "A cool tea bag on closed lids is probably fine for comfort, but I'd keep anything homemade out of the eye itself, and if it isn't clearly better in a few days, or it starts to hurt, let's get it checked." With a friend who trusts silver, "I know the drops helped you before, but this time the eye is getting worse, and that's the signal to stop and have someone look." And with new parents, "Any eye goo in a new baby is worth a call. It's probably nothing serious, but newborn eyes are one place we don't wait."

## A Brief Reflection

Sight is a gift most of us don't notice until it's threatened. Caring for our eyes, and helping others care for theirs, is one way of looking after the bodies we've been given. Seeking skilled help and using gentle home comforts work together, and neither signals a lack of faith or independence. There is quiet kindness in holding a warm cloth for a feverish child through a long night, and patience in letting an eye heal on its own timetable.

## Closing: Gentle Care and Sharp Eyes

Most red, sticky, sore eyes get better with time, clean hands, compresses, and patience. Know the handful of warning signs: real pain, light sensitivity, blurred vision, a lens wearer's painful eye, any newborn's eye discharge, shingles near the eye, and swollen lids with fever. When one appears, get the eye seen that day. Keep anything non-sterile, acidic, or gritty off the eye surface. When in doubt, have it checked, because eyes don't always give second chances. In the great majority of cases, the eye heals completely.
