## A Quiet Epidemic You May Not Have Noticed

Here is a number worth sitting with: by the year 2050, roughly half the world's population is expected to be nearsighted.[^1] That's nearly five billion people squinting at things that are more than a few feet away. And one in ten people globally — approximately 925 million — is projected to have *high* myopia, a degree of nearsightedness serious enough to substantially raise the risk of permanent vision loss.[^2]

This is not a slow drift. Within a single generation, myopia rates have doubled in many countries. Something has changed — and fast. Genetic mutations don't spread across a population in thirty years. This is a lifestyle and environment story, and understanding it may be one of the most useful things you can do for your eyes or the eyes of a child you love.

Myopia — also called nearsightedness or shortsightedness — simply means that nearby objects look clear, while things at a distance appear blurry. The word comes from the Greek for "to close the eye," which is exactly what many nearsighted people instinctively do when trying to focus. It is the most common refractive error in the world, and its prevalence is growing fastest among children and adolescents, particularly in urban areas and East Asia.[^3]

> **Key Fact**
> Global myopia prevalence among children and adolescents climbed from 24% in 1990 to nearly 36% in 2023 — an increase of nearly 50% in three decades. In some East Asian countries, up to 80–90% of high school graduates are nearsighted.[^4]

The picture is especially striking in East Asia, where urbanization, intensive schooling, and reduced outdoor time have combined to create myopia rates that would have been unimaginable two generations ago.[^5] But it is not only an East Asian story. Rates are rising in Europe, North America, and across much of the developing world.[^6]

> **Key Fact**
> High myopia — a prescription of −6.00 diopters or worse — carries dramatically elevated risks of retinal detachment, glaucoma, and permanent vision loss. These risks don't disappear after corrective surgery. Understanding this distinction matters long before a prescription reaches that threshold.

## What Is Actually Happening Inside the Eye

Understanding myopia means understanding the eyeball itself — because the real story isn't just about blurry vision. It's about what happens to an eye that grows in the wrong direction.

A normal eye is roughly spherical, about 23 to 24 millimeters from front to back. When parallel light rays enter the eye and land precisely on the retina at the back, you see clearly. In a myopic eye, the eyeball has grown too long — stretched from front to back like a slightly elongated ball. That extra length means light focuses in front of the retina rather than on it, and distant objects blur.[^7]

This is called *axial myopia*, and it is the most common form. A less common form, *refractive myopia*, involves the cornea or lens being too curved, bending light too sharply. Both produce the same result — blurry distance vision — but axial elongation is the form that raises long-term health concerns.

Myopia is classified by severity using diopters, the units used to measure lens power:

- **Low myopia:** −0.50 to −3.00 diopters
- **Moderate myopia:** −3.00 to −6.00 diopters
- **High myopia:** −6.00 diopters or worse

This distinction matters for more than just prescription strength. The farther into the high-myopia range a person progresses, the greater the mechanical strain on the internal structures of the eye.[^8]

> **Key Fact**
> Glasses and contact lenses correct the optical error — they bend light so it lands on the retina. But they do not stop the eye from continuing to elongate. A child who starts wearing glasses at age seven will typically keep developing more myopia year after year unless something intervenes at the level of growth, not just vision correction.

When an eyeball grows too long, the retina, blood vessels, and connective tissue that line its interior are stretched thin over a larger surface area. Think of pulling a rubber glove over a larger object — the material gets thinner and more fragile.[^9] This explains why high myopia raises the risk of:

- **Retinal detachment** — the retina pulling away from the underlying tissue, a medical emergency. People with high myopia face roughly 12 times the retinal detachment risk compared to those without myopia.[^10]
- **Glaucoma** — elevated intraocular pressure or structural damage to the optic nerve. For each additional diopter of myopia, glaucoma risk increases by approximately 20%.[^11]
- **Myopic maculopathy** — degenerative changes to the macula, the central part of the retina responsible for sharp, detailed vision.[^12]
- **Cataracts** — clouding of the lens, occurring earlier in people with high myopia.

None of these complications are corrected by LASIK or other refractive surgeries. Surgery reshapes the cornea to fix the optical problem, but the elongated eyeball remains exactly as elongated as before. The elevated risk follows the person throughout their life. This is one of the most important and underappreciated facts about myopia.

## What's Driving the Epidemic: Causes and Risk Factors

### Genetics: A Real Factor That Doesn't Explain Everything

Myopia clearly runs in families. If one parent is nearsighted, a child's risk increases to roughly 1.5 times that of a child with no myopic parents. If both parents are myopic, that risk roughly triples.[^13] Twin studies show heritability estimates ranging from 60% to 90%, suggesting that genes play a meaningful role in who develops myopia.[^14]

But here is the problem with a purely genetic story: genes don't change in thirty years. The rapid rise in myopia prevalence over a single generation tells us that something in the environment has shifted in a fundamental way. Genes set the susceptibility; environment pulls the trigger.[^15]

The most accurate way to think about it: some people carry a higher genetic load of myopia risk. In prior generations, with different lifestyles, many of those people never developed significant myopia. Today's environment — more indoor time, more close work, less bright outdoor light — is activating that risk in far more people, far earlier.

### Near Work and Screens: A Real Association, With Nuances

The relationship between sustained near work — reading, screen time, close-up tasks — and myopia development is real and well documented.[^16] Children who spend more time reading or on screens tend to develop myopia more often and progress more quickly.

But the causal chain is less clear-cut than it might seem. Is it the near work itself? The sustained focus? The lack of time looking into the distance? Or is it simply that near work keeps children indoors, away from something else that turns out to matter much more?

Researchers have proposed the "accommodation fatigue" hypothesis — the idea that sustained close focusing strains the ciliary muscles inside the eye and somehow signals the eye to grow longer. The "defocus" hypothesis suggests that when the eye is focused up close for long periods, peripheral vision is focused behind the retina, which acts as a growth signal. Both are plausible; neither is definitively proven.[^17] What is clear is that the near-work association exists, even after controlling for outdoor time — though the two are difficult to fully separate in real life.

### Outdoor Time: The Most Surprising and Well-Supported Finding

If you remember nothing else from this document, remember this: **the single most powerful, accessible, low-cost, low-risk intervention for reducing myopia in children is time spent outdoors**.

This finding emerged over the last twenty years and has been replicated across multiple countries and study types. It was, initially, genuinely surprising to researchers. It still surprises most parents.[^18]

The association is strong: children who spend more time outdoors are significantly less likely to develop myopia, and those who are already myopic may progress more slowly. What's remarkable is that the protective effect of outdoor time appears to work *even when near work is not reduced*. It's not about resting the eyes from close work. It's about something in the outdoor environment itself.

> **Key Fact**
> A landmark randomized controlled trial in Guangzhou, China assigned children an extra 40 minutes of outdoor time per school day. After one year, the group with more outdoor time had a significantly lower rate of new-onset myopia than controls. A trial in Taiwan found that 80 minutes of daily outdoor time could reduce myopia incidence by 50%.[^19]

The leading hypothesis involves retinal dopamine. Bright outdoor light — even on an overcast day, outdoor light is typically 10 to 100 times brighter than indoor lighting — triggers the release of dopamine in the retina. Dopamine appears to act as a natural brake on axial elongation, slowing or stopping the eye from growing too long.[^20]

There may also be a role for the broader spectrum of natural light, the way pupils constrict outdoors (increasing depth of focus), and exposure to shorter wavelengths of light. The precise mechanism is still being studied, but the effect is consistent enough that major public health programs have been built around it.

Taiwan's "Tian-Tian 120" initiative — recommending 120 minutes of outdoor time daily for schoolchildren — was implemented nationally in schools. Follow-up analysis showed a reversal of the long-term trend toward declining visual acuity in the years following the program's implementation.[^21] Singapore has implemented similar guidance. These are not small pilot projects — they are national policies responding to robust evidence.

What counts as "outdoor time" for these purposes? The key ingredient appears to be natural light intensity. Sitting by a window does not appear to provide the same benefit as being actually outside, because even bright indoor environments near windows deliver far lower light intensity than being outdoors. Activities range from structured sports to unstructured play to simply being outside — the light is the common thread, not the activity type.[^22]

The generally recommended threshold is approximately **1 to 2 hours per day** of outdoor time for children, especially in the primary school years. This is a straightforward, free, widely available intervention with no meaningful downsides — and it is dramatically underused in most populations where myopia is rising.

> **Key Fact**
> Children who spend fewer than 90 minutes per day outdoors face two to three times the risk of myopia onset compared to those who spend more time outside. The benefit appears dose-dependent: more outdoor time generally means more protection, up to a threshold.[^23]

### Diet and Nutrition: Compelling Hypotheses, Emerging Evidence

The relationship between diet and myopia is one of the more intriguing areas of current research — genuinely interesting, plausible in its mechanisms, but less robustly established than the outdoor-time finding. It deserves honest exploration, not overclaiming.

**The high-glycemic diet hypothesis** dates back to work by Cordain and colleagues in 2002 and has been supported by subsequent research.[^24] The proposed mechanism: diets high in refined carbohydrates and sugars produce repeated spikes in blood glucose and insulin levels. This hyperinsulinemia suppresses insulin-like growth factor binding protein-3 (IGFBP-3) while increasing free insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) — a potent stimulator of cell growth throughout the body, including the sclera, the outer wall of the eyeball. Chronic elevation of IGF-1 may accelerate the scleral growth that underlies axial elongation.[^25]

Several population-level observations lend support to this idea. Myopia rates have risen in parallel with the adoption of Westernized, high-glycemic diets across many cultures. Some research finds associations between high refined carbohydrate intake and myopia risk in children, though the data is not always consistent, and disentangling dietary patterns from the many other lifestyle factors that cluster with them is genuinely difficult.[^26]

This remains a hypothesis with encouraging but not definitive support. What it does suggest is that diets lower in refined carbohydrates and added sugars — which are broadly healthful for other reasons — may be one modifiable factor in the overall picture.

**Lutein and zeaxanthin** are carotenoid pigments concentrated in the macula and known to protect against oxidative damage from light. They are found in leafy greens, eggs, and colorful vegetables. There is emerging evidence — including a recent meta-analysis — suggesting that combined supplementation with lutein and zeaxanthin may have a measurable effect on myopia progression in adolescents, particularly at higher doses and over longer durations.[^27] The evidence is promising but still developing.

**Omega-3 fatty acids** support retinal function and have anti-inflammatory properties relevant to eye health generally.[^28] Direct myopia-specific evidence is limited.

**Vitamin D** has been explored as a potential mediator between outdoor time and myopia — sunlight generates vitamin D in the skin, and vitamin D receptors are present in the eye. Some studies find associations between lower vitamin D levels and higher myopia prevalence, but whether this reflects vitamin D itself or simply the outdoor light that generates it remains unclear.[^29]

None of these dietary factors are anywhere near as well-established as the outdoor-time finding for myopia specifically. But they are broadly supportive of good nutrition and represent a reasonable area of attention — particularly the shift away from high-glycemic diets, which have multiple reasons to be moderated regardless of eye health.

### Sleep and Circadian Rhythm

This is an emerging area of interest. A 2023 systematic review found implications for an association between myopia and poor sleep quality, insufficient sleep duration, and irregular sleep timing in children, though findings across studies have been mixed.[^30]

The proposed mechanism involves circadian disruption: the eye appears to need normal light-dark cycling to regulate healthy growth. Animal studies show that chicks deprived of regular darkness develop more myopia.[^31] Children with myopia have been found to have delayed melatonin timing and lower overall melatonin output compared to non-myopic children.[^32] Artificial light at night, by suppressing melatonin and disrupting circadian rhythms, may interfere with the normal regulatory signals that keep the eye growing at the right pace.

This is not yet a settled science, but it adds to the picture of myopia as a condition sensitive to the broader light environment — not just how much bright light children get during the day, but also how much darkness they get at night.

### Urban vs. Rural: An Important Pattern

Across studies globally, children in urban environments have roughly 2.6 times the risk of myopia compared to children in rural areas.[^33] This gradient has been documented in China, Singapore, Europe, and elsewhere.

The explanation is almost certainly multifactorial: urban children tend to spend less time outdoors, engage in more indoor near work, eat more processed foods, and live in built environments with limited green space. The urban-rural gap is a natural experiment of sorts, and it broadly supports the lifestyle factors covered above.

## Prevention: What Actually Helps

### Well-Established

**Outdoor time** is the most important preventive strategy currently supported by evidence, and it is described in detail above. The recommendation: at least 1 to 2 hours of actual outdoor time daily for children, especially during the primary school years. This is the window when myopia most commonly develops, and the window when intervention has the greatest impact.

**Early eye exams** catch myopia at the stage when intervention is most meaningful. Myopia onset before age 10 is associated with faster progression and higher eventual prescription levels. Pediatric eye exams around ages 3 to 5 — and then annually or biannually during school years — allow practitioners to track eye growth, catch early myopia, and discuss control options before progression becomes advanced.[^34]

A critical point: children often do not know they are not seeing clearly. They have never experienced normal distance vision. Parents and teachers may not notice either. The first school vision screen that reports "20/20" is not the same as a comprehensive exam by an eye care professional using cycloplegic refraction — a technique that relaxes the focusing muscles and gives a more accurate picture of true refractive error.

### Emerging Approaches Growing in Clinical Use

**Orthokeratology (Ortho-K)** involves wearing specially designed rigid gas-permeable contact lenses overnight. The lenses gently reshape the front surface of the cornea during sleep, so the wearer can see clearly during the day without glasses or contacts. But the more significant benefit for children: Ortho-K has been shown to slow the rate of axial elongation — meaning it not only corrects vision but also appears to slow myopia progression.[^35] Multiple studies and clinical reviews support its use in pediatric myopia management, and both FDA-cleared and widely used international versions are available. It requires ongoing professional monitoring and carries some risk of infection if lens hygiene protocols are not followed carefully.

**Low-dose atropine eye drops** (typically 0.01%–0.05% concentration) represent one of the most studied pharmacologic approaches to myopia control in children. Atropine, even at very low concentrations, has been shown in multiple trials and meta-analyses to reduce myopia progression.[^36] A 2024 meta-analysis found that low-dose atropine reduced myopia incidence in at-risk children and slowed the rate of prescription change and axial elongation.[^37] Higher doses work faster but carry more side effects (light sensitivity, difficulty with near vision); low-dose protocols are designed to balance efficacy with tolerability. This requires a prescribing clinician, and protocols are still being refined, but this is increasingly part of routine pediatric eye care.

**Defocus-incorporated soft contact lenses** — including the MiSight 1 day lens (CooperVision) — represent an FDA-approved option for myopia control in children aged 8 to 12. The lens uses a dual-focus optical design that corrects the central prescription while simultaneously creating peripheral defocus signals that appear to slow axial elongation. The pivotal clinical trial showed approximately 59% reduction in myopic progression and 52% reduction in axial elongation over three years compared to standard contact lenses.[^38] Seven-year follow-up data continues to support long-term benefits.

**Multifocal and bifocal spectacle lenses** designed specifically for myopia control (including DIMS technology and similar approaches) have shown more modest but real effects on slowing progression in some trials — generally in the range of 30–50% reduction in axial elongation compared to standard single-vision lenses.[^39] These are currently being adopted in clinical practice.

### Lifestyle and Environmental Adjustments

These are not alternatives to the options above — they're complementary foundations:

The **20-20-20 rule** (every 20 minutes of close work, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds) has modest direct evidence for reducing myopia progression, but it does appear to reduce eye strain and may help interrupt sustained accommodation. The rationale is sound. It costs nothing. It is worth adopting.

**Limiting prolonged near work sessions** without breaks — particularly in young children — is sensible based on what we understand about accommodation and retinal defocus signals, even if direct controlled evidence is limited. Regular movement and postural breaks serve multiple purposes beyond eye health.

**Ensuring adequate reading light** and maintaining comfortable reading distances (at least 30 cm) are basic hygiene practices supported by clinical guidance.

**Screen time guidelines for young children** from the American Academy of Pediatrics suggest avoiding screen use (except video calling) for children under 18 to 24 months, limiting to 1 hour per day for ages 2 to 5, and consistent limits with content monitoring beyond that.[^40] The myopia literature broadly supports limiting intensive near-screen exposure in preschool and early school years, when the eye is developing most rapidly.

### Nutritional and Lifestyle Approaches: Honest Framing

Based on the evidence reviewed:

- Reducing dietary refined carbohydrates and added sugars is a reasonable, broadly healthful step that may reduce one potential driver of myopia progression — the IGF-1 pathway. The direct myopia evidence is emerging rather than definitive, but the general dietary direction is sound.
- Foods rich in lutein and zeaxanthin (dark leafy greens, eggs, corn, orange and yellow peppers) and omega-3 fatty acids (fatty fish, walnuts, flaxseed) support eye health broadly. Emerging evidence on lutein/zeaxanthin and myopia progression specifically is encouraging.
- Adequate sleep, regular outdoor light exposure, and limiting artificial light at night are consistent with the growing picture of myopia as a condition sensitive to the body's circadian environment.

These approaches are worth pursuing as part of overall health — they are not myopia cures, and they should not replace clinical monitoring and intervention. But they represent a sensible lifestyle foundation.

### What Probably Doesn't Help (or Needs Much More Evidence)

**Eye exercises and vision training for structural myopia** occupy contested territory. It is worth being clear about what the evidence shows and what it does not.

The Bates Method, developed in the early 20th century by ophthalmologist William Bates, is based on the idea that mental strain causes muscular tension that distorts the eyeball, and that relaxation exercises can reverse myopia.[^41] Controlled studies have not found that the Bates Method or similar eye exercise approaches change axial length — the fundamental structural change underlying most myopia.[^42] The sclera, the outer coat of the eye, is a rigid structure that cannot be reshaped by extraocular muscle activity. Modern imaging has confirmed this.

That said, the experience of people who practice these techniques deserves respectful attention. Some report subjective improvements in visual clarity. Plausible explanations include: relaxation of accommodative spasm (temporary focusing tension that adds to blurriness), reduced eye strain, improved awareness and interpretation of blurred images, and perhaps a modest pinhole effect from pupil miosis. None of these involve changing the underlying axial length, but they are not meaningless either.

The honest summary: eye exercises have not been shown in peer-reviewed literature to reduce myopia through structural change. They may provide some symptom relief and are not harmful. For people who practice them and feel benefit, respectful acknowledgment of that experience is appropriate — alongside honesty that the mechanism is unlikely to involve actual reversal of the underlying structural change.

Similarly, special diets or supplements claiming to "reverse" adult myopia substantially exceed current evidence. Once axial elongation has occurred, it has not been reversed by any dietary or behavioral intervention studied to date. What is possible — and worth pursuing — is slowing progression, especially in children during the years of active eye development.

## Managing Myopia: Glasses, Contacts, and Surgery

### Standard Glasses and Contacts

Conventional glasses and single-vision contact lenses correct the optical error — they adjust where light focuses so that it lands on the retina. They work well for vision correction but do not slow eye growth or reduce the long-term risks associated with myopia.[^43] This is why standard correction, while necessary, is not sufficient as a strategy in children with progressive myopia.

When choosing vision correction for a myopic child, it is worth explicitly discussing myopia control options with the eye care provider — not assuming that a standard prescription is the complete picture.

### Laser Eye Surgery

LASIK, PRK, and SMILE are refractive surgeries that reshape the cornea using a laser to correct the optical prescription. They are effective for vision correction in adults with stable prescriptions and appropriate corneal thickness. Outcomes are generally excellent when candidates are selected carefully.[^44]

What surgery does not do — and this is genuinely important and frequently misunderstood — is change the shape of the eyeball. The cornea is reshaped; the elongated eyeball remains elongated. All of the elevated risks associated with high myopia — retinal detachment, glaucoma, myopic maculopathy — persist after surgery, because the structural vulnerabilities that cause those risks are in the elongated eye itself, not in the optical prescription.[^45]

A person who undergoes LASIK for −8.00 diopters and achieves 20/20 vision afterward still has a highly myopic eye at elevated risk for retinal and optic nerve complications. They need ongoing monitoring for that reason, just as they did before surgery. This is not a reason to avoid surgery — it can be a life-changing and appropriate choice — but it is a reason to have accurate expectations about what surgery achieves.

Surgery is elective and not appropriate for people whose prescriptions are still changing. It is generally considered for adults with stable prescriptions and adequate corneal tissue. Dry eyes, thin corneas, and certain corneal conditions may be contraindications.

> **Key Fact**
> LASIK corrects how the eye focuses. It does not undo the axial elongation that defines myopia. After surgery, continued monitoring for myopia-related complications — especially for those with prescriptions that were −6.00 or higher — remains as important as before.

### When to See an Eye Care Professional

**For children:** A first comprehensive eye exam around age 3 to 5, and then annually through the school years. Signs that warrant prompt evaluation: squinting, holding objects very close, sitting close to screens or boards, complaints of headaches or eye strain, or teacher reports of difficulty seeing the board.

**For adults with stable myopia:** Regular dilated eye exams — at least every 1 to 2 years, or more frequently for high myopia. The dilation allows the eye care provider to examine the peripheral retina for degenerative changes that can precede retinal detachment.

**Seek immediate care if you experience any of the following:**
- A sudden increase in floaters (spots or strings floating in vision)
- Flashes of light, especially in peripheral vision
- A shadow, curtain, or veil across part of your vision

These are warning signs of retinal detachment, which is a medical emergency. Prompt evaluation and treatment can prevent permanent vision loss. Do not wait. Do not drive yourself if these symptoms are severe.

## Special Considerations

### Children and Teenagers: The Highest-Stakes Population

Myopia onset before age 10, and progression through adolescence, carries the highest risk of eventually reaching high myopia. The younger a child starts, the more time there is for the eye to keep growing — and the higher the final prescription is likely to be.[^46]

This is the population for whom early intervention matters most. A child who develops myopia at age 7 and receives no myopia-control management may end up with a −7.00 or −8.00 prescription by their mid-teens. A child who receives outdoor time guidance, early detection, and appropriate myopia-control measures may end up at −3.00 or −4.00. That difference in final prescription corresponds to a meaningfully different lifetime risk profile for serious eye disease.

Parents who learn their child is myopic should understand: getting glasses is not the end of the conversation. It's the beginning. A conversation with the eye care provider about myopia control — not just vision correction — is appropriate and important.

### Adults with Stable Myopia

Myopia progression typically slows substantially in the mid-to-late twenties as the eye reaches structural maturity. Adults with stable prescriptions are not facing the same trajectory as children with progressive myopia. The lifestyle factors covered in this document — outdoor time, diet, sleep hygiene — remain relevant for general eye health and may contribute to slowing any remaining progression. Regular dilated eye exams for monitoring are appropriate throughout life.

### High Myopia: A Distinct Category

Anyone with a prescription of −6.00 diopters or worse is in a category that warrants more careful, ongoing monitoring regardless of age.[^47] The elevated risks of retinal detachment, glaucoma, myopic maculopathy, and early cataract are real and clinically significant. Regular dilated fundus examination — not just a prescription check — is essential. Any sudden visual changes should be evaluated promptly. An ophthalmologist (not just an optometrist) may be appropriate for ongoing care in severe high myopia.

The good news for adults with already-established high myopia: many of the lifestyle factors described here — particularly the dietary and sleep recommendations — are worthwhile general health investments. And the urgency of outdoor time and myopia control is most critical for the children in their lives.

## Practical Takeaways

**Go outside — really.** The outdoor-time finding is the most actionable, most underused, and most surprising thing in the myopia literature. Two hours of outdoor time per day for children is not a luxury; it is a meaningful protective measure supported by randomized controlled trial evidence and national public health programs. It can be play, a walk, sports, time in a garden — the light is the thing. Indoor light, even near a bright window, does not substitute.

**Catch it early.** Children need comprehensive eye exams — not just school vision screenings. Exams around age 3 to 5, and annually through the school years, allow practitioners to catch myopia at the stage when control strategies work best. Children who squint, sit close to screens, or struggle to see boards need evaluation, not waiting.

**Glasses correct vision. They don't stop the clock.** If your child is diagnosed with myopia, ask specifically about myopia control options — orthokeratology, low-dose atropine, or specialized contact lenses or spectacles designed to slow progression. These conversations are appropriate and important. Standard single-vision glasses are not the only option.

**Diet and lifestyle are foundations, not cures.** Reducing refined carbohydrates and sugars, eating plenty of leafy greens and foods rich in lutein and omega-3s, protecting sleep, and limiting bright artificial light at night are all reasonable and broadly healthful steps. They are not myopia cures — but they support the conditions under which the eye is most likely to develop and age well.

**High myopia needs ongoing monitoring.** If your prescription is −6.00 or higher — whether you have had surgery or not — continue regular dilated eye exams and don't dismiss sudden visual changes. Know the warning signs of retinal detachment and treat them as the emergency they are.

**This is a conversation for an eye care professional.** The goal of this document is to help you understand the landscape and ask better questions — not to replace clinical guidance. Myopia management is a field that has changed significantly in recent years, and an eye care provider who is current on the evidence will have options that weren't standard practice ten years ago.

## Scope of Practice Note

This document is intended for education, not clinical management. A coach or community health educator can appropriately discuss the lifestyle factors in this document — outdoor time, dietary quality, sleep, screen habits — with clients and their families, and can encourage timely eye exams. Prescribing, recommending specific medications (including atropine), and interpreting clinical findings are outside the scope of coaching practice and require a licensed eye care professional. If a client or family member reports sudden visual changes, refer promptly.
