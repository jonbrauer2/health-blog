## 1. Introduction: Taking Off Your Shoes and Asking Good Questions

Take off your shoes and walk across a damp lawn at sunrise. Notice how the cool grass feels under your feet. Most people would say that’s just pleasant. A growing wellness movement says it’s medicine.

“Earthing” or “grounding” has exploded across social media, podcasts, and online stores. Influencers credit it with curing chronic pain, reversing inflammation, fixing insomnia, lowering blood pressure, and even slowing aging. Companies sell sheets, mats, patches, socks, and shoes that promise to bring the same benefits indoors — often for hundreds of dollars.

So which is it? Real biology with measurable effects? A pleasant practice oversold by marketing? Or somewhere in the middle?

This handout walks you through what earthing actually is, what the research really shows (the good and the not-so-good), and how to make a smart, low-pressure decision for yourself and your family. We’ll respect your experiences, take the science seriously, and not try to sell you anything.

Expect this much honesty up front: the marketing far outruns the evidence. But walking barefoot on the grass is also a wonderful thing to do, and you don’t need a $300 sheet to get most of what people report loving about earthing.

-----

## 2. What Is Earthing/Grounding?

**Earthing** (also called **grounding**) is the practice of putting your bare skin in direct contact with the earth’s surface — grass, soil, sand, or unsealed concrete — or using a conductive product that connects you to the ground wire of your home’s electrical outlet.[^1]

The basic idea, according to its supporters, is that the earth carries a mild negative electrical charge. When your skin touches it, free electrons flow from the ground into your body. Those electrons supposedly act like antioxidants, neutralize “free radicals,” calm inflammation, and improve everything from sleep to heart health.[^2]

### A Short History

People have walked barefoot for thousands of years, and almost every culture has traditions of connecting with the land. But the modern earthing movement traces back to one man: **Clint Ober**.

Ober spent his career in the cable television industry, where electrical grounding is critical to keeping signals stable. After a serious health scare in the 1990s, he started wondering whether the human body might need grounding the way cable systems do. He rigged up a homemade conductive bedsheet, slept on it, and reported better sleep and less pain.[^3] From that personal experiment, he founded **EarthFx Inc.** and built the earthing product industry that exists today.

### Key Proponents — and Their Financial Interests

Almost every well-known earthing study comes from a small group of researchers with documented financial ties to the industry. This matters, and it’s important to say it plainly:

- **Gaétan Chevalier, PhD** — a physicist and the director of the Earthing Institute. He has been a paid contractor for EarthFx Inc.
- **James L. Oschman, PhD** — a biologist and frequent co-author with Chevalier. Also a paid contractor for EarthFx.
- **Stephen Sinatra, MD** — a cardiologist, author of the popular book *Earthing*, and seller of grounding products.
- **Karol Sokal, MD & Pawel Sokal, MD** — a father-and-son team of Polish physicians who conducted some of the earlier physiological studies; they appear to be the most independent of the major investigators.
- **Maurice Ghaly, MD & Dale Teplitz** — authors of the most-cited sleep and cortisol study.

In the 2015 *Journal of Inflammation Research* paper, the disclosure states that Chevalier and Oschman “are independent contractors for EarthFx Inc., the company sponsoring earthing research, and own a small percentage of shares in the company.”[^2] The same paper acknowledges that “Clinton Ober of EarthFx Inc. has provided continuous support and encouragement for the research.” A nearly identical disclosure appears in the 2012 review.[^1]

That doesn’t make the research wrong. But it does mean you should read it the same way you’d read a vitamin study funded by a vitamin company: carefully, and looking for independent replication.

## 3. What Do Proponents Claim?

Earthing advocates make a long list of health claims. Here’s the full picture:

- Reduces chronic inflammation
- Lowers chronic pain
- Improves sleep quality and shifts cortisol back to a normal daily rhythm
- Speeds healing of wounds, including stubborn ones
- Reduces muscle soreness after exercise
- Thins the blood, lowering cardiovascular risk
- Calms the autonomic nervous system (shifts you from “fight or flight” toward “rest and digest”)
- Improves mood and reduces anxiety and depression
- Helps blood-sugar regulation
- Slows aging and supports skin health[^2][^4]

### The Proposed Mechanisms

How is the earth supposed to do all this? Proponents offer several overlapping theories:

1. **Electron transfer.** The earth has a near-infinite supply of free electrons. Skin contact lets those electrons flow into your body, where they act as natural antioxidants.[^5]
1. **Free-radical neutralization.** Inflammation is partly driven by reactive oxygen species (free radicals). Electrons from the earth supposedly cancel them out.[^1]
1. **Autonomic nervous system effects.** Grounding is said to shift heart rate variability (HRV) toward a calmer, parasympathetic state.[^6]
1. **Cortisol normalization.** Grounding during sleep reportedly resets the daily cortisol rhythm, lowering nighttime levels.[^7]
1. **Blood viscosity.** Earthing supposedly increases the negative charge (zeta potential) on red blood cells, making them repel each other and flow more easily.[^8]

These mechanisms range from theoretically plausible (electrons can absolutely neutralize free radicals — that’s basic chemistry) to highly speculative (the idea of “electron deficiency” as a disease state is not an established concept in physiology).

## 4. What Does the Research Actually Show?

Let’s go through the actual studies — the ones that get cited over and over — and what they really found.

### The Cortisol/Sleep Study (Ghaly & Teplitz, 2004)[^7]

**12 participants** slept on a grounded conductive sheet for 8 weeks. Researchers measured cortisol levels in saliva at 4-hour intervals. Nighttime cortisol dropped, and the daily cortisol rhythm looked more normal. Participants also reported better sleep and less pain. There was **no control group** — every participant was grounded, and they all knew it.

### The Polish Physiology Studies (Sokal & Sokal, 2011)[^9]

A father-and-son team in Poland ran a series of small experiments (groups of 12 to 84 people) looking at calcium, electrolytes, thyroid hormones, glucose, and immune response after grounding. They reported statistically significant changes — for example, lower glucose in a group of 12 people with type 2 diabetes after 72 hours of grounding. The Sokals appear to be the most independent of the major earthing researchers, but the sample sizes remain very small.

### Heart Rate Variability (Chevalier & Sinatra, 2011)[^6]

**28 healthy people** were monitored for 2 hours, with 40 minutes of grounding via electrode patches. Researchers reported a significant improvement in HRV. The participants served as their own controls.

### Blood Viscosity / Zeta Potential (Chevalier et al., 2013)[^8]

**10 healthy adults** sat in reclining chairs for 2 hours, grounded by patches on their hands and feet, with finger-stick blood samples taken before and after. The researchers reported that the negative charge on red blood cells increased by an average factor of 2.7, suggesting thinner blood and improved flow.

### Muscle Soreness (Brown et al., 2010)[^10]

**8 healthy participants** did calf-burning exercise to create delayed-onset muscle soreness. Four were grounded; four were not. The grounded group had milder changes in white blood cells, lower creatine kinase, and less pain. The study explicitly called itself a “pilot.”

### A More Rigorous Recovery Study (Müller et al., 2019)[^11]

This is where things get more interesting. A team at the University of Salzburg ran a **triple-blinded** (participant, tester, and data analyst) trial with **22 participants** recovering from intense downhill running. The grounded sleeping group showed less reduction in jumping performance and lower creatine kinase compared to a sham-grounded group. Researchers reported reduced inflammation markers, though pain scores didn’t differ significantly. This study is unusual because it had real blinding and was conducted by researchers without obvious earthing-industry ties.

### A Strong Independent Negative Study (Muñiz-Pardos et al., 2022)[^12]

This is the study earthing proponents rarely mention. A team based at the University of Zaragoza and the International Federation of Sports Medicine ran a **double-blind, randomized crossover trial** of “grounding shoes” in **10 elite male runners**, including two Olympic/World Championship athletes. They measured running economy, blood lactate, heart rate, and perceived exertion at 80% of the runners’ anaerobic thresholds.

Their finding, stated bluntly: grounding stimulus does not improve the energy cost of running or the physiological/perceptual responses of elite athletes.[^12] The study concluded that grounding in shoe designs is not an effective alternative for well-trained athletes to improve their running efficiencies. Notably, this study was funded by adidas AG — a corporate sponsor that would have happily marketed positive results — and it still found nothing.

### Physiological Measurements vs. Subjective Reports

Here’s a useful way to sort the evidence:

- **Subjective reports** (sleep quality, pain ratings, mood) are the easiest to influence with expectation. They are also the most commonly improved outcomes in earthing studies.
- **Objective physiological markers** (cortisol, blood viscosity, HRV, creatine kinase) are harder to influence with belief alone — but they’re also harder to interpret without large, replicated, well-blinded studies.

The pattern across earthing research is consistent: small numbers, modest changes, mostly subjective endpoints, and very few rigorous, independent replications.

## 5. The Evidence Quality Problem

If you’ve read this far, you may already see the issue. Now let’s name it directly.

### Sample Sizes Are Tiny

The headline earthing studies enrolled between 8 and 60 participants — usually 10 to 30. In mainstream medical research, a clinical trial typically needs hundreds or thousands of participants to draw strong conclusions. With ten people, an effect can easily appear or disappear by chance.[^13]

### The Same Authors Keep Citing Each Other

A skeptical review has described the pattern this way: earthing studies involve the same small group of authors citing each other, use tiny sample sizes, and employ vague language that wouldn’t meet basic scientific standards.[^14] Whatever you think of the tone, the observation is accurate: a few names — Chevalier, Sinatra, Oschman, Sokal, Brown — appear repeatedly in author lists and reference lists.

### Conflicts of Interest Are Substantial

As noted above, multiple key researchers have financial ties to EarthFx Inc. The 2015 *Journal of Inflammation Research* paper openly states that Chevalier and Oschman own a small percentage of shares in the company.[^2] This is allowed in research, but it requires extra caution from readers.

### Blinding Is Genuinely Hard

In a “double-blind” study, neither the participant nor the researcher knows who got the real treatment. With earthing, that’s tough. People can sometimes feel a subtle difference, and researchers can usually tell which mat is plugged in. A few studies (notably Müller et al., 2019[^11] and Muñiz-Pardos et al., 2022[^12]) have used carefully designed sham devices, but most have not.

### Short Durations and Weak Controls

Most earthing studies run for hours, days, or a few weeks — rarely months. Many have no control group at all, or a control group that is too small to be meaningful.

### Publication Bias

In any field where industry funds the research and proponents own the journals’ favorite review topics, there is a strong risk that **negative studies don’t get published**. As neurologist Steven Novella has noted, the earthing literature shows classic signs of weak science: “Studies generally have small numbers of participants, focus on subjective outcomes (the usual suspects of pain, ‘well-being’, and stress), and are poorly controlled. Never do we see all features of rigor simultaneously.”[^15]

### Compared to Practices with Better Evidence

To put this in context: **forest bathing** (shinrin-yoku), which simply means spending time among trees, has been studied in a 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis of 22 studies. The review concluded that in all but two included studies, cortisol levels were significantly lower after intervention in forest groups compared to controls.[^16] That’s much closer to the kind of evidence base needed to make confident health claims — and it doesn’t require buying anything.

## 6. Sorting Out What Matters: The Confounders

If you walk barefoot in a park for thirty minutes a day and start feeling better, can you really credit the electrons in the soil? Probably not — at least, not those alone. Here’s everything else that happens when you “ground” outdoors:

- **Sunlight and vitamin D.** Even short outdoor exposure boosts vitamin D synthesis, which supports immune function, mood, and bone health.
- **Movement.** People who earth tend to walk more. Walking improves cardiovascular health, sleep, and mood.
- **Fresh air.** According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, indoor pollutant concentrations are typically 2 to 5 times higher than outdoor concentrations, and Americans spend roughly 90% of their time indoors.[^17] Simply stepping outside reduces exposure to indoor allergens, mold, and stale CO₂.
- **Nature exposure.** Time in green spaces lowers stress hormones and improves attention, well-documented in environmental psychology research.[^16]
- **Mindfulness.** Walking barefoot slows you down and pulls your attention into the present moment — a mild form of meditation.
- **Relaxation response.** Lying on a blanket in the grass for thirty minutes is calming, period. So is sitting quietly on a mat indoors, with or without a wire.
- **Placebo and expectation.** Believing a practice will help often produces measurable improvements in pain, sleep, and mood, even without any active ingredient.
- **Lifestyle clustering.** People who try earthing are often making other changes at the same time — eating better, sleeping more, reducing screen time, getting outdoors. Any of these can drive results.

To isolate an earthing-specific effect, a study would need to compare grounded outdoor time to ungrounded outdoor time, ideally using sham mats indoors with careful blinding. Very few studies have done this rigorously. The honest truth is that for most people, the benefits associated with earthing are very hard to separate from the benefits of “spending more time outside, moving, and resting.” And those benefits are real and well-supported — they just don’t require electrons from the soil.

## 7. What About Earthing Products?

Walk into the wellness section of the internet and you’ll find:

- **Grounding sheets** for your bed (typically $100–$300)
- **Grounding mats** for under your desk or feet ($30–$150)
- **Patches and bands** that stick to your skin ($20–$50 per pack)
- **Grounding shoes** with conductive plugs in the sole ($60–$200)
- **Bundle deals** that can run $500 or more

Most of these connect via a cord to the round “ground” hole in a standard three-prong electrical outlet. The grounding wire in your home is, in fact, connected to a metal rod driven into the soil outside, so in theory the products do create an electrical path to the earth.

### Are They Worth It?

Here’s a fair summary:

- **Evidence for products vs. direct outdoor contact.** The studies on indoor grounding sheets and patches are essentially the same set of small studies discussed above. There is no strong evidence that products produce effects that walking barefoot outdoors cannot.
- **Financial motivations.** This is an industry that exists because someone decided to sell something. Many products are sold by people with direct financial stakes — including the researchers studying them.
- **Conductivity drops over time.** An independent bench test found that silver-coated copper resistance in grounding sheets climbed significantly with repeated washing, to the point where the product functioned as “a comfortable cotton sheet with decorative thread and no meaningful electrical function.”[^18]
- **Wildly varying quality.** Independent reviewers have found that some inexpensive mats never had proper conductivity to begin with.[^18]

### Safety

Properly made grounding products plug only into the ground pin of an outlet and do not carry current under normal conditions. WebMD’s guidance is worth noting: “Wiring that’s not properly functioning could cause an electric shock. Check with your doctor before you start grounding, especially if you’re pregnant or have allergies, immune system problems, existing nerve damage, or issues with your feet.”[^19] Don’t use plug-in products during thunderstorms, and stop if you feel unwell.

### EMF Claims

Some marketing claims that earthing products also block or neutralize “harmful EMFs” from Wi-Fi and cell phones. There is no strong evidence that the low-level non-ionizing EMFs in your home cause harm, and no convincing evidence that grounding sheets address them either. Be skeptical of any product sold using fear of EMFs.

### Bottom Line on Products

If you have money to spare and you find the routine pleasant, a properly made grounding product is unlikely to hurt you. But if you can’t afford one, **you are not missing out on a proven therapy.** The free version — bare feet, real ground, ten minutes — is what the research is mostly about anyway.

## 8. A Balanced Perspective

Let’s be fair to both sides.

**On the proponent side:** Some earthing claims are biologically plausible. Electrons really can neutralize free radicals. The earth really does carry a mild negative charge. People who practice earthing often report meaningful improvements in sleep, mood, and pain. These experiences are real to them and deserve respect, not mockery.

**On the skeptical side:** Personal stories are not the same as evidence. Many earthing claims have grown well beyond what the actual data supports. The phrase “natural” does not automatically mean “safe” or “effective” — that’s the **naturalistic fallacy**.

The truth lives in the middle. Earthing has:

- **Low risk** in its free form (walking barefoot in safe places)
- **Modest, unproven benefits** when measured against rigorous standards
- **High potential cost** when it comes to commercial products
- **A genuine danger** only if it replaces medical care for serious conditions

The Cleveland Clinic’s framing is a useful guide: “Earthing’s safe for most people and may have a positive impact on your physical or mental health. But until there’s more research on the topic, it should always be a complement to — not a replacement for — evidence-based medicine.”[^20]

If you have diabetes, please don’t stop your medication because you read about a 12-person Polish study. If you have chest pain, don’t reach for a grounding patch.

## 9. Practical Guidance

### Free and Low-Cost Ways to Try Earthing

You don’t need to buy anything to test this practice.

1. **Walk barefoot on grass, sand, or soil for 15–30 minutes a day.** Morning dew is fine. So is your own backyard.
1. **Garden with your hands in the dirt.** Bonus: you get sunlight, gentle exercise, and often something nice to eat.
1. **Wade in a lake, stream, or the ocean.** Natural bodies of water are excellent conductors.
1. **Sit on the ground.** A blanket between you and the grass is fine for comfort but reduces direct contact.
1. **Make it a family routine.** A barefoot walk after dinner becomes a small daily ritual that pulls everyone outdoors.

### Safe Practices

- Choose clean, glass-free, chemical-free surfaces.
- People with diabetic neuropathy or open foot wounds should avoid going barefoot outside — talk to your doctor first.
- Watch for ticks in grassy or wooded areas.
- Use sunscreen for longer outdoor sessions.
- If you have a pacemaker, blood-thinning medications, or other medical implants, ask your doctor before trying any indoor grounding products.

### Reasonable Expectations

Don’t expect a cure. Expect what walking barefoot outdoors has always given humans: a small mood lift, a chance to slow down, and the simple pleasure of being part of the natural world. If you also sleep a bit better, great. If your chronic pain meaningfully improves, even better — but if it doesn’t, that doesn’t mean you did anything wrong.

### If You Choose Not to Try Earthing

You’re not missing out. Practices with stronger evidence and similar low cost include:

- **Walking briskly for at least 2.5 hours per week.** A large women’s health study found this level of walking was associated with roughly a 30% reduction in cardiovascular events.[^21]
- **Forest bathing or simple time among trees.** Cortisol-lowering effects shown in a meta-analysis of 22 studies.[^16]
- **Consistent sleep schedule.** The single most reliable way to improve sleep.
- **Mindfulness or contemplative practice.** Well-documented stress reduction.
- **Regular movement.** Walking, gardening, dancing, stretching — anything regular.
- **A diet rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and minimally processed foods.**
- **Social connection.** A meta-analysis of 148 studies covering 308,849 participants found that people with stronger social relationships had a 50% increased likelihood of survival compared to those with weaker social relationships.[^22]

Most of what people credit to earthing is probably captured in this list anyway.

## 10. When to Seek Professional Help

Earthing is not a treatment for any specific disease. Please see a qualified healthcare professional for:

- Chest pain, shortness of breath, or signs of stroke (call emergency services)
- Persistent fevers, unexplained weight loss, or new lumps
- Severe, persistent, or worsening pain
- Symptoms of depression, anxiety, or trauma that affect daily functioning
- Diabetes management — never adjust medication on your own based on a wellness practice
- High blood pressure or cardiovascular disease
- Wounds that aren’t healing, especially in diabetics
- Sleep problems that last more than a few weeks

If a wellness influencer or salesperson tells you to skip your medication, prescription, or scheduled procedure in favor of grounding, walk away. That’s a red flag, not a treatment plan.

-----

## 11. Conclusion

Earthing is one of those rare topics where the honest answer disappoints almost everyone. The breathless health claims you see online are not supported by strong evidence. The dismissive eye-rolls aren’t quite right either — there are some interesting small studies, a couple of plausible mechanisms, and many people who genuinely feel better after spending time barefoot outside.

Three takeaways worth keeping:

1. **The marketing far exceeds the evidence.** Be skeptical of any product or person promising big health outcomes from earthing.
1. **The free version is the safest bet.** Walking barefoot on natural ground costs nothing, carries little risk, and captures most of what the research has actually studied.
1. **The benefits people experience are likely real — but probably come from being outdoors, moving, relaxing, and connecting with nature.** That’s worth doing whether or not the electron theory holds up.

Better research is needed: larger studies, better blinding, longer durations, independent funding, and pre-registered outcomes. Until that arrives, earthing belongs in the category of “low-risk, possibly helpful, definitely overpromised.”

For health coaches and ministry leaders: this is a teaching opportunity. People are hungry for simple, natural ways to feel better. Help them go outside, move their bodies, slow down, and care for the places they live. You can do all of this without selling anything or promising what can’t be delivered. If a sense of awe and gratitude shows up while bare feet touch the earth, that’s a gift — and one that doesn’t need a wire to be real.

*Stay curious. Stay grounded — in the broader sense of the word.*
