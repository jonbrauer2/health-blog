## Introduction

Few decisions carry as much quiet power as the choices we make about what enters our bodies in the months before and during pregnancy. A developing baby is built, cell by cell, from the nutrients, oxygen, and chemistry of the person carrying them—and that construction happens fastest and most vulnerably in the earliest weeks, often before a pregnancy is even confirmed. This guide is an invitation to step into that window with knowledge rather than anxiety.

The science here is genuinely encouraging: many of the most important protective steps are simple, affordable, and within reach. Taking a folic acid supplement, choosing a glass of water over a glass of wine, swapping a fragranced lotion for a plain one, asking a pharmacist one extra question—these small acts add up to real protection. At the same time, no one does pregnancy perfectly, and this guide is not about guilt. It is about giving you and your partner clear, current information so you can make the choices that fit your life.

Throughout, we lean on guidance from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the World Health Organization (WHO), and peer-reviewed research. Where the science is uncertain, we say so. Where it is settled, we say that too. Think of this as a companion to—not a substitute for—the relationship you build with your own healthcare provider.

## 1. Medications and Pharmaceuticals in Pregnancy

Most people are surprised to learn how common medication use is during pregnancy: research in the pharmacology literature notes that **nine out of ten women take at least one medication during pregnancy**, and the use of four or more medications has roughly doubled over three decades.[^1] The goal is never to avoid all medication—untreated illness can itself harm a pregnancy—but to use the *right* medication, at the *right* dose, for the *right* reason.

### Understanding the New Label System (PLLR)

For decades, drugs carried simple letter grades—**A, B, C, D, and X**—that were widely misunderstood as a ranking of safety. In December 2014 the FDA finalized the **Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR)**, which took effect **June 30, 2015**, removing those letter categories from prescription drug labels.[^2] In their place are three narrative sections—**Pregnancy, Lactation, and Females and Males of Reproductive Potential**—that summarize the actual human and animal data, dosing considerations, and a risk summary.[^3] The PLLR applies to prescription drugs and biologics only; **over-the-counter (OTC) medicines are not affected** and carry no PLLR narrative.

> **KEY FACT:** The old A/B/C/D/X pregnancy categories were retired in 2015 because they were “overly simplistic and misleading”—clinicians often treated them as a grading system, wrongly assuming an untested “Category B” drug was safer than a well-studied “Category C” one.[^4]

### Critical Windows of Development

Timing matters enormously. The **period of organogenesis—roughly weeks 3 through 8 after conception**—is when the heart, neural tube, limbs, and major organs form, making this the window of greatest vulnerability to structural birth defects. The brain, however, develops across the *entire* pregnancy and beyond, which is why some exposures carry risk in every trimester.

### Common OTC Medications

- **Acetaminophen (Tylenol)** is generally the preferred OTC option for pain and fever in pregnancy. It is also extraordinarily common: the FDA has reported that approximately 65–70% of pregnant U.S. women use acetaminophen at some point during pregnancy.[^5] It is not risk-free; some studies have raised questions about associations with neurodevelopment. The prudent approach is the lowest effective dose for the shortest time, after discussing persistent pain with a provider.
- **NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin, diclofenac, celecoxib):** In October 2020 the FDA recommended **avoiding NSAIDs at 20 weeks of pregnancy and later**, because they can cause low amniotic fluid (oligohydramnios) and premature closure of a fetal heart vessel (the ductus arteriosus).[^6] Some prescription NSAID use early in pregnancy has also been linked to miscarriage risk.
- **Antihistamines, decongestants, and cold remedies** vary widely; some are far better studied than others, and multi-symptom products can hide an NSAID or alcohol. Always read the full ingredient list.

### When Medication Is Necessary: Risk versus Benefit

Chronic conditions—asthma, diabetes, epilepsy, depression, high blood pressure, autoimmune disease—usually need *continued* treatment in pregnancy, because uncontrolled disease is often more dangerous than the medication. The right move is rarely to stop a prescription cold; it is to plan ahead with your provider, ideally *before* conception, to choose the safest effective option.

> **WHEN TO CONSULT YOUR PROVIDER:** Never start, stop, or change a prescription medication on your own when pregnant or trying to conceive. Bring every medication, vitamin, and supplement you take—including OTC and herbal products—to a preconception or first prenatal visit for review.

## 2. Vitamins and Supplements

### Prenatal Vitamins and the Folate Story

If there is one universally agreed-upon supplement, it is **folic acid**. The CDC and the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommend that **all people capable of becoming pregnant take 400–800 micrograms (mcg) of folic acid daily**, ideally **starting at least one month before conception** and continuing through early pregnancy.[^7][^8] The reason is timing: the neural tube—which becomes the brain and spine—closes in the first 28 days, often before a person knows they are pregnant. Periconceptional folic acid can prevent **50% or more of neural tube defects** such as spina bifida and anencephaly.[^9]

> **KEY FACT:** Folic acid is the *only* form of folate proven in randomized trials and food-fortification programs to prevent neural tube defects; the CDC notes no studies show other folate forms provide the same protection.[^8] Check that your supplement lists “folic acid.”

### Other Key Nutrients

- **Iron** supports the large increase in blood volume and helps prevent maternal anemia; needs rise substantially in pregnancy.
- **Vitamin B12** works alongside folate and is especially important for those eating mostly plant-based diets.
- **Vitamin D** supports bone development; many people enter pregnancy with low levels.
- **Omega-3s (DHA/EPA):** A landmark 2018 Cochrane review of 70 trials found omega-3 supplementation reduced **preterm birth (before 37 weeks) from 13.4% to 11.9%** and **early preterm birth (before 34 weeks) from 4.6% to 2.7%**—described by the lead author as drops from 134 to 119, and from 46 to 27, per 1,000 births, respectively.[^10] Optimal intake appears to be a daily supplement of 500–1,000 mg of long-chain omega-3s (with at least 500 mg DHA) from about 12 weeks.

### Mega-Dosing and the “More Is Better” Trap

Vitamins cross the placenta, and more is not always better. **High-dose vitamin A (retinol)** is a known teratogen and can cause birth defects; this is why prenatal formulas cap vitamin A and often substitute beta-carotene. Take a prenatal vitamin as directed, and treat high-dose single supplements as medications to be cleared with a provider.

### Food First

Supplements fill gaps; they do not replace a varied diet. A pattern rich in leafy greens, beans, whole grains, fruits, nuts, and—if desired—lower-mercury fish supplies folate, iron, fiber, and antioxidants in forms the body uses efficiently.

## 3. Tobacco and Smoking

Smoking in pregnancy has declined meaningfully. According to CDC National Vital Statistics data, the share of women who smoked during pregnancy fell from 7.2% in 2016 to 3.7% in 2022. In the 2021 Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System, 5.4% of women reported smoking during pregnancy and 12.1% reported smoking before pregnancy.[^11] However measured, the harm of continued smoking is not in dispute.

### How Smoking Harms

Cigarette smoke delivers **nicotine** (a vasoconstrictor that narrows blood vessels and reduces blood flow to the placenta) and **carbon monoxide** (which binds hemoglobin and cuts oxygen reaching the fetus). The result is a developing baby that is chronically short of oxygen and nutrients.

The documented consequences are serious: **preterm delivery, restricted fetal growth and low birth weight, placenta previa, placental abruption, premature rupture of membranes, cleft lip and palate, stillbirth, and sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS)**.[^12] A dose–response analysis of approximately 25 million mother–infant pairs found that even 1–2 cigarettes a day in the first trimester raised preterm birth risk by about 31%, climbing with heavier use.[^13] Encouragingly, that same study found that women who quit *before* pregnancy had preterm risk comparable to non-smokers.

> **KEY FACT:** **Secondhand and thirdhand smoke matter too.** Secondhand smoke exposure in pregnancy is associated with miscarriage, low birth weight, preterm birth, and SIDS. Thirdhand smoke—the toxic residue that settles into furniture, carpets, and dust—can linger long after a cigarette is extinguished.

### Vaping and E-Cigarettes

E-cigarettes are *not* a safe alternative in pregnancy. They deliver nicotine—which the CDC notes can damage a fetus’s developing brain and lungs—plus flavoring chemicals and trace metals.[^14] A PRAMS surveillance study found e-cigarette–only use in pregnancy was associated with significantly increased risk of preterm birth (adjusted RR 1.29) and low birth weight (adjusted RR 1.38) compared with non-use.[^15] Because product contents vary so widely, the full risks remain incompletely characterized.

### Quitting: You Don’t Have to Do It Alone

Behavioral counseling is first-line, and quitlines work. **Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT)** in pregnancy is a decision to make with a provider, weighing the known harms of continued smoking against NRT’s more limited risks.

> **RESOURCE:** Call **1-800-QUIT-NOW (1-800-784-8669)** for free, confidential coaching in every U.S. state.

## 4. Alcohol

The guidance here is unusually unanimous. **ACOG, the CDC, and the WHO all state there is no known safe amount of alcohol at any stage of pregnancy**, and no safe type of alcohol—wine and beer are not safer than spirits.[^16]

### Why Alcohol Is Uniquely Damaging

**Alcohol crosses the placenta freely**, so the fetal blood alcohol level rises to match the mother’s. But the fetus cannot clear it efficiently—the fetal liver lacks the necessary enzymes—so alcohol and its toxic metabolite acetaldehyde linger in fetal tissues. Alcohol interferes with cell division, growth, and the migration of developing brain cells.

Neuroimaging confirms the damage: prenatal alcohol exposure is associated with changes to the **corpus callosum** and reductions in the **hippocampus** (critical for memory), cerebellum, and basal ganglia.[^17] Because these structures develop throughout gestation, **all trimesters carry risk—not just the first**.

> **KEY FACT:** **Up to 1 in 20 U.S. school-aged children may have a fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD)**—drawn from a landmark study of 6,639 first-graders that found conservative FASD prevalence of 1.1%–5.0%.[^18] That makes FASD one of the leading *preventable* causes of developmental disability. FASDs are 100% preventable by not drinking during pregnancy.

### Compassion for Those Struggling

Many pregnancies are unplanned, and many people drink before they know they are pregnant. The message from the science is hopeful: stopping at any point benefits the baby, because the brain continues developing for the rest of pregnancy. If alcohol is hard to stop, that is a medical issue deserving support, not shame.

## 5. Caffeine

Caffeine is the world’s most popular psychoactive substance, and the advice has tightened. **ACOG advises limiting caffeine to less than 200 mg per day**—roughly one 12-ounce cup of coffee—while the WHO has used a threshold of less than 300 mg/day.

Newer evidence questions whether any amount is truly safe. A widely discussed 2021 narrative review in *BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine* analyzed 48 observational studies and meta-analyses and concluded that **maternal caffeine consumption is reliably associated with negative pregnancy outcomes including miscarriage, stillbirth, low birth weight, and childhood leukemia, and that no clearly safe level has been established**.[^19] This was an observational review and cannot prove causation—mainstream bodies like ACOG still use the 200 mg guideline—but the overall trend favors caution.

> **KEY FACT:** Caffeine hides in more than coffee: tea, chocolate, cola and many sodas, energy drinks, and some OTC pain and cold medicines all contribute. A single energy drink can exceed the entire daily limit.

**Practical swaps:** decaf coffee and tea, herbal teas confirmed safe with your provider (avoid medicinal-strength herbal blends), sparkling water with fruit, warm milk, or simply smaller portions.

## 6. Environmental Exposures and Chemicals

We live in a chemical world, and while it is impossible to avoid everything, a few targeted changes meaningfully reduce a developing baby’s exposure to substances that can interfere with development.

### Endocrine Disruptors

Chemicals like **BPA (bisphenol A), phthalates, and parabens** can mimic or block hormones. Prenatal exposure to phthalates and BPA has been associated in cohort studies and reviews with effects on neurodevelopment, including links to attention, behavior, and cognitive problems.[^20] These show up in some plastics, food-can linings, vinyl, fragranced products, and many cosmetics.

### Pesticides

Long-running birth cohort research has linked prenatal exposure to **organophosphate pesticides** (measured by urinary metabolites) to poorer cognitive function, lower IQ, attention problems, and autism-related traits in children.[^21] Washing produce, choosing organic for the most heavily sprayed items when feasible, and avoiding pesticide application during pregnancy are reasonable steps.

### Heavy Metals

- **Lead:** **“There is no safe level of lead in blood.”** As of October 2021, the CDC uses a blood lead reference value of **3.5 µg/dL** to flag elevated levels, and estimates roughly half a million U.S. children meet or exceed it.[^22] Sources include old paint (pre-1978 homes), some imported pottery and cosmetics, and contaminated water.
- **Mercury:** See the Key Fact below.
- **Arsenic:** Found in some water supplies and, in lower amounts, in rice products.

> **KEY FACT — Fish in pregnancy:** The FDA and EPA advise people who are pregnant or breastfeeding to eat **8 to 12 ounces (2–3 servings) per week of lower-mercury fish** such as salmon, sardines, shrimp, and pollock, while **avoiding the highest-mercury fish: shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish (Gulf of Mexico), marlin, orange roughy, and bigeye tuna**.[^23] Fish is genuinely good for the developing brain—the goal is choosing the right kinds.

### Air Quality, VOCs, and Fragrance

Household cleaners and fresh paint can release **volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and formaldehyde**; air pollution and fine particulate matter have been linked to adverse birth outcomes. “Fragrance” or “parfum” on a label can legally hide dozens of undisclosed chemicals, including phthalates.

### Occupational Exposures

Jobs in dry cleaning (solvents), nail and hair salons (formaldehyde, toluene), agriculture (pesticides), and certain manufacturing or healthcare settings may carry exposures worth discussing with both an employer and a provider.

## 7. Preconception Health

### Why the Months Before Matter Most

Here is the central insight of this entire guide: **the most critical period of development often occurs before a person realizes they are pregnant.** Organ systems begin forming within weeks of conception, so optimizing health *before* the test turns positive is the single highest-leverage strategy.

> **KEY FACT:** Folate status takes weeks to build up in the body, which is why guidelines recommend starting folic acid **1–3 months before conception**—not after a positive test.[^8]

### Epigenetics and Fetal Programming

The **Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD)** framework proposes that conditions in the womb can “program” lifelong risk of chronic disease, including heart disease, diabetes, and metabolic conditions.[^24] Prenatal exposures can alter **gene expression** (epigenetics) without changing the DNA sequence itself, and some of these changes may carry across generations.

### Fathers Matter Too

Preconception health is not only a maternal responsibility. **Sperm take roughly three months to mature**, and during that window paternal smoking, heavy alcohol use, obesity, heat, and toxin exposure can increase **sperm DNA fragmentation** and epigenetic changes.[^25] A systematic review found paternal smoking of more than 10 cigarettes a day before conception was associated with increased pregnancy-loss risk.[^26] The practical takeaway: a partner’s three-month “clean-up” before trying to conceive is a real, evidence-supported contribution.

### Other Preconception Priorities

- **Optimize weight** and **manage chronic conditions** (diabetes, hypertension, thyroid, mental health) to stable control.
- **Update vaccinations**, including MMR and varicella if needed, *before* pregnancy (these live vaccines are not given during pregnancy).
- **Review all medications** with a provider for pregnancy compatibility.

### A Word on “Detoxing”

The body already has a sophisticated detox system: the liver, kidneys, gut, and skin. You support it by eating well, staying hydrated, sleeping, moving, and—most powerfully—**reducing the toxins coming in** (tobacco, alcohol, unnecessary chemicals). There is **no good evidence** that commercial “detox” teas, cleanses, or supplements help, and some are unsafe in pregnancy.

## 8. Lifestyle Medicine

The same habits that build a healthy life build a healthy pregnancy.

- **Nutrition:** A whole-food, largely plant-forward pattern supplies folate, iron, fiber, and antioxidants. Aim for consistency and variety.
- **Exercise:** ACOG recommends **at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week** for those with uncomplicated pregnancies—brisk walking, swimming, stationary cycling, prenatal yoga.[^27] Benefits include lower risk of gestational diabetes, excessive weight gain, and possibly preeclampsia, plus better mood. Contraindications exist (certain heart or lung conditions, cervical insufficiency, persistent bleeding, preeclampsia, preterm labor), so clear your plan with a provider.
- **Stress:** Chronic stress elevates **cortisol**, which can influence fetal development and epigenetic programming. Stress reduction is prenatal care.
- **Sleep:** Aim for 7–9 hours. After about 28 weeks, sleeping on the **left side** improves blood flow to the placenta.
- **Social support:** Strong social support is associated with reduced preterm birth risk—relationships are protective.
- **Nature:** Time outdoors lowers stress and reduces exposure to indoor air pollutants.

> **KEY FACT:** **Fewer than 25% of pregnant people in the U.S. meet the 150-minute weekly activity guideline**—making movement one of the most under-used, freely available tools in prenatal health.[^28]

## 9. Childhood Cancer and Developmental Disorders

This is a sobering but important topic, handled here with care rather than alarm. The point is not fear—it is motivation for the protective steps above.

### In Utero Origins of Childhood Cancer

For the most common childhood cancers—**leukemias and brain tumors**—evidence increasingly points to origins that begin *before birth*. Studies of identical twins and “backtracking” analyses of newborn blood spots show that the initiating genetic events of childhood leukemia frequently arise **in utero**, with a second “hit” after birth driving progression.[^29] Per *Cancer Statistics, 2025*, **leukemia is the most common childhood cancer, accounting for 28% of cases, followed closely by central nervous system tumors at 27%**; five-year relative survival for all childhood cancers has risen to **85% during 2014–2020**.[^30]

### Fetal Programming and Neurodevelopment

Consistent with the DOHaD framework, several prenatal exposures are associated with neurodevelopmental conditions:

- **Autism spectrum disorder (ASD):** associated in studies with prenatal air pollution, certain pesticides, and—strongly—the anti-seizure drug **valproate**. The CDC’s ADDM Network estimated ASD prevalence at **1 in 31 (32.2 per 1,000) eight-year-olds** in surveillance year 2022.[^31]
- **ADHD:** linked to prenatal tobacco and alcohol exposure and some medication exposures.
- **Valproate** is a powerful illustration of why preconception medication review matters: first-trimester exposure carries roughly a **1–2% risk of neural tube defects** (10–20 times the baseline) and a major malformation rate of approximately 10.7%, plus documented risks to cognitive development.[^32][^33]

> **KEY FACT:** These associations are exactly why the precautionary, preconception approach pays off: the exposures most linked to childhood cancer and neurodevelopmental disorders—tobacco, alcohol, pesticides, certain medications—are largely the ones you can plan around.

## 10. Navigating the Hard and Controversial Questions

### “Everything in Moderation”—Why Pregnancy Is Different

For everyday life, moderation is wise advice. Pregnancy is a special case because a developing fetus has no protective buffer, cannot metabolize toxins efficiently, and is building irreplaceable structures on a tight schedule. This is the **precautionary principle**: when a substance could cause permanent harm and the safe level is unknown, the wise default is avoidance—not because a single exposure guarantees harm, but because the downside is irreversible.

### Cannabis and CBD

As cannabis becomes legal in more places, use in pregnancy has risen—but legality is not safety. **THC, the psychoactive component, is fat-soluble and crosses the placenta**, reaching a fetal concentration of roughly 10% of the maternal level; cannabinoid receptors appear in the fetus as early as five weeks.[^34] ACOG associates prenatal cannabis use with **low birth weight, small-for-gestational-age infants, NICU admission, perinatal mortality, and possible long-term effects on attention, memory, and behavior**, and advises cessation during pregnancy and lactation.[^34] **No safe amount has been established, and the safety of CBD is unknown.**

### Vaccines in Pregnancy

Here the evidence supports *action*, not avoidance. ACOG and the CDC strongly recommend several vaccines during pregnancy: the **flu shot**, **Tdap** (given at 27–36 weeks to protect the newborn from whooping cough), the **COVID-19 vaccine**, and the **RSV vaccine** (at 32–36 weeks in season).[^35] ACOG states there is no evidence of adverse fetal effects from inactivated, mRNA, or toxoid vaccines. Live vaccines (MMR, varicella) are deferred until after pregnancy.

### Conflicting Advice

When sources disagree, prioritize ACOG, CDC, and your own provider. Use **MotherToBaby** (a service of the Organization of Teratology Information Specialists) for free, science-based answers about specific exposures: [mothertobaby.org](https://mothertobaby.org).

### Herbal Supplements

“Natural” does not mean safe, and most herbs lack pregnancy safety data. Several are clearly **contraindicated**: **blue cohosh** (linked in case reports to newborn stroke and heart failure), **pennyroyal** (can cause miscarriage), **high-dose licorice** (associated in research with shorter gestation and later cognitive and attention problems in children), and uterine stimulants like **black cohosh, mugwort, tansy, and rue**.[^36] Tell your provider about every herbal product.

## Contraindications and When to Seek Medical Advice

> **WHEN TO CONSULT YOUR PROVIDER — Quick Checklist**
> 
> Reach out **promptly** if you:
> 
> - Take any prescription medication, especially for epilepsy (valproate), acne (isotretinoin), blood thinning (warfarin), blood pressure (ACE inhibitors/ARBs), or mood disorders.
> - Take herbal or “detox” products, or high-dose vitamins (especially vitamin A).
> - Have a chronic condition (diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disease, autoimmune disease, depression/anxiety).
> - Smoke, vape, drink alcohol, or use cannabis and want help stopping.
> - Have had a prior pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect or other birth defect.
> - Work around chemicals, solvents, pesticides, radiation, or heavy metals.
> 
> Seek **urgent** care for: vaginal bleeding, severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, signs of preeclampsia (severe headache, vision changes, sudden swelling), reduced fetal movement, contractions before 37 weeks, or fever.

## Practical Action Steps

### Before You Conceive: A 3-Month Preparation Plan

**Month 1 — Foundation**

- Start a daily **prenatal vitamin with 400–800 mcg folic acid**.
- Book a **preconception visit**; bring every medication and supplement.
- Stop alcohol, tobacco, vaping, and cannabis. **Partners: begin your own three-month reset.**

**Month 2 — Optimize**

- Get chronic conditions to stable control; update vaccines.
- Reduce caffeine toward under 200 mg/day.
- Begin or maintain approximately 150 minutes/week of moderate activity.

**Month 3 — Refine the Environment**

- Swap fragranced and high-VOC products for fragrance-free options.
- Address home hazards (lead paint, water quality).
- Prioritize sleep, stress reduction, and social support.

### Questions to Ask Your Healthcare Provider

1. Are all of my current medications and supplements safe to continue?
1. Should I take anything beyond a standard prenatal vitamin (e.g., extra DHA, vitamin D, iron)?
1. Given my health history, am I at higher risk for any specific complications?
1. Which vaccines do I need before or during pregnancy?
1. Are there exposures in my home or job I should address?

### Safe Alternatives to Common Household Products

|Instead of…                     |Try…                                                                         |
|--------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Fragranced lotions and cleaners |**Fragrance-free** products (look for “fragrance-free,” not just “unscented”)|
|Harsh chemical cleaners         |Vinegar, baking soda, plain soap and water                                   |
|Plastic food containers (heated)|Glass or stainless steel; never microwave plastic                            |
|Conventional pest sprays        |Traps, sealing entry points; avoid spraying while pregnant                   |
|Air “fresheners”                |Open windows, houseplants, simmer citrus/herbs                               |

### Reading Medication and Product Labels: What to Look For

- On medicines: read the full **“Drug Facts”** active *and* inactive ingredients; watch for hidden **NSAIDs** and **alcohol** in multi-symptom products.
- On cosmetics: scan for **“fragrance/parfum,” phthalates, parabens, formaldehyde-releasers**.
- Use the **EWG Skin Deep** database to check personal-care products: [ewg.org/skindeep](https://www.ewg.org/skindeep/).
- When unsure, ask a **pharmacist**—they are free, expert, and accessible.

### Creating a Healthier Home Environment

- Ventilate when cleaning, painting, or cooking.
- Remove shoes at the door to keep out lead dust and pesticide residue.
- Filter drinking water if lead or arsenic is a concern; test older homes.
- Choose fresh or frozen whole foods over heavily packaged ones when you can.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**1. I drank alcohol / had a few coffees before I knew I was pregnant. Did I harm my baby?**
Try not to panic—this is extremely common. A single or occasional exposure early on is very different from ongoing use, and the brain and organs keep developing for the whole pregnancy, so the best thing you can do is stop now. Mention it to your provider honestly; they are there to help, not judge.

**2. Is decaf coffee okay?**
Yes, in normal amounts. Decaf contains only trace caffeine and is a good way to keep a comfortable ritual while staying well under the 200 mg/day guideline.

**3. Are vaccines really safe in pregnancy?**
For the recommended ones—flu, Tdap, COVID-19, RSV—yes. ACOG and the CDC find no evidence of fetal harm from these inactivated, mRNA, or toxoid vaccines, and they protect both you and your newborn. Live vaccines (MMR, varicella) are the exception and are given before or after pregnancy.[^35]

**4. Can I keep taking my antidepressant / blood pressure / thyroid medication?**
Often yes—untreated illness can be riskier than the medication. Never stop on your own; plan with your provider, ideally before conception, to find the safest effective option.

**5. Is marijuana safe now that it’s legal where I live?**
No. Legal status is not a safety rating. THC crosses the placenta, and ACOG links prenatal cannabis use to low birth weight, NICU admission, and possible long-term brain effects. CBD safety in pregnancy is unknown. The recommendation is to stop.[^34]

**6. Do I need expensive organic everything and a chemical-free home?**
No. Focus on the highest-impact, lowest-cost steps: folic acid, no alcohol or tobacco, fragrance-free basics, washing produce, and lower-mercury fish. Reducing the biggest exposures is the goal—not perfection.

**7. What about my partner—does his health matter before conception?**
Yes. Sperm take about three months to mature, and a partner’s smoking, heavy drinking, and toxin exposure can affect sperm DNA and pregnancy outcomes.[^25][^26] A shared three-month healthy reset benefits everyone.

## A Closing Thought

The creation of a new human being is among the most extraordinary processes in nature—and you get to be its steward. That stewardship is not about fear or flawless execution; it is about attention, small wise choices, and self-compassion when things are imperfect. Whatever your beliefs, the months around conception invite a kind of mindfulness: caring for your own body as the first home another person will ever know. Start where you are, do the next right thing, and lean on your healthcare team. That is more than enough.
