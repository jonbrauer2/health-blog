Few ingredients sit more quietly in our pantries than the small boxes of baking soda and baking powder. They make biscuits fluffy, pancakes tender, and quick breads rise in minutes. Yet for more than 150 years, dietary reformers, physicians, and—more recently—peer-reviewed researchers have raised questions about what these alkaline powders may be doing inside the body. The science is now nuanced enough to move beyond simple “good or bad” answers. This handout summarizes what current evidence says, where caution is warranted, and how to make practical, healthful choices in your own kitchen.

-----

## 1. What These Powders Actually Do Inside the Body

**Baking soda** is pure sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃). It is roughly 27% sodium by weight, meaning one teaspoon (about 4.6 g) contains approximately 1,260 mg of sodium—more than half the World Health Organization’s recommended daily ceiling of less than 2,000 mg per day.[^1] **Baking powder** combines sodium bicarbonate with one or more acid salts (such as monocalcium phosphate, sodium aluminum sulfate, or sodium aluminum phosphate) plus a starch buffer.

### Blood pH and Acid–Base Balance

The body keeps arterial blood pH within a narrow band (7.35–7.45) using the bicarbonate buffer system. In healthy people, a modest dietary intake of bicarbonate is buffered by the lungs (exhaling CO₂) and excreted by the kidneys without disturbing pH.[^2] However, **large doses overwhelm this system**, producing metabolic alkalosis—a state associated with hypokalemia (low potassium), tetany, lethargy, and, in severe cases, arrhythmia.[^3] A documented case of acute baking-soda ingestion produced hypernatremic metabolic alkalosis and cerebral hemorrhage.[^4]

### Electrolytes, Cellular Metabolism, and Tissue Oxygenation

Sodium bicarbonate loading raises serum sodium and lowers ionized calcium and potassium.[^5] An older but frequently cited animal study found that rapid IV sodium bicarbonate shifted the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve leftward, theoretically reducing oxygen release to tissues.[^6] In clinical practice, this effect is short-lived and usually inconsequential at dietary doses, but it underscores why bicarbonate is not biologically inert.

### Cardiovascular Implications

A double-blind crossover trial in healthy adults found that a single performance-enhancing dose of sodium bicarbonate (300 mg/kg) did not raise systolic blood pressure but did modestly raise diastolic pressure by **+3.3 ± 1.1 mmHg (P = .01)** and heart rate by **+10.1 ± 2.4 beats per minute (P = .002)**—and caused diarrhea in 91% of participants.[^7] The bigger cardiovascular concern is **chronic sodium load**: a 2020 *BMJ* dose-response meta-analysis of 85 randomized trials reported that each 50 mmol (≈1.15 g) reduction in 24-hour sodium excretion was associated with a 1.10 mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure.[^8] Globally, excess sodium intake is linked to an estimated 1.7 million deaths each year.[^1]

> **Key Fact #1**
> One teaspoon of baking soda contains about **1,260 mg of sodium**—roughly 63% of the WHO’s daily upper limit and more sodium than four standard servings of potato chips.[^1]

## 2. The Digestive System: A More Complicated Story Than Folklore Suggests

The stomach normally maintains a pH between 1.5 and 3.5—acid strong enough to denature proteins, activate pepsin, and kill most ingested microbes. Drinking a teaspoon of baking soda dissolved in water can momentarily raise stomach pH above 7 and neutralize acid, which is why it has been used for over a century as a home antacid. The FDA and Mayo Clinic still list sodium bicarbonate as an approved short-term antacid.[^9]

But there are real downsides:

- **Rebound acid secretion.** When stomach pH suddenly rises, gastrin release increases, prompting the stomach to produce more acid afterward—the opposite of what someone with reflux wants long term.[^10]
- **Impaired protein digestion.** Pepsin works only in acid; chronic neutralization can reduce protein breakdown and downstream amino-acid absorption.[^11]
- **Reduced absorption of minerals.** Stomach acid is required to free non-heme iron, calcium, magnesium, and vitamin B12 from food. Long-term acid suppression—by any means—is linked to deficiencies in these nutrients.[^12]
- **Risk of gastric rupture.** When bicarbonate meets stomach acid, it produces carbon dioxide gas. Case reports document spontaneous stomach rupture and life-threatening hypernatremia from excessive ingestion.[^4]

Contrary to old claims, peptic-ulcer formation is not directly caused by baking soda; *Helicobacter pylori* and NSAIDs are the dominant culprits. But people with active ulcers, gastritis, or reflux should not rely on baking soda as a remedy—safer, regulated antacids are available.[^10]

## 3. Nutritional Trade-Offs: What Happens to Vitamins and Minerals

The alkaline environment created by baking soda destroys several water-soluble vitamins.

- **Thiamine (B1)** is highly heat- and alkali-sensitive. A 1990 analysis of typical meals found cooking losses of thiamine averaged 40–50%, with pH being a key accelerator.[^13]
- **Vitamin C** is rapidly degraded above pH 6. Adding baking soda to preserve the green color of vegetables comes at the cost of nearly all ascorbic acid.[^14]
- **Riboflavin (B2)** and certain essential amino acids (notably lysine) are also damaged in alkaline conditions.[^14]

### Bone Health, Minerals, and a Counterintuitive Finding

A common older claim is that baking soda causes calcium loss. In fact, modern research shows the opposite for *bicarbonate itself*: alkali loads tend to **reduce** urinary calcium excretion and may even support bone.[^15] [^16] The bone-health concern comes from two other ingredients in baking powder:

- **Phosphate additives.** Unlike phosphorus in whole foods (~40–60% absorbed), inorganic phosphate from additives is absorbed at >90%.[^17] Elevated serum phosphate is linked to vascular calcification, cardiovascular mortality, and accelerated bone turnover, especially in people with reduced kidney function.[^18]
- **High sodium intake** independently promotes urinary calcium loss and is associated with reduced bone density in postmenopausal women.[^19]

> **Key Fact #2**
> Phosphorus from food additives is absorbed at roughly **double the rate** (>90%) of phosphorus naturally present in plants and meats (40–60%), making industrial phosphate—including some baking-powder acids—a hidden risk factor for kidney and cardiovascular disease.[^17]

## 4. The Aluminum Question: Honest Science on a Contested Topic

Many double-acting baking powders sold in the United States contain **sodium aluminum sulfate (SAS)** or **sodium aluminum phosphate (SALP)** as their slow-acting acid. Both SAS and SALP are listed as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the FDA, and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) authorizes SALP (E541) for limited bakery uses.[^20] Aluminum-free alternatives (e.g., Rumford brand) use only monocalcium phosphate and are widely available.

### What the Body Does with Food-Grade Aluminum

Only about **0.1–0.3% of ingested aluminum** is absorbed across the healthy gut, and most is excreted in urine within days.[^21] The European Food Safety Authority has set a Tolerable Weekly Intake (TWI) of **1 mg aluminum per kg body weight per week**—about 60 mg/week for a 60-kg adult.[^22] Average dietary aluminum intake in European adults is 0.2–1.5 mg/kg/week, meaning **some individuals, and especially children, can exceed the TWI from diet alone**, even without antacids.[^21]

### Aluminum and Alzheimer’s Disease: Where the Evidence Stands Now

- A 2016 meta-analysis of 8 studies (n = 10,567) reported that chronically aluminum-exposed individuals were 71% more likely to develop Alzheimer’s disease (OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.35–2.18).[^23]
- A 2015 meta-analysis of 9 antacid-use studies (n > 6,000) found **no association** between regular aluminum-containing antacid use and Alzheimer’s (OR 1.0, 95% CI 0.8–1.2).[^24]
- A 2025 PRISMA-compliant systematic review and meta-analysis of 54 studies concluded there is a “significant association between aluminum exposure and increased dementia risk,” while explicitly acknowledging that “conflicting evidence complicates aluminum’s definitive role as a causal factor.”[^25]
- EFSA’s 2008 evaluation concluded that dietary aluminum does not present a meaningful Alzheimer’s risk in people with normal kidney function.[^22]

The honest summary: **observational studies show a modest, real correlation between high aluminum exposure and dementia, but causality remains unproven**, and the contribution from modern baking powder is small compared to processed cheese, pancake mixes, antacids, and antiperspirants. The precautionary approach—choosing aluminum-free baking powder when convenient—is reasonable, but the claim that baking powder *causes* Alzheimer’s is not supported by current evidence.

### Tartrate-Based Baking Powders

So-called “cream of tartar” (potassium bitartrate) is a winemaking byproduct and is GRAS. It is rich in potassium (~495 mg per teaspoon)—generally beneficial, but potentially hazardous in **advanced kidney disease**, where impaired potassium clearance can produce dangerous hyperkalemia.[^26] The 19th-century concern that tartrate “injures the kidneys” is not supported by modern toxicology at normal culinary doses.[^20]

> **Key Fact #3**
> Aluminum from food additives is absorbed at only **0.1–0.3%**, far less than from many medications. Yet because intake is widespread, EFSA estimates that some adults and many children **already meet or exceed the safe weekly limit** from diet alone.[^21] [^22]

## 5. When to Use Caution

Certain individuals should be particularly thoughtful about both ingredients:

- **Hypertension or family history of high blood pressure.** Sodium bicarbonate adds significantly to daily sodium load. Most international guidelines recommend <2,300 mg/day, and ideally <1,500 mg/day for adults with hypertension.[^27]
- **Chronic kidney disease (CKD).** Damaged kidneys cannot efficiently excrete excess sodium, phosphate, potassium, or aluminum. Recent nephrology guidance advises CKD patients to **avoid phosphate additives entirely**—and that includes some baking powders.[^28]
- **Heart failure or fluid-retention conditions.** Sodium drives fluid retention and can worsen edema.
- **Sodium-restricted diets** (low-sodium diet prescribed for any reason).
- **Osteoporosis or low bone density.** High dietary sodium and inorganic phosphate accelerate bone loss.[^19]
- **Children and pregnant women.** Children’s relative aluminum exposure can reach 2.3 mg/kg/week from typical diets, more than double the adult TWI.[^21] Sodium bicarbonate is also explicitly not recommended as a home antacid during pregnancy.[^9]
- **People taking diuretics or ACE inhibitors.** Bicarbonate and potassium imbalances can interact dangerously.

## 6. Practical Steps: What You Can Do This Week

You do not need to throw out your baking soda. You can, however, **reduce reliance on chemical leavening** by using older, time-tested techniques that often produce better flavor and nutrition.

### Evidence-Based Alternatives

- **Yeast-leavened breads** allow long fermentation, which improves digestibility and lowers the glycemic index modestly.[^29]
- **Sourdough fermentation.** A 2023 systematic review of 25 randomized clinical trials (542 participants) found that most showed sourdough lowered glycemic response compared to yeast bread, and that lactic-acid bacteria pre-digest gluten and reduce FODMAPs, making sourdough gentler on sensitive stomachs.[^29] [^30]
- **Whipped egg whites.** Stiff-peaked egg whites trap air mechanically and can leaven sponge cakes, soufflés, popovers, and angel food cakes without any chemical leavener.
- **Steam leavening.** Hot ovens turn water in batter to steam, which alone lifts popovers, Yorkshire pudding, and cream puffs.
- **Creaming butter and sugar** (or whipping cream) mechanically incorporates air bubbles before baking.

### Tips for Light, Chemical-Free Bread

1. Use *fresh* yeast and give dough enough time (at least 1–2 hours for a single rise; 12–24 hours for a slow-cool ferment).
1. Knead well to develop gluten; a strong, elastic dough holds gas bubbles.
1. For quick breads without baking powder, fold in two stiff-beaten egg whites at the end.
1. Add 1 tablespoon of vinegar or lemon juice to milk to create buttermilk, which produces tenderness even with reduced leavener.

### Reading Labels: Hidden Sources of Bicarbonate and Aluminum

Watch for these on ingredient lists, especially in pancake mixes, biscuit mixes, frozen doughs, self-rising flour, and processed cheese:

- Sodium bicarbonate / “bicarbonate of soda”
- Sodium aluminum sulfate (SAS)
- Sodium aluminum phosphate (SALP, or **E541**)
- Aluminum sulfate, alum, or “potassium alum”
- Phosphate compounds: monocalcium phosphate, disodium phosphate, sodium acid pyrophosphate (SAPP)

If you want to keep using baking powder, **choose an aluminum-free brand**. Rumford and several other manufacturers produce aluminum-free formulations. Read both the principal ingredient list and the baking powder’s own ingredient statement.

## 7. Ellen White’s Counsel — A Scholarly Reflection

Ellen G. White, writing across the second half of the 19th century, was unusually consistent on this subject. In *The Ministry of Healing* (1905) she wrote: “The use of soda or baking powder in breadmaking is harmful and unnecessary. Soda causes inflammation of the stomach and often poisons the entire system.”[^31] Earlier, in *The Health Reformer* (1873) and in *Counsels on Diet and Foods*, she warned that saleratus (an older name for sodium or potassium bicarbonate) “eats the coatings of the stomach, causes inflammation, and frequently poisons the entire system.”[^32] She recommended unbolted (whole-grain) flour, yeast-raised loaves, and **graham gems** mixed with water, milk, and sometimes egg as wholesome alternatives.

### Historical Context Matters

Reading these passages without historical context can mislead in either direction. In White’s day:

- “Saleratus” was often impure, sometimes mixed with potassium carbonate—much stronger and harsher than today’s purified sodium bicarbonate.
- Most commercial baking powders in the 1880s–90s used **alum (potassium or sodium aluminum sulfate)** as the acid component. Late-19th-century chemistry was actively documenting alum’s astringent and irritating action on the stomach, and U.S. government referees in 1914 themselves noted that aluminum compounds could cause “catharsis and occasionally colic” at higher doses.[^33]
- Home cooks routinely overused saleratus to mask sour, poorly-risen bread—the practice she explicitly criticized.

### Where Modern Science Aligns

- Her concern about **stomach inflammation** finds support in evidence that frequent or excessive bicarbonate use disrupts gastric acidity, may cause rebound hyperacidity, and in extremes can rupture the stomach.[^4]
- Her preference for **yeast-leavened, whole-grain bread** anticipates the present consensus that fiber, fermentation, and slow leavening improve metabolic outcomes.[^29]
- Her warning about **aluminum-containing baking powders** was even more apt for 19th-century alum-based products than it is for modern aluminum-free varieties.

### Where Modern Science Adds Nuance

- Modern, food-grade sodium bicarbonate, used **occasionally** in small amounts (¼ to 1 teaspoon per recipe spread across many servings), does not “poison the entire system” in healthy adults.
- Aluminum-free baking powders—unavailable in her day—largely sidestep her strongest concern.
- The claim that bicarbonate itself demineralizes bones is not supported; in isolation, bicarbonate is actually bone-protective.[^15] [^16] The bone risk comes primarily from sodium and phosphate.

The most respectful reading is that her counsel was **directionally correct** given the products of her era, that the underlying principle—choose slowly fermented, whole-food breads over chemically leavened ones—remains sound nutritional advice, and that contemporary science adds important precision rather than overturning her core message.

## A Brief Reflection on Stewardship

Whether one approaches health from a religious or secular perspective, there is a shared insight in the wisdom traditions: the human body is a gift worth tending carefully. The biblical idea that the body is a “temple” (1 Corinthians 6:19) and the secular conviction that good health is a foundation for a meaningful life converge on the same practical point: **small, daily food choices accumulate into long-term well-being.** Choosing a slowly fermented loaf over a quick chemical biscuit will not, by itself, transform anyone’s health. But it is one of many small acts of attentiveness that, taken together, honor the bodies we have been given and the people who depend on us.
